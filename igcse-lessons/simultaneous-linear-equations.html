Simultaneous Linear Equations

Finding the one pair of values that satisfies two linear equations at once — by elimination, by substitution and from a graph.

Learning objectives What you will be able to do

  • Solve simultaneous equations by substitution, using brackets correctly.
  • Interpret the solution as the point of intersection of two straight lines.
  • Form and solve a pair of equations from a word problem.

1 ·  What a simultaneous pair means

One linear equation in two unknowns has endlessly many solutions: for 2x + y = 11 you can pick almost any x and find a y to match. But there is usually exactly one pair that works for two equations at the same time, and finding it is what this lesson is about.

Definition Simultaneous equations are two (or more) equations that must be true at the same time. Solving them means finding the value of every unknown that satisfies every equation — two unknowns need two equations.
x = 4,   y = 3  →  2(4) + 3 = 11 ✓     3(4) − 2(3) = 6 ✓ Check in BOTH equations, every time

2 ·  Method 1: elimination

Add or subtract the two equations so that one unknown disappears, leaving a simple linear equation. This only works when the coefficients of that unknown match in size, so sometimes you must multiply an equation first.

Add or subtract? Look at the signs

Once the coefficients of the letter you want to remove are the same size:

  • Same signs → Subtract  (+3x and +3x)
  • Different signs → Add  (+4y and −4y)

Some students remember it as SSS — Same Sign, Subtract.

Example 1Solve 3x + 2y = 19 and 3x − y = 4.
1
Label the equations. The coefficients of x are already both +3. (1)  3x + 2y = 19
(2)  3x − y = 4
2
Same signs, so subtract: (1) − (2). Take great care with 2y − (−y) = 3y. (3x − 3x) + (2y − (−y)) = 19 − 4
3y = 15  →  y = 5
3
Substitute y = 5 back into the simpler equation, (2). 3x − 5 = 4  →  3x = 9  →  x = 3
4
Check in both original equations. (1) 3(3) + 2(5) = 9 + 10 = 19 ✓
(2) 3(3) − 5 = 4 ✓
x = 3,   y = 5
Watch out Subtracting a negative term is where most marks are lost. Write the subtraction line out in full — that line itself can earn a method mark.

If neither pair of coefficients matches, multiply one or both equations by whatever makes them match — every term on both sides, using the lowest common multiple of the two coefficients.

Example 2Solve 2x + 3y = 12 and 5x − 2y = 11.
1
Neither 3 nor 2 divides into the other, so multiply both equations. The lowest common multiple of 3 and 2 is 6. (1)×2:  4x + 6y = 24
(2)×3:  15x − 6y = 33
2
+6y and −6y: different signs, so add. 19x = 57  →  x = 3
3
Substitute into the original (1) — not a scaled line — so a multiplying slip would be caught. 2(3) + 3y = 12  →  3y = 6  →  y = 2
4
Check in both originals. (1) 6 + 6 = 12 ✓
(2) 15 − 4 = 11 ✓
x = 3,   y = 2
Note Multiply every term, including the number on the right-hand side. Forgetting the right-hand side is the second most common error in this topic.

3 ·  Method 2: substitution

Make one letter the subject of one equation, then replace that letter in the other equation. You are left with a single equation in a single unknown. It is the natural choice when one equation already arrives as y = …

The four steps

  1. Make x or y the subject of the easiest equation — pick the letter whose coefficient is 1 or −1 if there is one.
  2. Substitute that expression into the other equation — put it in brackets.
  3. Solve the resulting linear equation.
  4. Put the value back into your subject formula for the second unknown, then check in both.
Example 3Solve 2x + y = 11 and 3x − 2y = 6 by substitution.
1
Equation (1) has a y with coefficient 1, so make y the subject of it. y = 11 − 2x
2
Substitute into (2), in brackets. 3x − 2(11 − 2x) = 6
3
Expand carefully: −2 × −2x = +4x. 3x − 22 + 4x = 6
7x = 28  →  x = 4
4
Back into the subject formula, then check in both equations. y = 11 − 2(4) = 3
(1) 2(4) + 3 = 11 ✓   (2) 3(4) − 2(3) = 6 ✓
x = 4,   y = 3
Exam tip Unless a question says “use substitution”, either method earns full marks. Elimination is usually faster when every coefficient is a whole number; substitution wins when one equation already gives y in terms of x.

4 ·  The graphical meaning and word problems

Each linear equation draws a straight line, so the pair that satisfies both equations must lie on both lines — it is the point of intersection.

One solution

Different gradients, so the lines cross exactly once. This is the ordinary case.

No solution

Same gradient, different intercepts — the lines are parallel, e.g. y = 2x + 1 and y = 2x − 3.

Infinitely many

One equation is a multiple of the other, so the lines are identical, e.g. x + y = 5 and 2x + 2y = 10.

From words to a pair of equations

  1. Define your letters in writing, with units: “let p be the cost of one pen in dollars”.
  2. Each separate fact gives you one equation — two unknowns means two facts.
  3. Keep the units consistent, solve, then answer the actual question in a sentence with units.
Example 43 pens and 2 notebooks cost $11.60. 5 pens and 4 notebooks cost $20.80. Find the cost of one pen and the cost of one notebook.
1
Define the letters, then write one equation per purchase. Let p = cost of one pen ($), n = cost of one notebook ($)
(1)  3p + 2n = 11.60
(2)  5p + 4n = 20.80
2
Multiply (1) by 2 so both have 4n, then subtract (same signs). (1)×2:  6p + 4n = 23.20
(6p − 5p) + (4n − 4n) = 23.20 − 20.80
p = 2.40
3
Substitute into (1). 3(2.40) + 2n = 11.60  →  2n = 4.40  →  n = 2.20
4
Check in both. (1) 7.20 + 4.40 = 11.60 ✓
(2) 12.00 + 8.80 = 20.80 ✓
a pen costs $2.40 and a notebook costs $2.20
Exam tip Money answers must be given to 2 decimal places: write $2.40, not $2.4.

Practice Now you try

Do every question on paper first, then open the solution and mark your own working.

Practice 1Solve 4x + y = 13 and 2x + y = 7.
1
Same signs on y, so subtract. 2x = 6  →  x = 3
2
Substitute into the second equation. 2(3) + y = 7  →  y = 1   (check: 12 + 1 = 13 ✓)
x = 3,   y = 1
Practice 2Solve 3x + 2y = 13 and x − 2y = −1.
1
+2y and −2y: different signs, so add. 4x = 12  →  x = 3
2
Substitute into the first equation. 9 + 2y = 13  →  y = 2   (check: 3 − 4 = −1 ✓)
x = 3,   y = 2
Practice 3Use substitution to solve y = 3x − 4 and 2x + y = 11.
1
Replace y in the second equation, in brackets. 2x + (3x − 4) = 11  →  5x = 15  →  x = 3
2
Back into the subject formula. y = 3(3) − 4 = 5
x = 3,   y = 5
Practice 4Solve 3x + 2y = 5 and 2x + 5y = −4.
1
Eliminate x: multiply (1) by 2 and (2) by 3. 6x + 4y = 10
6x + 15y = −12
2
Same signs, so subtract. 11y = −22  →  y = −2
3
Substitute into (1). 3x + 2(−2) = 5  →  3x = 9  →  x = 3
check (2): 6 + (−10) = −4 ✓
x = 3,   y = −2
Practice 54 coffees and 3 teas cost $16.10. 2 coffees and 5 teas cost $15.40. Find the cost of one coffee and one tea.
1
Define letters and form the equations. (1)  4c + 3t = 16.10
(2)  2c + 5t = 15.40
2
Multiply (2) by 2 and subtract. (2)×2:  4c + 10t = 30.80
7t = 14.70  →  t = 2.10
3
Substitute into (1). 4c = 16.10 − 6.30 = 9.80  →  c = 2.45
a coffee costs $2.45 and a tea costs $2.10

Common mistakes Lose no easy marks

Slipping on a double negative when subtracting In 2y − (−y) the answer is 3y, not y. Write the subtraction line out in full instead of doing it in your head.
Multiplying only the left-hand side When you scale an equation, every term is multiplied — including the number on the right. x − 2y = −5 doubled is 2x − 4y = −10.
Substituting without brackets Writing 3x − 2 × 11 − 2x instead of 3x − 2(11 − 2x) changes the sign of the second term and wrecks the answer.
Exam tip Always finish by substituting your pair into both original equations. It takes fifteen seconds and it is the only way to be certain — a pair that fits one equation but not the other scores nothing.

Exit check Prove it before you leave

Exit 1To eliminate y from 5x + 4y = 22 and 3x − 4y = 2, do you add or subtract?
Add — the signs of 4y are different. (8x = 24, so x = 3.)
Exit 2Solve y = x + 2 and x + y = 10.
x + (x + 2) = 10 gives x = 4, y = 6.
Exit 3Why do y = 2x + 1 and y = 2x − 3 have no simultaneous solution?
Same gradient, different intercepts — the lines are parallel and never meet.
