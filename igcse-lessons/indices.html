Indices

The laws of indices applied to algebraic terms, including zero, negative and fractional powers — and solving equations by matching bases.

Learning objectives What you will be able to do

  • Use the laws of indices to simplify products, quotients and powers of algebraic terms.
  • Apply an index outside a bracket to every factor inside, including the coefficient.
  • Interpret zero, negative and fractional indices, and evaluate expressions like 84/3 and 16−1/2.
  • Solve index equations by writing both sides as powers of a common base.

1 ·  Index notation and the laws

An index (plural indices) is shorthand for repeated multiplication: 3x4 = 3 × x × x × x × x. The 3 is the coefficient, x is the base, and the small raised 4 is the index or power. Every law below is just a shortcut for writing the multiplication out in full — if you forget one, expand a small case and read the answer off.

LawRuleAlgebraic example
Multiplyingam × an = am+nx³ × x² = x5
Dividingam ÷ an = am−nx5 ÷ x² = x³
Power of a power(am)n = amn(x³)² = x6
Power of a product(ab)n = anbn(2x)³ = 8x³
Zero indexa0 = 1  (a ≠ 0)7x0 = 7
Where the first three come from x³ × x² = (xxx)(xx) = x5 — you are just counting xs. Dividing cancels them, and (x³)² = x³ × x³ = x6.
Watch out The laws work only when the bases match, and only for multiplying and dividing: x² + x³ is not x5 — it will not simplify at all. Also 3x² and (3x)² are different: at x = 2 they give 12 and 36. The bracket changes everything.

(b) Divide the coefficients, subtract the indices. 20 ÷ 5 = 4    a6 ÷ a² = a4

(c) The index outside applies to everything inside — the 2 as well as the x5. 2³ = 8    (x5)³ = x15

2 ·  Zero, negative and fractional indices

The laws above force the meaning of these indices — they are not arbitrary definitions.

Zero index

x5 ÷ x5 = 1, but the law gives x0. So a0 = 1 — 5x0 = 5, but (5x)0 = 1.

Negative index

x² ÷ x5 = 1x³, and the law gives x−3. So a−n = 1an — a reciprocal, not a negative answer.

Fractional index

a1/2 × a1/2 = a1, so a1/2 = √a and a1/3 = 3√a. In am/n the bottom is the root, the top is the power.

Exam tip For am/n always take the root first, then the power — the numbers stay small. 82/3 = (3√8)² = 2² = 4 is far easier than squaring 8 and then cube-rooting 64.

3 ·  Solving index equations

When the unknown sits in the index, you cannot solve by the usual balancing moves. Instead write both sides as powers of the same base — once the bases match, the indices must be equal.

If ap = aq then p = q Valid for any base a > 0, a ≠ 1

Straightforward: 2x = 32

Write 32 as a power of 2: 32 = 25, so x = 5.

Reciprocal: 5x = 1125

1/125 = 1/5³ = 5−3, so x = −3.

Exam tip Learn the small powers by heart — 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64, 128; 3, 9, 27, 81; 5, 25, 125. Spotting that both 9 and 27 are powers of 3 is the whole question.
Example 4Solve 9x = 27.
1
9 and 27 are not powers of each other, but both are powers of 3 — that is the common base. 9 = 3²    27 = 3³
2
Rewrite the left-hand side using (am)n = amn. 9x = (3²)x = 32x
3
Bases now match, so the indices are equal. 32x = 3³  →  2x = 3
x = 32 = 1.5
Check 91.5 = (√9)³ = 3³ = 27.

Common mistakes Lose no easy marks

Using the laws on a sum x² + x³ is not x5 — and it will not simplify at all. The adding-indices law is for multiplying terms with the same base, never for adding them.
Forgetting the coefficient inside a bracket (2x5)³ = 8x15, not 2x15. An index outside a bracket applies to every factor inside, the number included.
Reading a negative index as a negative answer 16−1/2 is +1/4, not −4. A negative index means reciprocal; the sign of the answer never changes.
Exam tip For am/n take the root first, then the power — the numbers stay small. And when the unknown is in the index, hunt for a common base before doing anything else: 4, 8 and 16 are all powers of 2; 9, 27 and 81 are all powers of 3.
