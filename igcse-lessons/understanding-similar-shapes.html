Understanding Similar Shapes

When one shape is an enlargement of another, lengths, areas and volumes each scale by a different factor — learn to move between k, k² and k³ with confidence.

Learning objectives What you will be able to do

  • State the two conditions for shapes to be similar and find the linear scale factor k from a pair of corresponding lengths.
  • Use k to find missing lengths on similar shapes, in either direction.
  • Apply the area factor k² and the volume factor k³ to areas, surface areas, volumes and capacities.
  • Work backwards: square-root an area ratio, or cube-root a volume ratio, to recover k.
  • Handle map and model scales such as 1 : 20, including unit conversions.

1 ·  What “similar” means

Two shapes are similar if one is an exact enlargement of the other — the shape is unchanged, only the size differs. This idea runs from simple missing-side questions right up to three-dimensional model and container problems.

Definition Two shapes are similar when their corresponding angles are equal and their corresponding sides are in the same ratio. That common ratio is the linear scale factor, written k.
k = length on the large shapecorresponding length on the small shape Always put matching sides on top and bottom

Corresponding sides and angles

Exam tip Before you calculate anything, write the two shapes down in corresponding order, for example “ABC is similar to PQR”. Then AB matches PQ, and you cannot pair the wrong sides.
Example 1Triangle ABC has sides 6 cm, 8 cm and 10 cm. It is similar to triangle PQR, in which the side corresponding to the 6 cm side is 15 cm. Find the other two sides of PQR.
1
Find the linear scale factor from the pair you know, large over small. k = 15 ÷ 6 = 2.5
2
Multiply each remaining side of the small triangle by k. x = 8 × 2.5 = 20
3
y = 10 × 2.5 = 25
4
Check the answer is consistent: 15, 20, 25 is 5 × (3, 4, 5), so it is still a right-angled triangle.
x = 20 cm,   y = 25 cm
Watch out If you are going from the large shape to the small one, divide by k (or use a scale factor less than 1). Getting the direction wrong turns a 4-mark question into 0.

2 ·  The area and volume scale factors

Here is the idea that examiners test hardest. If every length is multiplied by k, then area is multiplied by k² and volume is multiplied by k³.

length × k     area × k²     volume × k³ k is always the linear scale factor

Why area uses k²

An area is two lengths multiplied together, so it picks up the factor k twice: k × k = k².

Why volume uses k³

A volume is three lengths multiplied together, so the factor appears three times: k × k × k = k³.

Watch out — the classic trap If the lengths of a shape are doubled, its area is not doubled. It is multiplied by 4. Never apply the linear factor to an area or a volume. Surface area counts as an area, so it uses k² too.
Example 2Two similar rectangles have corresponding sides of 4 cm and 10 cm. The area of the smaller rectangle is 24 cm². Find the area of the larger.
1
Find the linear scale factor first. k = 10 ÷ 4 = 2.5
2
Areas use k², so square it. k² = 2.5² = 6.25
3
Multiply the known area by the area scale factor. area = 24 × 6.25 = 150
4
Check independently: the small rectangle must be 4 × 6, so the large one is 10 × 15 = 150. ✓
area of the larger rectangle = 150 cm²
Watch out 24 × 2.5 = 60 is the trap answer. It uses the linear factor on an area.
Example 3Two mathematically similar jugs hold 250 ml and 2000 ml. The smaller jug is 12 cm high and has a surface area of 180 cm². Find the height and the surface area of the larger jug.
1
Capacity is a volume, so the ratio given is k³. k³ = 2000 ÷ 250 = 8
2
Cube root to reach the linear factor. k = ∛8 = 2
3
Height is a length, so multiply by k. h = 12 × 2 = 24
4
Surface area is an area, so multiply by k² = 4. S = 180 × 4 = 720
height = 24 cm,   surface area = 720 cm²
Watch out Three different factors appear in one question: k for the height, k² for the surface area, k³ for the capacity. Decide what kind of quantity each part is before you multiply.

3 ·  Working backwards to k

Questions often give you the areas or the volumes and ask for a length. Then you must undo the squaring or cubing: take the square root of an area ratio, or the cube root of a volume ratio, to get back to k.

k = √(area factor)      k = ∛(volume factor) Always return to the linear factor before touching a length

Given lengths

k → square it for areas, cube it for volumes.

Given areas

Divide to get k², square root to get k, then work on.

Given volumes

Divide to get k³, cube root to get k, then work on.

Example 4Two similar triangles have areas 45 cm² and 125 cm². The base of the smaller triangle is 12 cm. Find the base of the larger triangle.
1
Divide large by small to get the area scale factor. k² = 125 ÷ 45 = 25/9
2
Square root to come back to the linear factor. k = √(25/9) = 5/3
3
Now, and only now, use it on a length. b = 12 × 5/3 = 20
4
Check: (5/3)² = 25/9 = 2.777… and 45 × 2.777… = 125. ✓
base of the larger triangle = 20 cm
Exam tip Keep the ratio as a fraction rather than a rounded decimal. Here 125 ÷ 45 = 2.7̇ — rounding it early costs accuracy marks.

Common mistakes Lose no easy marks

Applying the linear factor to an area or volume If lengths double, area is multiplied by 4 and volume by 8 — never by 2. Decide what kind of quantity you are scaling before you multiply, and remember that surface area and capacity count as area and volume.
Jumping straight from an area ratio to a volume ratio You cannot cube an area ratio to get a volume ratio. Always come back to the linear factor first: area ratio → square root → k → cube → volume ratio.
Scaling in the wrong direction Compute k as large ÷ small using corresponding lengths. Going from the large shape to the small one means dividing by k, not multiplying.
Exam tip A map or model scale such as 1 : 20 is always a linear ratio, so areas differ by 20² and volumes by 20³. Unit changes obey the same rule: 1 m² = 10 000 cm² and 1 m³ = 1 000 000 cm³.

Exit check Prove it before you leave

Exit 1A shape is enlarged with scale factor 3. What happens to its area and its volume?
Area × 9 and volume × 27.
Exit 2Two similar shapes have areas in the ratio 49 : 16. What is the ratio of their lengths?
7 : 4 — square root each part of the area ratio.
Exit 3A model is built to a scale of 1 : 50. Is 50 the length, area or volume factor?
The length (linear) factor — areas scale by 50² = 2500 and volumes by 50³ = 125 000.
