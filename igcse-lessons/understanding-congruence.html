Understanding Congruence

What congruent really means, the four conditions that prove two triangles congruent, and how to set out a proof that earns full marks.

Learning objectives What you will be able to do

  • Explain the difference between congruent and similar shapes.
  • Recognise and name the four congruence conditions for triangles: SSS, SAS, ASA (including AAS) and RHS.
  • Explain why SSA and AAA do not prove congruence.
  • Read lengths and angles from a congruence statement using corresponding order.
  • Set out a congruence proof as three facts with reasons, then a conclusion naming the condition.

1 ·  Congruent or only similar?

Two shapes are congruent if you could cut one out and place it exactly on top of the other. Same shape, and same size. You are allowed to turn it round, slide it, or flip it over — a reflected shape is still congruent.

Definition Two shapes are congruent when all corresponding sides are equal and all corresponding angles are equal. The symbol is ≡: we write △ABC ≡ △PQR.

Congruent or only similar?

Congruent

  • Corresponding angles equal
  • Corresponding sides equal
  • Scale factor k = 1
  • Equal areas and equal perimeters

Similar

  • Corresponding angles equal
  • Corresponding sides in a fixed ratio
  • Any scale factor k
  • Areas in the ratio k²
Note Every pair of congruent shapes is also similar, with k = 1. The reverse is not true: similar shapes are congruent only when the scale factor happens to be 1.

2 ·  The four conditions for congruent triangles

You do not need all six measurements to prove two triangles congruent. Three carefully chosen facts are enough — but only if they come in one of these four patterns.

SSS — three sides

All three pairs of sides are equal.

SAS — two sides, included angle

The angle must be between the two sides.

ASA (or AAS) — two angles, one side

Two angles fix the third, so any matching side will do.

RHS — right angle, hypotenuse, side

Right-angled triangles only, with the hypotenuse as one of the sides.

Watch out — two patterns that prove nothing SSA (two sides and an angle that is not between them) and AAA (three angles) are not conditions. AAA proves the triangles are similar only. SSA can give two completely different triangles, as shown below.

Why SSA fails

Example 1In triangle ABC, AB = 7 cm, AC = 5 cm and angle A = 58°. In triangle PQR, PQ = 7 cm, PR = 5 cm and angle P = 58°. Are the triangles congruent?
1
List the three given facts as matching pairs. AB = PQ = 7 cm
AC = PR = 5 cm
angle A = angle P = 58°
2
Check where the angle sits. Angle A lies between AB and AC, and angle P lies between PQ and PR. It is the included angle in both.
3
Two sides and the included angle is the SAS condition, so the triangles are congruent. Write the vertices in corresponding order. △ABC ≡ △PQR (SAS)
Yes — congruent by SAS
Note The second triangle here is a mirror image of the first. Reflection does not change any length or angle, so the triangles are still congruent.
Example 2Two right-angled triangles each have a hypotenuse of 13 cm and one other side of 5 cm. Show that they are congruent, and find the remaining side.
1
Both triangles contain a right angle, so the special RHS condition is available. R: right angle in each
H: hypotenuse 13 cm in each
S: one other side 5 cm in each
2
That is exactly RHS, so the triangles are congruent — even if they are drawn in different orientations.
3
The third side is forced. Use Pythagoras. x² = 13² − 5² = 169 − 25 = 144
x = 12
congruent by RHS; the remaining side is 12 cm in both
Exam tip RHS is really SSA rescued by the right angle: Pythagoras fixes the third side, so only one triangle is possible. That is why it works when ordinary SSA does not.

3 ·  Correspondence and writing a proof

The order of the letters carries information. When you write △ABC ≡ △PQR you are stating that A matches P, B matches Q and C matches R — so AB = PQ, BC = QR and CA = RP.

The four lines of a congruence proof

  1. AB = PQ  (reason — given / radii / opposite sides of a parallelogram …)
  2. BC = QR  (reason)
  3. angle B = angle Q  (reason — alternate angles, common angle, …)
  4. Therefore △ABC ≡ △PQR (SAS)
Exam tip A congruence proof is worth 3 or 4 marks and has a fixed shape. Write three separate statements, each with a reason, then a conclusion naming the condition. Marks are given for the reasons, not for the length.
Example 3△ABC ≡ △QSR, with AB = 6 cm, BC = 8 cm and CA = 7 cm. Write down the lengths of QS, SR and RQ, and state which angle equals angle B.
1
Read the correspondence straight off the statement. A → Q,   B → S,   C → R
2
Match the sides letter for letter. AB → QS,   BC → SR,   CA → RQ
3
Copy the lengths across. QS = 6 cm,   SR = 8 cm,   RQ = 7 cm
4
Angles follow the same correspondence. angle B = angle S
QS = 6 cm, SR = 8 cm, RQ = 7 cm;   angle B = angle S
Watch out Do not assume the letters run in alphabetical order. Here B matches S, not R. Always read the order given in the question.
Example 4ABCD is a parallelogram and AC is a diagonal. Prove that △ABC ≡ △CDA, and hence that the opposite angles of a parallelogram are equal.
1
First pair of sides — opposite sides of a parallelogram are equal. AB = CD  (opposite sides of a parallelogram)
2
Second pair of sides, for the same reason. BC = DA  (opposite sides of a parallelogram)
3
Third fact: the diagonal belongs to both triangles. AC = CA  (common side)
4
Three pairs of equal sides is the SSS condition. △ABC ≡ △CDA  (SSS)
5
Corresponding angles of congruent triangles are equal, and angle B corresponds to angle D. angle ABC = angle CDA
△ABC ≡ △CDA (SSS), so the opposite angles are equal
Exam tip A shared side or a shared angle is nearly always one of your three facts. Write “common” as the reason — it is worth a mark.

Practice Now you try

Do every question on paper first, then open the solution and mark your own working.

Practice 1In △ABC and △DEF, AB = DE = 8 cm, angle A = angle D = 55° and angle B = angle E = 40°. Which condition proves congruence?
1
The 8 cm side AB lies between the two known angles at A and B.
2
Two angles and the included side is ASA. (With two angles known, the third is forced, so AAS would also do.)
ASA — △ABC ≡ △DEF
Practice 2△ABC ≡ △RST. Given AB = 6.2 cm, angle B = 74° and ST = 5 cm, write down RS, angle S and BC.
1
Read the correspondence from the statement. A → R,   B → S,   C → T
2
So RS = AB, angle S = angle B and BC = ST.
RS = 6.2 cm,   angle S = 74°,   BC = 5 cm
Practice 3Two triangles both have sides 7 cm and 10 cm and an angle of 40° that is not between those sides. Explain why they need not be congruent.
1
The given pattern is SSA — two sides and a non-included angle — which is not a congruence condition.
2
With the 40° angle fixed, the 7 cm side can swing to meet the third side in two different places, giving two genuinely different triangles with the same three measurements.
SSA is not a condition — the same data can produce two different triangles.
Practice 4PQRS is a rhombus whose diagonals meet at X. Prove that △PQX ≡ △RQX, and hence that the diagonals meet at right angles.
1
PQ = RQ  (sides of a rhombus)
2
PX = RX  (diagonals of a rhombus bisect each other)
3
QX = QX  (common)
4
Three pairs of equal sides. △PQX ≡ △RQX  (SSS)
5
So angle PXQ = angle RXQ. They lie on the straight line PR, so they add to 180° — each must be 90°.
△PQX ≡ △RQX (SSS), so the diagonals meet at 90°

Common mistakes Lose no easy marks

Using SSA or AAA as a condition Two sides and a non-included angle (SSA) can give two different triangles, and three angles (AAA) only prove similarity. The only valid conditions are SSS, SAS, ASA/AAS and RHS — and in SAS the angle must be the included angle.
Ignoring the letter order △ABC ≡ △QSR does not pair the letters alphabetically: here B matches S. Read side and angle pairs strictly from the correspondence given.
Assuming what you are asked to prove In a proof you may only use given facts, common sides or angles, and standard reasons (radii, opposite sides of a parallelogram, alternate angles). Quoting the result itself — “the diagonal of a kite bisects the angle” — earns nothing.
Exam tip Structure every proof the same way: three separate statements, each with a reason in brackets, then the conclusion naming the condition. A shared side (“common”) or radii of the same circle are the most frequently forgotten reasons.

Exit check Prove it before you leave

Exit 1Name the four conditions that prove two triangles congruent.
SSS, SAS, ASA (or AAS) and RHS.
Exit 2Two triangles have all three pairs of angles equal. Are they necessarily congruent?
No — AAA only proves they are similar; the sizes can differ.
Exit 3If △ABC ≡ △XYZ, which side equals BC?
YZ — B matches Y and C matches Z.
