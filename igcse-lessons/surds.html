Surds

Simplify roots, multiply and collect them like algebra, expand brackets, and rationalise denominators so that every answer is exact.

Learning objectives What you will be able to do

1 ·  What a surd is, and why we keep them

Definition A surd is a root that cannot be written exactly as a rational number, such as √2, √18 or 3√5. √16 = 4 is not a surd, because the root comes out exactly.

A calculator gives √2 = 1.414213562…, which is rounded and therefore slightly wrong. Writing √2 instead keeps the value exact. That is why questions say “give your answer in surd form” or “give an exact answer” — a decimal, however many digits, would lose the mark.

√a × √b = √(ab)      √a ÷ √b = √(a/b)      (√a)² = a The three rules that drive everything in this lesson
Watch out There is no rule for adding. √9 + √16 = 3 + 4 = 7, but √(9 + 16) = √25 = 5. So √a + √b ≠ √(a + b). This is the most heavily penalised error in the whole topic.

2 ·  Simplifying a surd

To simplify √n, split n into the largest perfect square factor times whatever is left, then take the square root of the square part outside.

Example 1Simplify √72, and explain why 2√18 is not a complete answer.
1
List the square numbers that divide 72: 4, 9, 36. The largest is 36. 72 = 36 × 2
2
Split the root and take the square part out. √72 = √36 × √2 = 6√2
3
Check numerically. √72 = 8.4852…    6 × 1.41421… = 8.4852… ✓
4
Using the factor 4 instead gives √72 = 2√18, which is correct but not simplified: 18 still contains the square factor 9, so 2√18 = 2 × 3√2 = 6√2.
√72 = 6√2
Exam tip If you pick a smaller square factor you still get there — just keep simplifying until the number under the root has no square factors left.

3 ·  Collecting like surds, and expanding brackets

Surds behave exactly like algebraic terms. You can only collect like surds — ones with the same number under the root — just as you can only collect like terms in algebra.

Like surds collect

5√2 + 3√2 = 8√2

7√3 − √3 = 6√3

Think of √2 as x: 5x + 3x = 8x.

Unlike surds do not

√2 + √3

cannot be simplified — leave it exactly as it is. Always simplify first, though: surds that look unlike often are not, as √12 + √27 = 2√3 + 3√3 = 5√3.

Example 2Simplify √50 + √18 − √8.
1
Simplify each surd separately, using the largest square factor each time. √50 = √25 × √2 = 5√2
√18 = √9 × √2 = 3√2
√8 = √4 × √2 = 2√2
2
They are now all multiples of √2, so collect the coefficients. 5√2 + 3√2 − 2√2 = (5 + 3 − 2)√2 = 6√2
6√2

Expand brackets exactly as you would in algebra, then use (√a)² = a to tidy up.

Note The third line is the key one. (a + b√c)(a − b√c) = a² − b²c is always rational: the two middle terms cancel. This pair is called a pair of conjugates, and it is the engine behind rationalising denominators.
Example 3Expand and simplify (a) (3 + √5)(2 − √5)  (b) (2√3 − 1)².
1
(a) Multiply every term in the first bracket by every term in the second. 3 × 2 = 6
3 × (−√5) = −3√5
√5 × 2 = 2√5
√5 × (−√5) = −(√5)² = −5
2
Collect the rational parts and the surd parts separately. (6 − 5) + (−3 + 2)√5 = 1 − √5
3
(b) Write the square out as two brackets — never square the terms separately. (2√3 − 1)(2√3 − 1)
4
Expand term by term, using (√3)² = 3. 2√3 × 2√3 = 4 × 3 = 12
2√3 × (−1) = −2√3  (this happens twice)
(−1) × (−1) = 1
5
Collect. 12 + 1 − 4√3 = 13 − 4√3
(a) 1 − √5    (b) 13 − 4√3
Watch out √5 × √5 = 5, not √25 left unsimplified and certainly not 25. And (2√3 − 1)² ≠ 12 + 1 — the middle terms matter.

4 ·  Rationalising the denominator

Mathematical convention says a final answer should have no surd in the denominator. Removing it is called rationalising, and it comes in two versions.

Single surd on the bottom

Multiply the top and bottom by that surd. Since √a × √a = a, the bottom becomes rational.

6/√3 = 6√3/3 = 2√3

Two terms: use the conjugate

Multiply top and bottom by the conjugate: change the sign in the middle. The bottom becomes a difference of two squares and so is rational.

1/(3 + √2) = (3 − √2)/7

Example 4Rationalise the denominator of 12 ÷ √6.
1
Multiply top and bottom by √6 — that is multiplying by 1, so the value is unchanged. 12/√6 × √6/√6 = 12√6 / 6
2
Cancel the rational numbers. 12 ÷ 6 = 2  →  2√6
2√6
Exam tip Do not multiply by √24 or anything clever — the smallest surd that clears the bottom is always the surd itself.
Example 5Rationalise the denominator of 83 − √5, giving your answer in the form a + b√5.
1
The conjugate of 3 − √5 is 3 + √5. Multiply top and bottom by it. 8/(3 − √5) × (3 + √5)/(3 + √5)
2
Bottom first — difference of two squares, so no surds survive. (3 − √5)(3 + √5) = 9 − 5 = 4
3
Now the top. 8(3 + √5) = 24 + 8√5
4
Divide every term by 4. (24 + 8√5)/4 = 6 + 2√5
6 + 2√5
Watch out Divide every term of the numerator by the denominator, not just the first. Writing (24 + 8√5)/4 = 6 + 8√5 throws away the accuracy mark.

Common mistakes Lose no easy marks

Adding under the root √a + √b ≠ √(a + b): compare √9 + √16 = 7 with √25 = 5. Only like surds can be collected, and only after each one is simplified.
Squaring a bracket term by term (2√3 − 1)² is not 12 + 1. Write the bracket twice and expand fully — the middle terms −4√3 carry marks.
Dividing only the first term After rationalising, divide every term of the numerator: (24 + 8√5)/4 = 6 + 2√5, never 6 + 8√5.
Exam tip “Exact answer” or “surd form” means no decimals anywhere. Keep every intermediate line in surds too — and use your calculator only to check, e.g. √72 = 8.485… = 6 × 1.414… ✓
