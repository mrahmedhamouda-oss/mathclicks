Cambridge IGCSE® Mathematics 0580 Extended Number Lesson

Limits of Accuracy

Every measurement is rounded — learn to state the upper and lower bounds of a rounded value, then combine bounds correctly in sums, differences, products and quotients.

← All lessons Objectives Starter Learn Practice Mistakes Exit check

Learning objectives What you will be able to do

  • Find the lower and upper bounds of a value rounded to any degree of accuracy — nearest unit, decimal places or significant figures.
  • Write the bounds as a single inequality LB ≤ x < UB, with the correct signs at each end.
  • Choose the right combination of bounds to find the maximum or minimum of a sum, difference, product or quotient.
  • Use the two bounds of a calculated answer to state it to a suitable degree of accuracy.

1 ·  Half a unit each way

A ruler marked in centimetres cannot tell you a length exactly. If a pencil is “27 cm to the nearest centimetre”, its true length sits somewhere in a small interval around 27. The ends of that interval are the lower bound and upper bound, and the exam expects you to state them precisely.

Definition For a value rounded to a given degree of accuracy, take half of that unit and go each way: lower bound = value − half unit, upper bound = value + half unit. For 27 cm to the nearest cm the unit is 1, half of it is 0.5, so the bounds are 26.5 and 27.5.

The interval on a number line

Why the mixed signs? A value of exactly 26.5 rounds up to 27, so it belongs inside the interval. A value of exactly 27.5 rounds up to 28, so it does not. The correct statement is

26.5 ≤ l < 27.5 The upper bound is 27.5, even though the length can never actually equal it
Watch out Never write the upper bound as 27.49 or 27.4999…. The examiner wants the exact boundary value 27.5, written with a < sign — and 27.5 is still the number you use in every calculation.
Example 1The length of a rod is 12.4 cm correct to 1 decimal place. Write down an inequality for the true length l.
1
1 decimal place means the value was rounded to the nearest 0.1. Halve that unit. 0.1 ÷ 2 = 0.05
2
Subtract and add the half unit. 12.4 − 0.05 = 12.35
12.4 + 0.05 = 12.45
3
Write it as one inequality: ≤ at the bottom, < at the top.
12.35 ≤ l < 12.45

Example 2The mass of a lorry is 8300 kg, correct to 2 significant figures. Write down its lower and upper bounds.
1
The two significant figures are the 8 and the 3. The 3 stands for 300, so the mass was rounded to the nearest 100 kg.
2
Half the unit is 50. 8300 − 50 = 8250
8300 + 50 = 8350
lower bound 8250 kg, upper bound 8350 kg  (8250 ≤ m < 8350)
Exam trap Reading 8300 as “nearest 1” gives 8299.5 and 8300.5. Always read the stated degree of accuracy before you touch the numbers.

3 ·  Calculating with bounds

When rounded measurements are combined, the answer has bounds too. The whole skill is deciding which bound of each quantity to use — and for a difference or a quotient you have to mix them.

CalculationMaximum usesMinimum uses
a + bUBa + UBbLBa + LBb
a − bUBa − LBbLBa − UBb
a × bUBa × UBbLBa × LBb
a ÷ bUBa ÷ LBbLBa ÷ UBb

Why a difference mixes the bounds

To make a − b as big as possible, start with as much as possible and take away as little as possible — upper bound of a, lower bound of b. Reverse it for the minimum.

Why a quotient mixes them too

A fraction is largest when the numerator is largest and the denominator is smallest — dividing by a smaller number gives a bigger answer. So the maximum of ab is UBaLBb.

Watch out Do not round the bounds themselves. Carry the exact boundary values through the whole calculation and round only the final answer — to 3 significant figures unless the question says otherwise.
Example 3A rectangle measures 8.4 cm by 5.7 cm, each correct to 1 decimal place. Find the upper and lower bounds of (a) its perimeter, (b) its area.
1
Half unit is 0.05, so write down all four bounds first — setting them out prevents nearly every mistake. length: 8.35 ≤ l < 8.45
width: 5.65 ≤ w < 5.75
2
(a) Perimeter is a sum, so the largest perimeter comes from the largest sides, the smallest from the smallest. max P = 2 × (8.45 + 5.75) = 28.4 cm
min P = 2 × (8.35 + 5.65) = 28 cm
3
(b) Area is a product: upper × upper and lower × lower. max A = 8.45 × 5.75 = 48.5875 cm²
min A = 8.35 × 5.65 = 47.1775 cm²
(a) 28 cm ≤ P < 28.4 cm    (b) 47.1775 cm² ≤ A < 48.5875 cm²
Exam tip Give bounds exactly when they are exact, as here. Rounding 48.5875 to 48.6 turns an upper bound into something the area could actually exceed.
Example 4A runner covers 250 m, measured to the nearest 10 m, in 20 s, measured to the nearest second. Find the upper and lower bounds of her average speed, to 3 significant figures.
1
Bounds of the distance (half unit 5) and the time (half unit 0.5). 245 ≤ d < 255
19.5 ≤ t < 20.5
2
Speed is a quotient, so the fastest speed uses the longest distance in the shortest time. max speed = 255 ÷ 19.5 = 13.0769…
3
The slowest speed uses the shortest distance in the longest time. min speed = 245 ÷ 20.5 = 11.9512…
4
Round only now, to 3 significant figures.
min speed = 12.0 m/s    max speed = 13.1 m/s  (3 sf)
Exam tip Saying “fastest = furthest in the least time” out loud gets the bounds the right way round every time.

Practice Now you try

Do every question on paper first, then open the solution and mark your own working.

Practice 1Write an inequality for the true value of: (a) 36 cm to the nearest cm  (b) 4.70 kg to 2 dp  (c) 1500 m to the nearest 100 m.
1
Halve each unit: 0.5, 0.005 and 50, then go each way from the rounded value.
(a) 35.5 ≤ x < 36.5  (b) 4.695 ≤ x < 4.705  (c) 1450 ≤ x < 1550
Practice 2The population of a town is 42 000, correct to 2 significant figures. Write down the lower and upper bounds.
1
The second significant figure is the 2, worth 2000 — so the value was rounded to the nearest 1000, and the half unit is 500. 42 000 − 500 = 41 500
42 000 + 500 = 42 500
lower bound 41 500, upper bound 42 500
Practice 3p = 6.3 and q = 2.8, each correct to 1 decimal place. Find the upper bound of p + q and the lower bound of p − q.
1
Bounds first. 6.25 ≤ p < 6.35
2.75 ≤ q < 2.85
2
A sum takes upper + upper; a difference mixes: minimum = lower of p minus upper of q. UB(p + q) = 6.35 + 2.85 = 9.2
LB(p − q) = 6.25 − 2.85 = 3.4
UB(p + q) = 9.2    LB(p − q) = 3.4
Practice 4A square has side 7.5 cm correct to 1 decimal place. Find the upper and lower bounds of its area.
1
Bounds of the side: 7.45 ≤ s < 7.55.
2
Area is s² — a product of the side with itself, so square each bound. min A = 7.45² = 55.5025 cm²
max A = 7.55² = 57.0025 cm²
55.5025 cm² ≤ A < 57.0025 cm²
Practice 5A car uses 45 litres of fuel, to the nearest litre, to travel 560 km, to the nearest 10 km. Find the maximum possible number of km per litre, to 3 significant figures.
1
Bounds first. 555 ≤ d < 565
44.5 ≤ f < 45.5
2
km per litre is a quotient — the maximum uses the longest distance on the least fuel. max = 565 ÷ 44.5 = 12.696…
12.7 km per litre (3 sf)

Common mistakes Lose no easy marks

Writing the upper bound as 27.49 or 27.499… The upper bound is the exact boundary value — 27.5 with a < sign. A string of 9s is never accepted, and it also gives wrong answers when you calculate with it.
Using upper with upper in a difference or quotient 15.65 − 9.25 uses both upper bounds and scores nothing. A difference and a quotient always mix an upper with a lower — check against the table before you compute.
Rounding bounds part-way through Carry exact boundary values through the whole calculation. Round once, at the very end, to 3 significant figures unless the question says otherwise.
Exam tip Before any bounds calculation, write all four bounds in a little table — LB and UB for each quantity. It takes ten seconds and prevents nearly every mixing mistake, and the written bounds are usually worth a method mark on their own.
