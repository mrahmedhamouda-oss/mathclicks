Percentages

Percentages of a quantity, one-step multipliers for increase and decrease, percentage change and profit — then the reverse percentages that separate the top grades.

Learning objectives What you will be able to do

  • Convert freely between percentages, decimals and fractions.
  • Find a percentage of a quantity, and write one quantity as a percentage of another.
  • Increase or decrease an amount in one step using a multiplier.
  • Calculate percentage change, profit and loss — always over the original amount.
  • Solve reverse percentage problems by dividing by the multiplier.

1 ·  Percentages, decimals and fractions

A percentage is simply a fraction with denominator 100: 37% = 37100 = 0.37. Everything in this lesson comes back to that one idea — if a question ever confuses you, write the percentage as a decimal and read the sentence again.

Definition To change a percentage to a decimal, divide by 100. To change a decimal to a percentage, multiply by 100. To change a percentage to a fraction, put it over 100 and cancel.

A percentage of a quantity

Turn the percentage into a decimal and multiply.

18% of 350 = 0.18 × 350 = 63

One quantity as a percentage of another

Write partwhole, then multiply by 100. The quantity after the word of goes on the bottom.

63 out of 180 = 63180 × 100 = 35%

Watch out Both quantities must be in the same units before you divide. To write 45 cm as a percentage of 2 m, change the 2 m into 200 cm first: 45200 × 100 = 22.5%.
Example 1(a) Find 18% of 350 kg. (b) Write 63 kg as a percentage of 180 kg.
1
(a) Write the percentage as a decimal and multiply. 18% = 0.18  →  0.18 × 350 = 63
2
(b) Put the part over the whole — “of 180 kg” makes 180 the denominator — then multiply by 100. 63 ÷ 180 = 0.35  →  0.35 × 100 = 35
(a) 63 kg    (b) 35%
Exam tip The two parts run in opposite directions. If you are unsure which way to divide, sanity-check the answer: 63 is roughly a third of 180, so 35% is believable.

2 ·  Increase and decrease with a multiplier

Finding the percentage and then adding or subtracting it is two steps and two chances to slip — and it collapses completely for repeated changes. Use a multiplier: one number that does the whole job.

increase by p% → × (1 + p100)      decrease by p% → × (1 − p100) The multiplier does the change in a single step
ChangeMultiplierChangeMultiplier
increase by 5%1.05decrease by 5%0.95
increase by 4.5%1.045decrease by 12%0.88
increase by 100%2decrease by 65%0.35
Note A multiplier bigger than 1 always means an increase; between 0 and 1 always means a decrease. Reading a multiplier the wrong way round is the single most common error in this topic.
Example 2A teacher earns $32 400 per year. Her salary rises by 4.5%. Calculate her new salary.
1
An increase, so the multiplier is 1 + 0.045. multiplier = 1.045
2
Multiply the original salary by it. 32 400 × 1.045 = 33 858
New salary = $33 858
Check The rise itself is 0.045 × 32 400 = 1458, and 32 400 + 1458 = 33 858 — same answer, but the multiplier got there in one line.

3 ·  Percentage change and repeated change

When a quantity changes you are often asked by what percentage. Compare the change with the original amount — never with the new one. For profit and loss the original is the cost price.

percentage change = changeoriginal amount × 100 change = new value − original value

If the same change happens again and again, multiply by the multiplier again and again — that is just a power: final = original × (multiplier)ⁿ. Combine different changes by multiplying their multipliers.

Watch out An increase of 20% followed by a decrease of 20% does not return you to the start: 1.2 × 0.8 = 0.96, a 4% loss overall. The second percentage is taken of a different amount, so percentages never simply cancel.
Example 3A trader buys 24 melons for a total of $96 and sells them all at $5.20 each. Calculate his percentage profit.
1
Find the total money received. 24 × 5.20 = 124.80
2
Find the profit: selling total minus cost total. 124.80 − 96 = 28.80
3
Divide by the cost price and multiply by 100. 28.80 ÷ 96 = 0.3  →  30%
Percentage profit = 30%
Exam tip This carries 3 marks: one for the total selling price, one for the profit, one for the percentage. Write all three lines even if your calculator does it in one go.
Example 4A car is bought for $18 000. Its value depreciates by 15% each year. (a) Find its value after 3 years. (b) Find the overall percentage loss in value.
1
(a) A 15% decrease each year is multiplier 0.85, applied 3 times. value = 18 000 × 0.85³
2
Work out the power first, keeping every digit. 0.85³ = 0.614125  →  18 000 × 0.614125 = 11 054.25
3
(b) The overall multiplier is 0.614125, so subtract 1. 0.614125 − 1 = −0.385875 → −38.5875%
(a) $11 054.25    (b) a loss of 38.6% (3 s.f.)
Exam tip Do not round 0.85³ to 0.61 part-way through — rounding early here changes the answer by about $25 and loses the accuracy mark.

4 ·  Reverse percentages

Here you are given the amount after a change and asked for the amount before it. Going forwards you multiply by the multiplier, so going backwards you divide by it.

original = amount after the changemultiplier Divide by the multiplier to undo the change

Why you cannot just add the percentage back

The 25% was taken off the original price, which you do not know yet. Adding 25% of the sale price gives 63 × 1.25 = 78.75 — the wrong answer.

Example 5In a sale all prices are reduced by 25%. A coat costs $63 in the sale. Calculate its price before the sale.
1
Decide the multiplier for the change that has already happened. 25% off → multiplier = 0.75
2
The sale price is 0.75 × the original, so divide to undo it. original = 63 ÷ 0.75 = 84
3
Check by going forwards again. 84 × 0.75 = 63 ✓
Price before the sale = $84
Exam trap The word that tells you it is a reverse percentage is a small one: “after a 25% reduction”, “including 15% tax”, “this is a 12% increase on last year”. Underline it before you start.

Common mistakes Lose no easy marks

Dividing by the wrong amount in percentage change The denominator is always the original amount — for profit and loss, the cost price. Dividing by the selling price is worth zero marks even if the rest of the working is perfect.
Adding the percentage back in a reverse problem A coat at $63 after 25% off did not lose 25% of $63. Adding 25% back gives $78.75; dividing by the multiplier gives the correct $84. “After”, “including” and “this is an increase on” all signal a reverse percentage.
Adding percentages across repeated changes Up 8% then down 5% is not +3%: 1.08 × 0.95 = 1.026, an increase of 2.6%. Only multipliers may be combined, never the percentages themselves.
Exam tip Every percentage answer can be checked forwards in one line: original × multiplier should reproduce the given amount. Do the check — it costs ten seconds and catches the classic reverse-percentage error every time.

Exit check Prove it before you leave

Exit 1What multiplier decreases an amount by 12%?
0.88 — and any multiplier below 1 means a decrease.
Exit 2Find 18% of 350.
0.18 × 350 = 63
Exit 3A price is $92 after a 15% increase. What was it before?
