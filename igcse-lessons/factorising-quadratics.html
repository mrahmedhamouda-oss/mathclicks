Factorising Quadratics

Factorising ax² + bx + c when a is not 1 with the ac method, spotting the difference of two squares, and turning a factorisation into the solutions of an equation.

Learning objectives What you will be able to do

  • Factorise ax² + bx + c when a ≠ 1 by splitting the middle term (the ac method).
  • Predict the signs of the factor pair from the signs of b and c, and always remove a common factor first.
  • Recognise and factorise a difference of two squares in one line.
  • Use the zero product property to solve a quadratic equation from its factorised form.

1 ·  The ac method: split the middle term

You already factorise x² + bx + c by finding two numbers with product c and sum b. When the coefficient of x² is not 1 that shortcut fails — the brackets now also have to multiply to give a. The reliable method is to split the middle term.

The ac method To factorise ax² + bx + c:
  1. Work out ac (multiply the first and last coefficients).
  2. Find two numbers with product ac and sum b.
  3. Use them to split the bx term into two terms.
  4. Factorise the four terms in two pairs — the bracket that appears twice is a factor.
6x² + 11x + 3  →  ac = 18,   b = 11  →  9 × 2 = 18  and  9 + 2 = 11
6x² + 9x + 2x + 3 = 3x(2x + 3) + 1(2x + 3) = (2x + 3)(3x + 1) The identical bracket is your proof the split was correct
Example 1Factorise (a) 3x² − 10x + 8  (b) 5x² + 9x − 2.
1
(a) ac = 3 × 8 = 24 and b = −10. The product is positive and the sum is negative, so both numbers are negative. −6 × −4 = 24  and  −6 + (−4) = −10
2
Split the middle term and factorise in pairs. 3x² − 6x − 4x + 8
= 3x(x − 2) − 4(x − 2)
= (x − 2)(3x − 4)
3
(b) ac = 5 × (−2) = −10 and b = 9. The product is negative, so one number is positive and one is negative. 10 × (−1) = −10  and  10 + (−1) = 9
4
Split and group. Watch the sign when you take out the second factor. 5x² + 10x − x − 2
= 5x(x + 2) − 1(x + 2)
= (x + 2)(5x − 1)
(a) (x − 2)(3x − 4)     (b) (x + 2)(5x − 1)
Watch out In (b) the last pair is −x − 2. You must take out −1, not +1, or the brackets will not match.

2 ·  Signs first, common factor first

The signs of b and c tell you the signs of the pair before you start searching — and “factorise fully” means take out any numerical common factor before the ac method.

Sign of cSign of bThe two numbers are…Example
positivepositiveboth positive2x² + 7x + 3 → 6 and 1
positivenegativeboth negative3x² − 10x + 8 → −6 and −4
negativepositiveone of each; the larger is positive5x² + 9x − 2 → 10 and −1
negativenegativeone of each; the larger is negative6x² − 5x − 6 → −9 and 4
Example 2Factorise fully 12x² + 22x + 6.
1
Every coefficient is even, so take out 2 first. The numbers left are much smaller. 12x² + 22x + 6 = 2(6x² + 11x + 3)
2
Factorise the bracket: ac = 18, b = 11, giving 9 and 2. 6x² + 9x + 2x + 3 = 3x(2x + 3) + 1(2x + 3)
3
Write the answer with the common factor still in front. = 2(2x + 3)(3x + 1)
12x² + 22x + 6 = 2(2x + 3)(3x + 1)
Check by expanding (2x + 3)(3x + 1) = 6x² + 11x + 3, and doubling gives 12x² + 22x + 6. Ten seconds well spent.

3 ·  The difference of two squares

When the middle term is missing and the two remaining terms are squares with a minus sign between them, the factors come in one line. This is the most heavily tested factorisation in Paper 2.

a² − b² = (a − b)(a + b) The difference of two squares

Why it works

(3x − 4)(3x + 4) = 9x² + 12x − 12x − 16 = 9x² − 16 — the middle terms cancel.

Two things it is not

  • x² + 16 does not factorise — a sum of two squares never does.
  • 2x² − 50 is not itself a difference of two squares. Take out the 2 first.
Example 3Factorise fully (a) 9x² − 16  (b) 2x² − 50  (c) 49 − 25y².
1
(a) Write each term as a square. 9x² − 16 = (3x)² − 4² = (3x − 4)(3x + 4)
2
(b) There is a common factor of 2. Take it out, and a difference of two squares appears. 2x² − 50 = 2(x² − 25) = 2(x − 5)(x + 5)
3
(c) The square being subtracted is the second term this time, so the number comes first. 49 − 25y² = 7² − (5y)² = (7 − 5y)(7 + 5y)
(a) (3x − 4)(3x + 4)    (b) 2(x − 5)(x + 5)    (c) (7 − 5y)(7 + 5y)

4 ·  Using the factorisation to solve

Factorising is not the point — solving is. Once the quadratic is written as a product equal to zero, one of the brackets must be zero.

Zero product property If A × B = 0 then A = 0 or B = 0. Set each bracket equal to zero in turn and solve the two small linear equations.
Watch out The right-hand side must be 0 before you factorise. From (x − 2)(3x − 4) = 6 you can say nothing useful. Rearrange to … = 0 first, every time.
Example 4Solve 6x² − 5x − 6 = 0.
1
ac = 6 × (−6) = −36, b = −5. One number of each sign, the larger negative. −9 × 4 = −36  and  −9 + 4 = −5
2
Split and group. 6x² − 9x + 4x − 6
= 3x(2x − 3) + 2(2x − 3)
= (2x − 3)(3x + 2)
3
Set each bracket to zero. 2x − 3 = 0 → x = 32
3x + 2 = 0 → x = −23
4
Check the first root by substituting. 6(1.5)² − 5(1.5) − 6 = 13.5 − 7.5 − 6 = 0  ✓
x = 1.5  or  x = −23
Exam tip Fractional roots are perfectly normal when a ≠ 1. Leave them as exact fractions unless the question asks for decimals.

Common mistakes Lose no easy marks

Not factorising fully 12x² + 22x + 6 = (4x + 6)(3x + 1) loses a mark — the first bracket still contains a factor of 2. Take out numerical common factors before the ac method, and keep them in the answer.
Solving when the right-hand side is not zero From (x − 2)(3x − 4) = 6 you cannot set each bracket to 6 — the zero product property only works for a product equal to zero. Rearrange first.
Sign slips in the grouping step When the second pair starts with a minus, take out a negative factor: −x − 2 = −1(x + 2). If your two brackets do not match, a sign is wrong.
Exam tip Always expand your brackets mentally as a check — and if no integer pair has product ac and sum b, stop searching: the question wants the quadratic formula instead, with answers to 2 decimal places.
