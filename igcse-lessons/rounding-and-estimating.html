Rounding and Estimating

Round to decimal places and significant figures without losing the accuracy mark, then use 1-figure estimates to check that any answer is sensible.

← All lessons Objectives Starter Learn Practice Mistakes Exit check

Learning objectives What you will be able to do

  • Round any number to a given number of decimal places, or to the nearest 10, 100 or 1000.
  • Round to a given number of significant figures, deciding correctly which zeros count.
  • Keep the trailing zeros that the required accuracy demands (2.40, 1.0, 20.0).
  • Estimate a calculation by writing every number correct to 1 significant figure.
  • Say whether an estimate is an over-estimate or an under-estimate, with a reason.

1 ·  The rounding rule

Rounding replaces a number by a nearby, simpler one — and the method never changes, whatever accuracy is asked for.

The rounding rule Find the deciding digit — the first digit you are about to throw away. If it is 5 or more, round the last kept digit up; if it is 4 or less, leave the last kept digit alone. Replace any discarded digits in front of the decimal point with zeros to hold the place value.

Why 347 rounds to 350

“2 decimal places” (2 d.p.) means keep exactly two digits after the decimal point. For the nearest 10, 100 or 1000 the deciding digit sits to the left of the point instead.

Keep the final zero 2.397 to 2 d.p. is 2.40, not 2.4. Two decimal places were asked for, so two must appear — dropping the zero loses the accuracy mark.
Example 1Round 7.4863 to (a) 3 d.p., (b) 2 d.p., (c) 1 d.p.
1
(a) Keep three decimals; the deciding digit is the fourth decimal, 3 — less than 5, so nothing changes. 7.486|3  →  7.486
2
(b) Keep two decimals; the deciding digit is 6, so round the 8 up to 9. 7.48|63  →  7.49
3
(c) Keep one decimal; the deciding digit is 8, so round the 4 up to 5. 7.4|863  →  7.5
(a) 7.486    (b) 7.49    (c) 7.5
Never round twice Always round the original number. Rounding in stages is unsafe: 0.449 → 0.45 → 0.5, whereas 0.449 to 1 d.p. is 0.4.

2 ·  Significant figures

Significant figures count from the first non-zero digit, reading left to right — they tell you how many digits actually carry information, whatever the size of the number.

Which zeros are significant?
  • Zeros before the first non-zero digit are never significant: in 0.00408 the significant figures are 4, 0, 8.
  • Zeros between significant digits always are: 4.05 has 3 s.f.
  • Zeros at the end of a whole number are place-holders: 46 000 written to 2 s.f. has significant figures 4 and 6.

Exam tip Unless a question says otherwise, 0580 answers are given correct to 3 significant figures (or exactly, if the answer is exact). Money goes to 2 decimal places; angles are usually asked to 1 decimal place.
Example 2Write 0.0030649 correct to 3 significant figures.
1
Find the first significant figure — the first non-zero digit. The two zeros after the point are only place-holders. 0.0030649  →  1st s.f. is 3
2
Count on: 3 (1st), 0 (2nd), 6 (3rd). The deciding digit is the next one, 4. 0.00306|49
3
4 is less than 5, so the 6 stays and everything after it goes. The leading zeros must stay — they fix the size of the number.
0.00306 (3 s.f.)
Example 3Round (a) 19.97 to 1 decimal place, (b) 0.9962 to 2 significant figures.
1
(a) The deciding digit is 7, so the 9 in the tenths place rounds up. That makes 10, so carry into the units column. 19.9|7  →  19.9 + 0.1 = 20.0
2
(b) The first two significant figures are 9 and 9; the deciding digit is 6, so the carry runs all the way up. 0.99|62  →  0.99 + 0.01 = 1.00  →  write 1.0
(a) 20.0    (b) 1.0
Watch out Answers of “20” and “1” would lose the mark — they do not show the required accuracy.

3 ·  Estimating with 1-figure rounding

An estimate is a quick, calculator-free check. The 0580 instruction is almost always the same: “By writing each number correct to 1 significant figure, estimate the value of…” You must show the rounded numbers — that line is where the method mark lives.

38.7 × 5.20.49 ≈ 40 × 50.5 = 2000.5 = 400 Each number to 1 significant figure, then work it out exactly
Do not round the answer as well Round the numbers first, then do the arithmetic exactly. Writing 200 ÷ 0.5 = 400 is right; approximating again at the end throws away the whole point.

To decide whether an estimate is too big or too small: rounding a number on the top up (or the bottom down) makes the estimate bigger; rounding the top down (or the bottom up) makes it smaller. If the roundings pull in opposite directions, compare with the calculator value. For a square root, trap the number between the square numbers either side: 36 < 48 < 49, so √48 is just under 7.

Example 4By writing each number correct to 1 significant figure, estimate 4.87 × 19.60.51 × 3.9. Is it an over- or under-estimate?
1
Round every number to 1 s.f. — show this line, it earns the method mark. ≈ (5 × 20) ÷ (0.5 × 4)
2
Top and bottom separately, exactly. 5 × 20 = 100
0.5 × 4 = 2
3
Divide. 100 ÷ 2 = 50
4
The roundings pull in different directions, so settle it against the exact value. 95.452 ÷ 1.989 = 47.99…  <  50
Estimate ≈ 50, an over-estimate (true value 48.0 to 3 s.f.)

Common mistakes Lose no easy marks

Dropping the trailing zero 2.397 to 2 d.p. is 2.40; 0.9962 to 2 s.f. is 1.0; 19.97 to 1 d.p. is 20.0. The accuracy asked for must be visible in the answer, or the mark is gone.
Rounding in two stages 7.4863 → 7.49 → 7.5 happens to work, but the method is unsafe: 0.449 → 0.45 → 0.5 is wrong, because 0.449 to 1 d.p. is 0.4. Always round the original number.
Estimating without showing the rounded numbers An estimate question is worth two marks: one for the line of 1 s.f. numbers, one for the value. Writing only the final number can score zero even when it is right.
Exam tip Never round mid-calculation — use Ans or the calculator memory between stages. If you must write a middle value down, keep at least 4 or 5 significant figures, and round only the final answer to 3 s.f.
