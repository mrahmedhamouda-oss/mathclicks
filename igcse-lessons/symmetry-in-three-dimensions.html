Cambridge IGCSE® Mathematics 0580 Extended Geometry Lesson

Symmetry in Three Dimensions

Planes of symmetry and axes of rotational symmetry — count them systematically instead of guessing, for every solid on the syllabus.

← All lessons Objectives Starter Learn Practice Mistakes Exit check

Learning objectives What you will be able to do

  • Recognise a plane of symmetry: a flat cut whose two pieces are mirror images, not merely congruent.
  • Count the planes of symmetry of a cuboid, a cube, a square-based pyramid and any prism using the cross-section rule.
  • State the planes of symmetry of the cylinder, cone and sphere, including which cuts do not work.
  • Identify axes of rotational symmetry, state their order, and describe an axis precisely by naming both points it passes through.

1 ·  Planes of symmetry

In two dimensions the mirror is a line. In three dimensions the mirror is a flat surface: a plane of symmetry. Everything you learned about line symmetry carries over — the test is still “does one half reflect onto the other?”

Definition A plane of symmetry is a flat surface that cuts a solid into two pieces such that each piece is the mirror image of the other in that plane. Every point of the solid has a matching point the same perpendicular distance on the other side of the plane.

The three planes of symmetry of a cuboid

Watch out Cutting a cuboid diagonally does produce two congruent pieces — but one is a rotation of the other, not a reflection, so it is not a plane of symmetry. The diagonal cut only works when the two faces it meets are equal squares — which is why a cube has extra planes and an ordinary cuboid does not.
Example 1A cuboid measures 8 cm by 5 cm by 3 cm. Write down the number of planes of symmetry and describe each one.
1
The three edge lengths are all different, so no diagonal cut can work. Take the pairs of opposite faces one at a time.
2
Parallel to the two 5 × 3 faces, halfway along the 8 cm edges. a 5 cm × 3 cm rectangular cut, 4 cm from each end
3
Parallel to the two 8 × 3 faces, halfway along the 5 cm edges. an 8 cm × 3 cm cut, 2.5 cm from each side
4
Parallel to the two 8 × 5 faces, halfway along the 3 cm edges. an 8 cm × 5 cm cut, 1.5 cm from each face
3 planes of symmetry — one for each pair of opposite faces
Exam tip A cuboid whose three edge lengths are all different has exactly 3 planes of symmetry: one parallel to each pair of opposite faces, halfway between them. Say it as “one for each pair of faces” and you will never miscount.

2 ·  The cube and the square prism

A cube is a cuboid whose edges are all equal, so it keeps the three “face” planes and gains six more. Count them in two groups and the answer is never in doubt.

Group 1 — parallel to the faces

One plane halfway between each pair of opposite faces. A cube has 3 pairs of opposite faces.

3 planes

Group 2 — the diagonal planes

Each one contains a pair of opposite edges. The 12 edges pair up into 6 such pairs, and every one of these cuts works because the faces of a cube are squares.

6 planes

3 + 6 = 9 planes of symmetry A cube has 9; a cuboid with different edges has only 3

Two of the six diagonal planes

Example 2A solid cuboid measures 4 cm by 4 cm by 10 cm. Find the number of planes of symmetry, and explain why the answer is not 3.
1
The cross-section is a square 4 × 4. Any plane that contains the long axis and is a line of symmetry of that square is a plane of symmetry of the solid. A square has 4 lines of symmetry. 4 planes containing the 10 cm axis
2
There is also one plane perpendicular to the long axis, halfway along it. 1 more plane, 5 cm from each square face
3
Add them. The answer is not 3 because the square cross-section allows the two diagonal planes that an ordinary cuboid lacks. 4 + 1 = 5
5 planes of symmetry

3 ·  Prisms, pyramids and curved solids

For a prism, 2D symmetry does the heavy lifting: every line of symmetry of the cross-section gives a plane containing the length, and there is always one extra cut halfway along.

Square-based pyramid: 4 planes

2 planes through the midpoints of opposite base edges, and 2 through the base diagonals. All four contain the apex.

Curved solids

Cylinder: every vertical plane through the axis, plus the one horizontal plane halfway up. Cone: every plane containing the axis — but a horizontal cut fails, because the top piece is a small cone and the bottom is a frustum. Sphere: every plane through the centre.

Example 3A prism is 12 cm long. Its cross-section is a triangle with sides 5 cm, 5 cm and 8 cm. How many planes of symmetry does the prism have?
1
Deal with the cross-section first. A triangle with sides 5, 5, 8 is isosceles, not equilateral, so it has just 1 line of symmetry. 1 plane containing that line and the 12 cm length
2
Then add the cut perpendicular to the length, halfway along. 1 more plane, 6 cm from each end
3
Total. 1 + 1 = 2
2 planes of symmetry
Watch out If the triangle were equilateral the answer would be 3 + 1 = 4, and if it were scalene it would be just 1 (only the halfway cut). Always check the cross-section before you write anything down.

4 ·  Axes of rotational symmetry

In three dimensions a shape rotates about a line, not a point. That line is an axis of rotational symmetry, and it has an order in exactly the same sense as in two dimensions.

Definition An axis of rotational symmetry is a line about which the solid can be turned so that it occupies the same space as before. The order is the number of positions in one full turn where the solid looks unchanged.

The three axes of a cuboid

How to describe an axis

Say where it goes in, and where it comes out: “the axis through the centres of the two 5 × 3 faces”, or “the vertical axis through the apex and the centre of the base”. A vague answer such as “down the middle” does not score.

Orders you should know

  • Cuboid: 3 axes, each of order 2
  • Square-based pyramid: 1 axis of order 4
  • Equilateral triangular prism: the long axis has order 3
  • Regular hexagonal prism: the long axis has order 6
  • Cylinder and cone: the axis has infinite order — any rotation at all works
Example 4A cuboid measures 6 cm by 6 cm by 6 cm. State (a) how many planes of symmetry it has, (b) the order of rotational symmetry about an axis through the centres of two opposite faces, (c) the order about a body diagonal.
1
All edges are 6 cm, so this cuboid is a cube. 3 face planes + 6 diagonal planes = 9
2
(b) Look down the axis through two opposite face centres: you see a square, and a square has rotational symmetry of order 4. Turning the cube through 90° about that axis leaves it looking identical. order 4 — it fits every 90°
3
(c) Look down a corner-to-corner body diagonal: the three faces meeting at that corner take each other’s places every 120°. 360° ÷ 120° = 3
(a) 9    (b) order 4    (c) order 3
Going further A cube has 13 axes altogether: 3 of order 4 through the centres of opposite faces, 4 of order 3 through opposite corners, and 6 of order 2 through the midpoints of opposite edges. You will not be asked to list all 13, but seeing the order-3 corner-to-corner axis is a genuine test of 3-D thinking.

Practice Now you try

Do every question on paper first, then open the solution and mark your own working.

Practice 1Write down the number of planes of symmetry of (a) a cuboid measuring 7 × 4 × 2, (b) a cube, (c) a sphere, (d) a square-based pyramid.
1
(a) All edges different, so only the three face-parallel cuts. (b) 3 face planes + 6 diagonal planes. (c) Any plane through the centre works. (d) 2 through opposite edge midpoints + 2 through the base diagonals, all containing the apex.
(a) 3  (b) 9  (c) infinitely many  (d) 4
Practice 2A prism 10 cm long has a cross-section that is an isosceles triangle with sides 6 cm, 6 cm and 4 cm. How many planes of symmetry does it have?
1
The isosceles cross-section has 1 line of symmetry, so 1 plane runs along the length; add the halfway cut. 1 + 1 = 2
2 planes of symmetry
Practice 3A cuboid measures 5 × 5 × 12 centimetres. Explain why it has more than 3 planes of symmetry and state how many it has.
1
The cross-section is a square, which has 4 lines of symmetry — so 4 planes contain the 12 cm axis, including two diagonal ones an ordinary cuboid lacks.
2
Add the plane 6 cm from each square face. 4 + 1 = 5
5 planes of symmetry
Practice 4Describe the axis of rotational symmetry of a cone and state its order.
1
Name both ends of the line: from the apex to the centre of the circular base. Any rotation at all about it maps the cone onto itself.
The line from the apex to the centre of the base; its order is infinite.
Practice 5A prism 15 cm long has a regular pentagonal cross-section. Find the number of planes of symmetry and the order of rotational symmetry about the long axis. Would your answers change if the pentagon were irregular but had one line of symmetry?
1
A regular pentagon has 5 lines of symmetry and order 5. planes = 5 + 1 = 6  ·  order about the long axis = 5
2
With only one line of symmetry: planes = 1 + 1 = 2, and order 1 (no rotational symmetry about the axis)
6 planes and order 5; the irregular version drops to 2 planes and order 1

Common mistakes Lose no easy marks

Accepting a congruent cut as a plane of symmetry A diagonal cut through a cuboid gives two congruent halves, but one is a rotation of the other, not a mirror image — so it is not a plane of symmetry. The diagonal cut only works when the faces it meets are equal squares.
Treating a square prism like an ordinary cuboid A 4 × 4 × 10 cuboid has 5 planes, not 3 — the square cross-section adds two diagonal planes. Always check whether any pair of edges is equal before counting.
Describing an axis vaguely “Down the middle” scores nothing. Name both points the axis passes through: the centres of two opposite faces, or the apex and the centre of the base.
Exam tip For any prism, count in two moves: lines of symmetry of the cross-section (each becomes a plane along the length) + 1 for the cut halfway along. The order of rotational symmetry about the long axis equals the order of the cross-section. Draw the cross-section first and the 3D question becomes a 2D one.

Exit check Prove it before you leave

Exit 1How many planes of symmetry does a cube have?
9 — 3 parallel to the faces and 6 diagonal planes through opposite edges.
Exit 2A prism has a regular hexagonal cross-section. How many planes of symmetry does it have?
6 + 1 = 7 — six along the length plus the halfway cut.
Exit 3What is the order of rotational symmetry of a square-based pyramid about its vertical axis?
Order 4 — it fits every 90° about the axis through the apex and the centre of the base.
