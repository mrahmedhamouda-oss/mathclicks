Conversion Graphs

Draw a straight line once and every conversion afterwards is free — learn to draw, read and interpret conversion graphs, including the ones that miss the origin.

Learning objectives What you will be able to do

  • Draw a conversion graph from a given rate, choosing sensible scales and two well-spaced points.
  • Read values off a conversion graph in both directions, showing dotted construction lines.
  • Interpret the gradient as the conversion rate and the intercept as a fixed charge.
  • Convert values beyond the end of the graph by scaling — and know when scaling is not allowed.

1 ·  What a conversion graph is

A conversion graph is a straight line that lets you swap between two units without doing any arithmetic at all. Put one quantity on each axis, draw the line once, and every reading afterwards is free. Sometimes you draw the graph; more often you are given one and asked to read values off it.

Definition Two quantities are in direct proportion when doubling one doubles the other, and when zero of one means zero of the other. Their conversion graph is a straight line through the origin.

A conversion graph for miles and kilometres

Example 1Given that 5 miles = 8 km, draw a conversion graph for distances from 0 to 50 miles.
1
Miles and kilometres are in direct proportion, so the graph is a straight line through the origin. That gives your first point for free. Point A: (0, 0)
2
A straight line needs only one more point, and the best one is at the far end of the axis — a long line is much more accurate to draw than a short one. Scale the conversion up by ×10. 5 miles = 8 km  →  50 miles = 80 km
3
Choose scales so the graph fills the grid: miles across 0–50, kilometres up 0–80. Plot (0, 0) and (50, 80) and join them with a ruler, extending the line right across the grid. Point B: (50, 80)
A straight ruled line from (0, 0) to (50, 80)
Exam tip Plot a third point as a check — here (25, 40). If it does not lie on your line, one of your points is wrong.

2 ·  Reading values off the graph

The examiner wants to see how you got your reading — draw the dotted lines on the graph.

Miles → km (left to right)

  1. Find the number of miles on the horizontal axis.
  2. Draw a dotted line straight up to the graph.
  3. Turn and go straight across to the vertical axis.
  4. Read the value where it lands.

km → miles (right to left)

  1. Find the number of kilometres on the vertical axis.
  2. Draw a dotted line straight across to the graph.
  3. Turn and go straight down to the horizontal axis.
  4. Read the value where it lands.
How accurate is a reading? Mark schemes normally allow about half a small square either side of the true value. Read to the nearest sensible division — never quote a graph reading to several decimal places.
Example 2Use the miles–kilometres graph above to convert (a) 30 miles into kilometres, (b) 60 km into miles.
1
(a) Start on the miles axis at 30. Go vertically up to the line, then horizontally across to the kilometre axis. 30 miles → 48 km
2
Check against the rate: one mile is 8 ÷ 5 = 1.6 km. 30 × 1.6 = 48  ✓
3
(b) Work the other way: across from 60 on the kilometre axis to the line, then vertically down. The reading falls halfway between 37 and 38. 60 km → 37.5 miles
4
Check by converting back. 37.5 × 1.6 = 60  ✓
(a) 48 km     (b) 37.5 miles
Exam tip Leave the dotted lines on the paper. If your number is slightly outside the accepted range but the construction lines are correct, you can still pick up the method mark.

3 ·  The gradient is the conversion rate

For a graph through the origin the equation is y = mx, and m — the gradient — is exactly the number of y-units in one x-unit.

gradient = riserun = 80 − 050 − 0 = 1.6 1.6 kilometres in every mile
Note The graph drawn the other way round (km across, miles up) has gradient 1 ÷ 1.6 = 0.625. The two rates are reciprocals — multiplying by one undoes multiplying by the other.

A graph drawn to 50 miles cannot show 250 miles. You do not redraw it — read a value that is on the graph, then scale the answer by the same factor.

Example 3The conversion graph only goes up to 50 miles. Use it to convert 250 miles into kilometres.
1
Divide the distance by a convenient factor so it fits on the graph. 250 miles = 10 × 25 miles
2
Read 25 miles off the graph in the usual way. 25 miles → 40 km
3
Because the quantities are in direct proportion, multiply the reading by the same factor of 10. 250 miles = 10 × 40 = 400 km
250 miles ≈ 400 km   (check: 250 × 1.6 = 400)
Watch out This scaling trick works only when the line passes through the origin. On a graph with a fixed charge, doubling the distance does not double the cost.

4 ·  When the line misses the origin

Plenty of real conversion graphs are straight lines that do not go through the origin: a taxi fare with a fixed charge before you travel a single metre, or the Celsius-to-Fahrenheit scale, where 0°C is already 32°F. Reading the graph works exactly the same way — but the two quantities are no longer in direct proportion.

Two straight lines, only one of them proportional

Taxi fare

A fixed charge of $2.50 plus $1.20 per kilometre.

F = 1.20d + 2.50

The intercept is the fare before you move; the gradient is the cost of each extra kilometre.

Celsius to Fahrenheit

0°C = 32°F and 100°C = 212°F.

F = 1.8C + 32

Gradient (212 − 32) ÷ 100 = 1.8, intercept 32.

Example 4A taxi charges a fixed $2.50 plus $1.20 for each kilometre. Draw a conversion graph for 0 to 20 km and use it to find (a) the fare for 14 km, (b) the distance covered for a fare of $17.50.
1
Work out two points. At d = 0 the fare is just the fixed charge; at d = 20 add twenty lots of $1.20. (0, 2.50)  and  20 × 1.20 = 24.00, so (20, 26.50)
2
Plot the points and rule the line — it cuts the vertical axis at $2.50, not at the origin.
3
(a) Up from 14 on the distance axis to the line, then across to the fare axis. 2.50 + 14 × 1.20 = 2.50 + 16.80 = 19.30
4
(b) Across from 17.50 on the fare axis, then down. To confirm the reading, subtract the fixed charge first, then divide by the rate. 17.50 − 2.50 = 15.00  →  15.00 ÷ 1.20 = 12.5 km
(a) $19.30     (b) 12.5 km
Exam tip Money answers are written to 2 decimal places — write $19.30, never $19.3.

Practice Now you try

Do every question on paper first, then open the solution and mark your own working.

Practice 1A conversion graph for kilograms to pounds is to be drawn for 0 to 20 kg, using 1 kg = 2.2 lb. (a) Write down the coordinates of two points you would plot. (b) Use the graph to convert 15 kg into pounds.
1
(a) Direct proportion, so start at the origin and use the far end of the axis. (0, 0)  and  20 × 2.2 = 44, so (20, 44)
2
(b) Up from 15 kg, across to the pounds axis. 15 × 2.2 = 33
(a) (0, 0) and (20, 44)     (b) 33 lb
Practice 2Use the same graph to convert 27.5 lb into kilograms.
1
Across from 27.5 lb to the line, then down. Going this way you divide by the rate. 27.5 ÷ 2.2 = 12.5
12.5 kg   (check: 12.5 × 2.2 = 27.5)
Practice 3The graph only reaches 20 kg. Explain how you would use it to convert 170 kg into pounds, and give the answer.
1
The line goes through the origin, so scaling is allowed. Read 170 ÷ 10 = 17 kg off the graph. 17 kg → 17 × 2.2 = 37.4 lb
2
Multiply the reading back by the same factor of 10. 170 kg = 10 × 37.4 = 374 lb
374 lb
Practice 4A taxi fare graph is a straight line through (0, 3) and (12, 21), fare in dollars, distance in kilometres. Find (a) the fixed charge, (b) the cost per kilometre, (c) the fare for 8 km, (d) the distance travelled for a fare of $16.50.
1
(a) The intercept is the fare at zero distance. fixed charge = $3.00
2
(b) The cost per kilometre is the gradient. (21 − 3) ÷ 12 = 18 ÷ 12 = 1.50
3
(c) Substitute into F = 1.5d + 3. F = 1.5 × 8 + 3 = 12 + 3 = 15
4
(d) Subtract the fixed charge first, then divide by the rate. (16.50 − 3) ÷ 1.5 = 13.5 ÷ 1.5 = 9
(a) $3.00  (b) $1.50 per km  (c) $15.00  (d) 9 km
Practice 5The temperature conversion line passes through (0, 32) and (100, 212). (a) Write its equation. (b) Convert 35°C to °F. (c) Find the temperature at which both scales show the same number.
1
(a) Gradient 180 ÷ 100 = 1.8, intercept 32. F = 1.8C + 32
2
(b) Substitute C = 35. F = 1.8 × 35 + 32 = 63 + 32 = 95
3
(c) Set the two readings equal and solve. x = 1.8x + 32  →  −0.8x = 32  →  x = −40
(a) F = 1.8C + 32   (b) 95°F   (c) −40°

Common mistakes Lose no easy marks

Quoting a graph reading to lots of decimal places Writing “47.83 km” from a graph claims an accuracy you cannot possibly have. Readings are accepted to about half a small square — read to the nearest sensible division, and calculate from the rate when an exact answer is wanted.
Dividing before subtracting the intercept Going backwards on a line y = mx + c, the intercept comes off first: for F = 1.8C + 32, compute C = (F − 32) ÷ 1.8, never F ÷ 1.8 − 32.
Scaling on a line that misses the origin 20°C is not twice as warm as 10°C in Fahrenheit: 10°C = 50°F but 20°C = 68°F. Doubling, halving and the read-then-scale trick only work when the line passes through the origin.
Exam tip Always draw and leave the dotted reading lines on the grid — up then across, or across then down. They are the evidence that earns the method mark even if your reading drifts slightly.

Exit check Prove it before you leave

Exit 1What does the conversion graph of two quantities in direct proportion look like?
A straight line through the origin.
Exit 2A conversion graph with kilometres across and miles up passes through (0, 0) and (40, 25). What is its gradient, and what does it mean?
25 ÷ 40 = 0.625 — there are 0.625 miles in every kilometre.
Exit 3A fare graph has equation F = 1.5d + 3. What is the fare for 4 km?
F = 1.5 × 4 + 3 = $9.00
