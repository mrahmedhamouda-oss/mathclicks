Construction

Ruler-and-compasses constructions of triangles, bisectors and perpendiculars, and the four standard loci used to shade a region — marks you can bank with nothing but care.

Learning objectives What you will be able to do

  • Construct triangles accurately from SSS, SAS and ASA information, choosing the right tools.
  • Construct the perpendicular bisector of a line and the bisector of an angle with ruler and compasses only.
  • Recognise the four standard loci and draw each one correctly.
  • Shade a region that satisfies two or more locus conditions, with the correct solid or dashed boundaries.

1 ·  Equipment, accuracy and arcs

Construction questions are marks you can bank, because there is no algebra to go wrong — only care. Bring the right kit and use it properly.

What you need

  • A ruler marked in millimetres.
  • A pair of compasses stiff enough to hold its setting — tighten the screw.
  • A sharp pencil (HB). A blunt pencil costs you the tolerance.
  • A protractor, for angles that are given to you.

How accurate must you be?

  • Lengths within ±2 mm of the correct value.
  • Angles within ±2°.
  • Draw a rough sketch first so you know where the shape will sit on the page.
  • Work in pencil, not pen — but never rub out your arcs.

The golden rule: leave your arcs showing

Watch out “Using ruler and compasses only” means the protractor stays in the case. A bisector measured with a protractor scores zero, even if the line is in exactly the right place.

2 ·  Constructing triangles

There are three sets of information that fix a triangle exactly. Learn which tool opens each one.

You are givenNameToolsMethod in one line
Three sidesSSSruler + compassesDraw the longest side, then swing an arc from each end.
Two sides and the angle between themSASruler + protractorDraw one side, measure the angle, mark the second side along it.
Two angles and a sideASAruler + protractorDraw the side, draw both angles at its ends, extend until the rays meet.
Example 1Construct triangle ABC with AB = 7 cm, AC = 6 cm and BC = 5 cm. Measure angle BAC.
1
Draw the longest side accurately with the ruler and label its ends. AB = 7.0 cm
2
Open the compasses to 6 cm against the ruler. With the point on A, draw an arc above the line.
3
Open the compasses to 5 cm. With the point on B, draw a second arc that crosses the first. The crossing point is C.
4
Join AC and BC with the ruler, leave both arcs on the page, and measure angle BAC with a protractor. angle BAC ≈ 44°
C is where the arcs cross; angle BAC = 44° (accept 42° to 46°)
Note Starting with the longest side keeps the arcs crossing at a good wide angle, which makes the point C easy to see.
Example 2Construct triangle PQR with PQ = 6 cm, angle P = 50° and PR = 4.5 cm. Measure QR.
1
Draw PQ = 6 cm and label both ends.
2
Put the centre of the protractor exactly on P with the base line along PQ, and mark 50° with a fine dot.
3
Rule a line from P through that dot, and measure 4.5 cm along it to fix R.
4
Join QR and measure it. QR ≈ 4.6 cm
QR = 4.6 cm (accept 4.4 cm to 4.8 cm)
Watch out Protractors have two scales. Angle P = 50° is clearly acute, so if your line looks obtuse you have read the wrong row of numbers.

3 ·  The two compass constructions

These appear again and again, both on their own and inside locus questions. In each one you keep the compasses at the same setting for a pair of arcs — that is what makes the construction exact.

Perpendicular bisector of AB

  1. Open the compasses to more than half of AB.
  2. Centre A: arcs above and below the line.
  3. Same radius, centre B: two more arcs, crossing the first pair.
  4. Join the two crossing points. It cuts AB in half at 90°.

Bisector of an angle

  1. Centre the vertex V: one arc crossing both arms, at P and Q.
  2. Centre P: an arc inside the angle.
  3. Same radius, centre Q: an arc crossing it at R.
  4. Join VR. The angle is now split into two equal parts.
Key idea Both constructions work because the arcs build a rhombus. Every side of a rhombus is equal, and its diagonals bisect each other at right angles and bisect its angles. That is the whole proof.

4 ·  Loci

Definition A locus (plural loci) is the set of all the points that satisfy a rule. Read the rule, decide which of the four standard pictures it is, then draw it.

Fixed distance from a point → circle

“Exactly 3 cm from P” is the circle itself; “less than 3 cm” is the region inside it.

Fixed distance from a line → racetrack

Two parallel lines a distance d away, joined by a semicircle at each end — the ends are the bit students forget.

Equidistant from two points → perpendicular bisector

“Nearer to A than to B” is the side of that line containing A.

Equidistant from two lines → angle bisector

“Nearer to one wall than the other” means the angle bisector of the corner where they meet.

Shading a region Draw each locus separately with arcs showing, decide which side of each boundary you want, shade only the overlap and label it R. Use a solid boundary when equality is allowed (“not more than 4 m”) and a dashed one when it is not (“less than 4 m”).
Example 3Two straight paths meet at a corner V. A bench must be the same distance from both paths and exactly 5 m from V. Describe how to find its position.
1
“Same distance from both paths” is the locus equidistant from two lines, so construct the bisector of angle V with compasses.
2
“Exactly 5 m from V” is the locus a fixed distance from a point, so draw an arc of radius 5 m centred on V, using the scale of the drawing.
3
The bench is where the two loci cross. Mark it clearly with a cross and label it.
The bench is where the angle bisector meets the arc of radius 5 m from V
Exam tip When a question says “equidistant”, it is telling you which construction to use. Two points → perpendicular bisector. Two lines → angle bisector.
Example 4A goat is tied by a 4 m rope to a post at A. A second post at B, 6 m from A, has a second goat on a 4 m rope. Shade the region that both goats can reach.
1
Each rope gives a locus “not more than 4 m from a point”, which is the region inside a circle of radius 4 m. Draw both circles with compasses, using the scale given.
2
“Both goats” means the region must satisfy both conditions at once, so you want the overlap of the two circles.
3
Shade only that lens-shaped overlap and label it R. Draw the boundaries solid, because the goat can reach exactly 4 m.
R is the lens-shaped overlap of the two circles
Watch out If the posts had been more than 8 m apart the circles would not meet at all and the answer would be “no such region”. Always check the two loci really do cross.

Practice Now you try

Do every question on paper first, then open the solution and mark your own working.

Practice 1Which construction gives the set of points the same distance from two fixed points A and B?
1
Two points, so bisect the line joining them.
The perpendicular bisector of AB.
Practice 2Describe the locus of points exactly 5 cm from a fixed point O, and the locus of points less than 5 cm from O.
1
A fixed distance from a point is always a circle; a strict inequality means the boundary is not included.
Exactly 5 cm: a circle of radius 5 cm, centre O. Less than 5 cm: the region inside that circle, with a dashed boundary.
Practice 3You must construct triangle XYZ with XY = 8 cm, XZ = 5 cm and YZ = 6 cm. Which side would you draw first, and why? Outline the construction.
1
This is SSS, so ruler and compasses. Draw the longest side first. XY = 8.0 cm
2
Arc of radius 5 cm from X; arc of radius 6 cm from Y. They cross at Z — join up and leave the arcs showing.
Draw XY = 8 cm first (the longest side), so the two arcs cross cleanly at Z.
Practice 4Explain why the perpendicular bisector construction needs the compasses opened to more than half of AB.
1
Each arc reaches at most its radius from its centre. If the radius were less than half of AB, the arcs from A and B would never meet.
With radius less than half AB the arcs never cross, so there are no crossing points to join. Equal radii also make the four points form a rhombus, which is what guarantees the bisector.
Practice 5A park is a rectangle PQRS with PQ = 60 m and PS = 40 m. A fountain must be nearer to P than to Q, and not more than 25 m from PS. Describe the two boundaries you would draw and the region you would shade.
1
“Nearer to P than to Q” — two points, so draw the perpendicular bisector of PQ and keep the half containing P.
2
“Not more than 25 m from PS” — a fixed distance from a line inside the park, so draw a line parallel to PS, 25 m from it, and keep the side towards PS. This boundary is solid because 25 m is allowed.
3
Shade the overlap of the two half-regions and label it R.
Perpendicular bisector of PQ (keep P’s side) and a solid line parallel to PS at 25 m (keep PS’s side); R is the overlap.

Common mistakes Lose no easy marks

Rubbing out the construction arcs The arcs are the evidence. The mark scheme says “correct arcs”, so a perfect answer with no arcs can only score the accuracy mark, never the method mark. Draw them clearly and leave them alone.
Reaching for the protractor when it is banned “Using ruler and compasses only” means exactly that. A bisector measured with a protractor scores zero even if it lands in the right place — and the protractor’s two scales also trip up SAS drawings, so check an angle looks acute when it should be.
Forgetting the ends and the boundary style in loci The locus a fixed distance from a segment has semicircular ends, not square ones. And a “less than” condition needs a dashed boundary, while “not more than” needs a solid one.
Exam tip “Equidistant” tells you which construction to use: two points → perpendicular bisector; two lines → angle bisector. Learn that pairing and locus questions become quick.

Exit 1What is the locus of points equidistant from two intersecting lines?
