Factorising

Expanding in reverse — take out common factors, factorise by grouping, spot the difference of two squares and crack simple quadratics.

Learning objectives What you will be able to do

  • Factorise fully by taking out the highest common factor of the numbers and the letters.
  • Factorise a four-term expression by grouping it into two pairs.
  • Recognise and factorise a difference of two squares on sight: a² − b² = (a + b)(a − b).
  • Factorise quadratics of the form x² + bx + c using product and sum.
  • Check every factorisation in seconds by expanding it back out.

1 ·  Taking out the highest common factor

When you expand, you remove brackets; when you factorise, you put them back. Every factorising answer can therefore be checked in seconds by expanding it again — and you should always do that check.

3x(2x − 5)   ← factorise — expand →   6x² − 15x Two ways of writing the same expression
Definition The highest common factor (HCF) of a set of terms is the largest expression that divides exactly into every one of them. To factorise, take the HCF outside a bracket and write what is left inside.

Deal with the numbers and the letters separately:

Numbers

Take the HCF of the coefficients. For 12 and 18 that is 6.

Letters

Take each letter that appears in every term, to its lowest power. From x³y and x²y² that gives x²y.

Example 1Factorise fully (a) 12x³y − 18x²y²  (b) −8a² + 12ab.
1
(a) Numbers: HCF of 12 and 18 is 6. Letters: take x² (lowest power of x) and y (lowest power of y). HCF = 6x²y
2
Divide each term by the HCF to find what goes inside. 12x³y ÷ 6x²y = 2x
18x²y² ÷ 6x²y = 3y
3
Write the answer and check by expanding. 6x²y(2x − 3y)  ✓
4
(b) The HCF is 4a, but the leading term is negative, so take out −4a — the signs inside then flip. −8a² ÷ (−4a) = 2a
12ab ÷ (−4a) = −3b
5
Check by expanding. −4a(2a − 3b) = −8a² + 12ab  ✓
(a) 6x²y(2x − 3y)     (b) −4a(2a − 3b)
Watch out “Factorise fully” means the bracket must have no common factor left. Writing 2x²y(6x − 9y) is not wrong arithmetic, but it is not fully factorised and it will not get the mark.

2 ·  Factorising by grouping

An expression with four terms often has no factor common to all four, but splits into two pairs that each have one. Factorise each pair — if the brackets that appear are identical, take that whole bracket out as the common factor.

ax + ay + bx + by = a(x + y) + b(x + y) = (x + y)(a + b) The repeated bracket is itself the common factor
Example 2Factorise 6x + 3y + 2ax + ay.
1
Split into two pairs: the first two share 3, the last two share a. (6x + 3y) + (2ax + ay)
2
Factorise each pair — the same bracket appears twice. 3(2x + y) + a(2x + y)
3
Take the repeated bracket out, and check by expanding. (2x + y)(3 + a) = 6x + 2ax + 3y + ay  ✓
(2x + y)(3 + a)
Exam tip If the two brackets do not match, re-pair the terms in a different order before deciding it will not work.
Watch out When the third term is negative, factorise the second pair with a negative outside so the brackets match: 2x² + 6x − x − 3 = 2x(x + 3) − 1(x + 3) = (x + 3)(2x − 1). Notice the −1; leaving it out is the classic slip.

3 ·  The difference of two squares

Expand (x + 5)(x − 5) and the middle terms destroy each other: x² − 5x + 5x − 25 = x² − 25. Read that backwards and you have a factorisation to recognise on sight.

a² − b² = (a + b)(a − b) Two squares, subtracted — and nothing in between

How to spot it

  • Exactly two terms, with a minus between them.
  • Each term is a perfect square: a square number, and every letter to an even power. 9x² = (3x)², 25 = 5², y6 = (y³)².
  • a² + b² does not factorise. Only the difference does.
Example 3Factorise (a) 9x² − 25  (b) 2x² − 32.
1
(a) Write each term as a square, then apply the rule with a = 3x, b = 5. 9x² = (3x)²,  25 = 5²
(3x + 5)(3x − 5)
2
Check: the outer and inner products cancel. 9x² − 15x + 15x − 25 = 9x² − 25  ✓
3
(b) 2x² is not a perfect square, so look for a common factor first. 2x² − 32 = 2(x² − 16)
4
Now the bracket is a difference of two squares. 2(x + 4)(x − 4)
(a) (3x + 5)(3x − 5)     (b) 2(x + 4)(x − 4)
Exam tip The difference of two squares also makes arithmetic easy without a calculator: 83² − 17² = (83 + 17)(83 − 17) = 100 × 66 = 6600.

4 ·  Factorising x² + bx + c

Expanding (x + p)(x + q) gives x² + (p + q)x + pq. To reverse it, find two numbers whose product is c and whose sum is b — always start with the product.

x² + bx + c = (x + p)(x + q)   where   pq = c  and  p + q = b Product then sum — always start with the product
Signs in x² + bx + cWhat p and q look likeExample
c positive, b positiveboth positivex² + 7x + 12 = (x + 3)(x + 4)
c positive, b negativeboth negativex² − 7x + 12 = (x − 3)(x − 4)
c negativeone of each sign; the larger takes the sign of bx² + x − 12 = (x + 4)(x − 3)
Example 4Factorise x² − 3x − 40.
1
c = −40 is negative, so the two numbers have opposite signs. pairs multiplying to 40: 1×40, 2×20, 4×10, 5×8
2
With opposite signs the pair must differ by 3, and the bigger number is negative because b = −3. use +5 and −8:  5 × (−8) = −40 ✓  5 + (−8) = −3 ✓
3
Write the brackets and check by expanding. (x + 5)(x − 8) = x² − 8x + 5x − 40  ✓
(x + 5)(x − 8)
Example 5Factorise completely 3x³ − 12x.
1
Common factor first: HCF of 3 and 12 is 3, and both terms contain x. 3x(x² − 4)
2
The bracket is a difference of two squares, so keep going. x² − 4 = (x + 2)(x − 2)
3
Put it together — “completely” means nothing factorises any further. 3x(x + 2)(x − 2)
3x(x + 2)(x − 2)
Watch out Stopping at 3x(x² − 4) loses a mark. Whenever a bracket is a difference of two squares, it can still be split.

Common mistakes Lose no easy marks

Not factorising fully A leftover common factor inside the bracket, or an unfactorised difference of two squares, costs the mark. 2x²y(6x − 9y) and 3x(x² − 4) are both unfinished.
Losing the −1 when grouping In 2x² + 6x − x − 3 the second pair needs −1(x + 3), not (x + 3) on its own — otherwise the brackets do not match and the signs go wrong.
Trying to factorise a sum of squares a² + b² does not factorise. Only the difference a² − b² splits into (a + b)(a − b).
Exam tip Every factorisation can be checked by expanding it back out — ten seconds that catches nearly every sign slip. Make the check a habit, especially when c is negative.
