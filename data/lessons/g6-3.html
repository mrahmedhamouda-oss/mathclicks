Core Concepts

1. What a median is

A median is the segment that connects a vertex to the exact middle of the side across from it. It is the "balance line" of the triangle — it always splits the opposite side into two congruent halves.

median — In a triangle, a median is a line segment with endpoints that are a vertex of the triangle and the midpoint of the side opposite the vertex.
centroid — Every triangle has three medians that are concurrent. The point of concurrency of the medians of a triangle is called the centroid, and it is always inside the triangle.
In △ABC, if L is the midpoint of AC,
then BL is a median and AL ≅ LC.

Every triangle has exactly three medians (one from each vertex), and all three always cross at a single point.

2. The Centroid Theorem — the 2 : 1 rule

The centroid does not sit halfway along a median. It sits two-thirds of the way down from the vertex, which leaves one-third for the other piece. That is why the two pieces are always in a 2 : 1 ratio.

Theorem 6.7 · Centroid Theorem
The medians of a triangle intersect at a point called the centroid that is two-thirds of the distance from each vertex to the midpoint of the opposite side.
If P is the centroid of △ABC and BL is a median, then
BP = 2⁄3 (BL)    PL = 1⁄3 (BL)
BP = 2(PL)    BP : PL = 2 : 1
BL = 3(PL) = 3⁄2 (BP)

Why it works: the whole median splits into 2 parts + 1 part = 3 equal parts. Once you know any one of the three quantities (whole median, long piece, short piece), the other two follow immediately.

1
Long piece (vertex → centroid) = 2 parts. Short piece (centroid → midpoint) = 1 part. Whole median = 3 parts.

3. Balance point / center of gravity

All polygons have a balancing point, or center of gravity. This is the point at which the weight of a region is evenly dispersed and all sides of the region are balanced. The centroid is the center of gravity for a triangular region.

Study Tip: "Centroid = center of gravity" is a useful memory hook. If you cut a triangle out of cardboard and balance it on a pencil point, the pencil sits on the centroid.

4. Finding a centroid on the coordinate plane

Because the centroid is the balance point, its coordinates are just the averages of the three vertices' coordinates.

For △ABC with A(x1, y1), B(x2, y2), C(x3, y3):
centroid = ( (x1 + x2 + x3)/3 , (y1 + y2 + y3)/3 )

You can also get there the long way: find the midpoint of one side, then travel 2⁄3 of the way from the opposite vertex toward that midpoint. The averaging formula is the same answer, only faster.

5. What an altitude is

An altitude is the "height" segment: drop straight down from a vertex, at a right angle, onto the line that holds the opposite side.

altitude of a triangle — a segment from a vertex of the triangle to the line containing the opposite side and perpendicular to that side.
orthocenter — Every triangle has three altitudes. If extended, the altitudes of a triangle are concurrent. The point of concurrency is called the orthocenter.

Notice the exact wording: to the line containing the opposite side. That phrase is what allows an altitude to land outside the triangle. An altitude can lie in the interior, in the exterior, or on the side of the triangle.

Watch Out! A median and an altitude are different segments. A median goes to the midpoint (it may hit the side at any angle). An altitude goes in perpendicular (it may hit the side anywhere, or miss the side entirely). They coincide only when the triangle is isosceles and you draw from the apex.

6. Where the orthocenter lives

Unlike the centroid, the orthocenter is not always inside the triangle. Its location tells you what kind of triangle you have.

Triangle typeOrthocenter locationAltitudes
AcuteInside the triangleAll three feet land on the sides
RightExactly at the right-angle vertexThe two legs are two of the altitudes
ObtuseOutside the triangleTwo altitudes land on extensions of the sides
2
The centroid is always inside. The orthocenter moves: inside → on the vertex → outside as the triangle goes acute → right → obtuse.

7. Finding an orthocenter on the coordinate plane

Two altitudes are enough — the third is guaranteed to pass through the same point, so you can use it as a check.

Graph △ABC so you can see roughly where the answer should land.
Pick a side, say AC, and compute its slope m.
The altitude from the opposite vertex B is perpendicular to that side, so its slope is the opposite reciprocal, −1/m.
Write that altitude's equation using point-slope form through vertex B.
Repeat steps 2–4 for a second side to get a second altitude equation.
Solve the system of the two equations. The solution (x, y) is the orthocenter.
Perpendicular slopes: m1 · m2 = −1  →  m2 = −1/m1
Point-slope form: y − y1 = m(x − x1)
Study Tip: Always look for a horizontal or vertical side first. If a side is horizontal, the altitude to it is vertical (x = a constant) — that instantly gives you the x-coordinate of the orthocenter with no algebra at all.
3
Unlike the centroid, you cannot average the vertices to get the orthocenter. There is no shortcut formula — you must intersect two altitudes.

Worked Examples

In △ABC, P is the centroid and BL = 6. Find BP and PL.
1BP = 2⁄3 BLCentroid Theorem
2BP = 2⁄3 (6) = 4BL = 6
3BP + PL = 6Segment Addition Postulate
44 + PL = 6BP = 4
5PL = 2Subtract.
BP = 4  and  PL = 2
Check: BP : PL = 4 : 2 = 2 : 1 ✓  and 4 + 2 = 6 = BL ✓
Watch Out! 2⁄3 applies to the whole median BL, not to the piece PL. Multiplying PL by 2⁄3 is the most common error in this lesson.
In △LMN, PY = 7. Find LP.

Because LX ≅ XM, X is the midpoint of LM, and NX is a median of △LMN. Likewise, Y and Z are the midpoints of MN and LN respectively, so LY and MZ are also medians of △LMN. Therefore, point P is the centroid of △LMN.

1LP = 2⁄3 LYCentroid Theorem
2LP = 2⁄3 (LP + PY)Segment Addition and Substitution
3LP = 2⁄3 (LP + 7)PY = 7
4LP = 2⁄3 LP + 14⁄3Distributive Property
51⁄3 LP = 14⁄3Subtract 2⁄3 LP from each side.
6LP = 14Multiply each side by 3.
LP = 14
Faster route: PY is the short piece (1 part) and LP is the long piece (2 parts), so LP = 2(PY) = 2(7) = 14 ✓. The algebra above proves that the shortcut is legitimate.
A triangular region has vertices A(1, 1), B(11, 5) and C(7, 10). Find the midpoint of AB and the coordinates of the balance point (centroid) of △ABC.
1mid AB = ( (1 + 11)/2 , (1 + 5)/2 )Midpoint Formula
2mid AB = (6, 3)Simplify.
3centroid = ( (1 + 11 + 7)/3 , (1 + 5 + 10)/3 )average the vertices
4centroid = ( 19/3 , 16/3 )Simplify.
midpoint of AB = (6, 3)  ·  centroid = ( 19⁄3 , 16⁄3 ) ≈ (6.33, 5.33)
Check with the Centroid Theorem: travel 2⁄3 of the way from C(7, 10) to the midpoint (6, 3).
x = 7 + 2⁄3(6 − 7) = 7 − 2⁄3 = 19⁄3 ✓   y = 10 + 2⁄3(3 − 10) = 10 − 14⁄3 = 16⁄3 ✓
Assumption to notice: averaging the three vertices assumes the region has uniform density, so the geometric centroid really is the balance point.
The vertices of △ABC are A(4, 0), B(−2, 4) and C(0, 6). Find the coordinates of the orthocenter of △ABC.

Step 1 — Graph △ABC. To find the orthocenter of △ABC, find the point where two of the three altitudes intersect.

Step 2 — Find equations of the altitudes.

Altitude from B to AC

slope of AC = (6 − 0)/(0 − 4) = −3⁄2
slope of altitude = 2⁄3
y − 4 = 2⁄3(x + 2)
y = 2⁄3 x + 16⁄3

Altitude from A to BC

slope of BC = (6 − 4)/(0 + 2) = 1
slope of altitude = −1
y − 0 = −1(x − 4)
y = −x + 4

Step 3 — Solve the system of equations.

1−x + 4 = 2⁄3 x + 16⁄3Substitution
2−3x + 12 = 2x + 16Multiply each side by 3.
3−5x = 4  →  x = −4⁄5Solve for x.
4y = −(−4⁄5) + 4 = 24⁄5Substitute back.
orthocenter = ( −4⁄5 , 24⁄5 ) = (−0.8, 4.8)
Check with the third altitude: slope of AB = (4 − 0)/(−2 − 4) = −2⁄3, so the altitude from C has slope 3⁄2: y = 3⁄2 x + 6. At x = −0.8: y = −1.2 + 6 = 4.8 ✓ All three altitudes concur.
Watch Out! The orthocenter is not the average of the vertices. Averaging A(4, 0), B(−2, 4), C(0, 6) gives ( 2⁄3 , 10⁄3 ) — that is the centroid, a completely different point.

Vocabulary check

median — vertex to midpoint of the opposite side.
centroid — point of concurrency of the three medians; always interior.
altitude of a triangle — vertex perpendicular to the line containing the opposite side.
orthocenter — point of concurrency of the three altitudes; may be inside, on, or outside.
Common mistakes to avoid
  • Applying 2⁄3 to a piece of the median instead of the whole median.
  • Assuming the centroid is the midpoint of the median — it is not; it is 2⁄3 of the way down.
  • Confusing a median with an altitude. Median → midpoint. Altitude → right angle.
  • Averaging the vertices to find an orthocenter. That formula only gives the centroid.
  • Forgetting that in an obtuse triangle two altitudes hit the extensions of the sides, so the orthocenter is outside.
  • Using the slope of the side instead of its opposite reciprocal when writing an altitude's equation.
  • Forgetting the negative sign: a slope of 3⁄2 gives a perpendicular slope of −2⁄3, not 2⁄3.
