Core Concepts

1 · What a circle actually is

A circle is not "a round shape" — it is a distance rule. Pick a point, then collect every point in the plane that sits exactly the same distance from it. That collection is the circle.

A circle is the set of all points in a plane that are the same distance from a given point
called the center of the circle.

A circle is named by its center. A circle whose center is C is written ☉C and read "circle C". That single symbol tells you everything: same center, same rule.

Notice: the center is not part of the circle. The circle is only the rim — the points at distance r. The center is at distance 0, so it is in the interior, not on the circle.

2 · Today's vocabulary

circle — the set of all points in a plane the same distance from a given point.
center of a circle — the given point that every point of the circle is measured from.
radius of a circle — a segment from the center to a point on the circle. Plural: radii.
chord of a circle — a segment whose endpoints are both on the circle.
diameter of a circle — a chord that passes through the center of the circle.
pi (π) — the irrational number equal to the ratio C/d for every circle.
concentric circles — coplanar circles that have the same center.
congruent circles — circles with congruent radii (Postulate 10.1).
Words that do double duty: radius and diameter name segments ("CF is a radius") and they name lengths ("the radius is 7 ft"). Read the sentence to tell which one is meant.
Watch out — chord vs. diameter. Every diameter is a chord, but most chords are not diameters. A chord only earns the name "diameter" when it goes through the center. The diameter is the longest chord a circle has.

3 · Radius and diameter relationships

A diameter is built out of two radii laid end to end, so the diameter is always exactly twice the radius. Turn that around and the radius is half the diameter.

Key Concept · Radius and Diameter Relationships
If a circle has radius r and diameter d, then the following relationships are true.
Radius Formula   r = d/2   or   r = ½d
Diameter Formula   d = 2r
1
All radii of a circle are congruent. That is the whole definition of a circle restated: every point on it is the same distance from the center.
2
Because a diameter is composed of two radii, all diameters of a circle are also congruent.

4 · Pi and the circumference formula

The circumference of a circle is the distance around it. Measure the circumference of any circle, divide by its diameter, and you always get the same number. That constant ratio is pi, written π, and it is irrational — its decimal never ends and never repeats.

Both circumference formulas come straight out of that one definition:

C/d = π   — Definition of pi
C = πd   — Multiply each side by d
C = π(2r)   — Substitute d = 2r
C = 2πr   — Simplify
Key Concept · Circumference Formula
Words: If a circle has diameter d or radius r, the circumference C equals the diameter times pi or twice the radius times pi.
Symbols:   C = πd   or   C = 2πr
Study Tip — levels of accuracy. Because π is irrational, its value cannot be written as a terminating decimal. Using 3 for π gives a quick mental estimate. Using 3.14 or 22/7 is closer. For the most accurate answer, use the π key on a calculator. Unless a problem says otherwise, assume the π key was used.
Watch out — r or d? C = πd and C = 2πr are the same formula. The classic error is feeding a radius into C = πd (which halves your answer) or a diameter into C = 2πr (which doubles it). Before you compute, write down which one you were given.

5 · Pairs of circles

As with other figures, pairs of circles can be congruent, similar, or share other special relationships. Because a circle is completely determined by its center and its radius, comparing two circles reduces to comparing two numbers.

Postulate 10.1
Two circles are congruent if and only if they have congruent radii.
Theorem 10.1
Words: All circles are similar.
Why: every circle has the same shape — you can always scale one circle onto another by the ratio of their radii, so a similarity transformation always exists.

Concentric circles are coplanar circles that have the same center. Two concentric circles never intersect, because every point of one is a different distance from the shared center than every point of the other.

Study Tip — points of intersection. Two distinct circles can meet in 2 points (they cross), 1 point (they are tangent — externally or internally), or 0 points (one is completely outside or completely inside the other, including the concentric case). Two circles can never meet in exactly 3 points.

Trap: RS looks like it "almost" reaches the center, but it misses it — it crosses ZT at Y, not at X. Close is not through. RS is a chord only. Also note Y is not a point of the circle, so XY is not a radius.
If TU = 14 feet, what is the radius of ☉Q?
1TU passes through Q ∴ TU is a diameter, d = 14definition of diameter
2r = d/2Radius Formula
3r = 14/2 = 7substitute and simplify
The radius of ☉Q is 7 feet.
Check: d = 2r = 2(7) = 14 ft ✓ — the diameter formula returns the value we started with.
Check  ·  If LM = 11 inches, what is the diameter of ☉L?
1L is the center ∴ LM is a radius, r = 11definition of radius
2d = 2r = 2(11) = 22Diameter Formula
The diameter of ☉L is 22 inches.
Compare the two parts. In the first problem you were handed a diameter and had to halve it; here you were handed a radius and had to double it. Always identify which segment you were given before you touch the formula.
A circular path has every point 18 meters from its center. Find the circumference of the path, exactly in terms of π and to the nearest hundredth.
1C = 2πrCircumference Formula
2C = 2π(18)substitution
3C = 36πsimplify
4C ≈ 113.10use a calculator (π key)
C = 36π m ≈ 113.10 m
Why keep 36π? 36π is the exact answer; 113.10 is only a rounded one. Whenever a question says "in terms of π", stop at 36π — do not multiply out.
Check with an estimate: using π ≈ 3, C ≈ 2(3)(18) = 108, which is close to 113.10 ✓

Step A — find the diameter.

1C = πdCircumference Formula

424.76 ≈ duse a calculator

Step B — use the diameter to find the radius.

5r = ½dRadius Formula

Check  ·  Find the diameter and radius of a circle to the nearest hundredth if the circumference is 94.2 yards.

Part A — quick estimate (π ≈ 3)

d ≈ 94.2 ÷ 3 = 31.4 yd
r ≈ ½(31.4) = 15.7 yd

Part B — exact to the hundredth (π key)

Levels of accuracy in action. The π ≈ 3 estimate (31.4) is useful for checking that your answer is in the right neighbourhood, but it is about 1.4 yd too big. Report the π-key value unless told otherwise.
The diameter of ☉K is 12 units, the diameter of ☉J is 20 units, and JD = 8 units. Find EK.

Step A — work inside ☉J to get DE.

1diameter of ☉J = 20 ∴ JE = 10JE is a radius of ☉J; r = ½d
2JD + DE = JESegment Addition Postulate
38 + DE = 10substitution
4DE = 2solve

Step B — work inside ☉K to get EK.

5diameter of ☉K = 12 ∴ DK = 6DK is a radius of ☉K; r = ½d
6DE + EK = DKSegment Addition Postulate
72 + EK = 6substitution
8EK = 4solve
EK = 4 units
Check: JK = JD + DE + EK = 8 + 2 + 4 = 14. Then JE = JK − EK = 14 − 4 = 10 ✓ (radius of ☉J) and DK = JK − JD = 14 − 8 = 6 ✓ (radius of ☉K).
The key move: DE is part of both radii — it is a piece of JE (radius of ☉J) and a piece of DK (radius of ☉K). That shared piece is what links the two circles. Find it first, then move to the second circle.

Problem 3

A circle has radius r = x/8. Find the diameter and the circumference in terms of x and π.
1d = 2r = 2(x/8)Diameter Formula
2d = 2x/8 = x/4simplify the fraction
3C = πd = π(x/4)Circumference Formula
4C = πx/4simplify
d = x/4  ·  C = πx/4
Sanity test: let x = 8. Then r = 1, d = 2 and C = 2π. The general answers give d = 8/4 = 2 ✓ and C = 8π/4 = 2π ✓

Problem 4

The diameter of ☉A is 26 units and the diameter of ☉B is 14 units. Points A, M, N, B are collinear in that order, M lies on ☉B, N lies on ☉A, and AM = 9. Find NB.
1diameter of ☉A = 26 ∴ AN = 13AN is a radius of ☉A
2AM + MN = AN → 9 + MN = 13Segment Addition Postulate
3MN = 4solve
4diameter of ☉B = 14 ∴ MB = 7MB is a radius of ☉B
5MN + NB = MB → 4 + NB = 7Segment Addition Postulate
6NB = 3solve
NB = 3 units
Check: AB = 9 + 4 + 3 = 16? No — AB = AM + MN + NB = 9 + 4 + 3 = 16 would put B 16 from A, but AN = 13 and NB = 3 give AB = 16 as well ✓ and MB = AB − AM = 16 − 9 = 7 ✓

Problem 5

Two concentric circles have radii 5 and 9. Find the difference of their circumferences, exactly in terms of π.
1C₂ = 2π(9) = 18πcircumference of the outer circle
2C₁ = 2π(5) = 10πcircumference of the inner circle
3C₂ − C₁ = 18π − 10πsubtract
4= 8πsimplify
8π units ≈ 25.13 units
Pattern worth remembering: the difference of circumferences is 2π(R − r) — it depends only on the difference of the radii, never on how big the circles are. Radii 105 and 109 give the same answer, 8π.

Problem 6

☉P has radius r and ☉Q has radius 3r. Compare their circumferences, and state what happens to the circumference of a circle when its radius is tripled.
1Cₕ = 2πrCircumference Formula for ☉P
2Cₘ = 2π(3r)substitute 3r for the radius
3Cₘ = 3(2πr) = 3CₕAssociative and Commutative Properties
Cₘ = 3Cₕ — tripling the radius triples the circumference.
Why: circumference is a linear function of the radius, C = 2πr. Multiply r by k and C is multiplied by the same k. (Area, which uses r², does not behave this way.)

☉
A circle is named by its center: ☉C. Two circles are congruent if and only if their radii are congruent, and all circles are similar.
π
π is the ratio C/d for every circle, and it is irrational. "In terms of π" means leave the π in the answer (36π), not 113.10.
≡
Within one circle, all radii are congruent and all diameters are congruent. That is what lets you transfer a length from one part of a figure to another.
Common mistakes to avoid
  • Putting a radius into C = πd (answer comes out half of what it should be).
  • Putting a diameter into C = 2πr (answer comes out double).
  • Calling every chord a diameter. A chord is a diameter only if it passes through the center.
  • Multiplying out 36π when the question asked for an exact answer in terms of π.
  • Rounding mid-problem: use the full calculator value, round only at the very end.
  • Using 3.14 (or 3) when the π key is available — it changes the hundredths digit.
  • Forgetting that concentric circles are not congruent — same center, but different radii.
  • Assuming a point that looks like the center actually is the center. Only labelled information counts.
