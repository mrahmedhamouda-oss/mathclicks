Core Concepts

1. Why we need a new tool

Right-triangle trigonometry (SOH-CAH-TOA) only works when a triangle has a 90° angle. Most triangles do not. The Law of Sines is the rule that lets you set up a proportion in any triangle — right or oblique — as long as you can pair up an angle with the side directly across from it.

Naming convention. In △ABC, the lowercase letter always names the side opposite the matching capital letter: side a is opposite ∠A, side b is opposite ∠B, side c is opposite ∠C. Every Law of Sines problem starts by finding these angle–side pairs.

Theorem 9.10 · Law of Sines
If △ABC has lengths a, b, and c, representing the lengths of the sides opposite the angles with measures A, B, and C, then
sin A / a  =  sin B / b  =  sin C / c
Because these are equal ratios, you may flip all three at once and use the equally valid form
a / sin A  =  b / sin B  =  c / sin C
Use the first form when you are solving for an angle (the unknown lands in the numerator); use the second form when you are solving for a side.

2. When can you use it?

You can solve a triangle with the Law of Sines whenever you know two angles and any side (AAS or ASA), or two sides and an angle opposite one of them (SSA).

AAS — two angles and a non-included side. Exactly one triangle. Pair the known side with its own angle, then cross-multiply.
ASA — two angles and the included side. Exactly one triangle. First use the Triangle Angle-Sum Theorem to get the third angle, which turns the problem into AAS.
SSA — two sides and an angle opposite one of them. This is the ambiguous case: 0, 1, or 2 triangles are possible.
SSS / SAS — cannot be started with the Law of Sines, because no angle–side pair is complete. Those need the Law of Cosines.

3. The method, step by step

Label the triangle so every lowercase side sits opposite its capital angle.
Find one complete angle–side pair (an angle whose opposite side you know). That pair is the ratio you can actually evaluate.
Set that ratio equal to the ratio containing the unknown. Use only two ratios at a time.
Cross multiply, then divide to isolate the unknown.
If the unknown is an angle, finish with sin−1. If it is a side, just evaluate. Keep the calculator in degree mode.

4. Vocabulary

ambiguous case — the SSA situation, in which the given measures of two sides and a non-included angle may produce zero, one, or two different triangles.
altitude h — the minimum distance from ∠C to AB when ∠A is acute. Since sin A = h/b, we get h = b sin A. Comparing a to h is what decides the ambiguous case.
Postulate 9.1 · Sine of an Obtuse Angle
Words: The sine of an obtuse angle is defined to be the sine of its supplement.
Symbols: If m∠A > 90° and x = sin A, then x = sin (180° − A).
sin 141° = sin 39°     sin 109° = sin 71°
This is exactly why SSA is ambiguous: two different angles — one acute, one obtuse — share the same sine, so sin−1 only ever hands you the acute one. You must check the obtuse partner yourself.

5. Key Concept · Possible Triangles in the SSA Case

You are given a, b, and m∠A; let h = b sin A be the altitude. Compare a with h and with b.

∠A is acute:

ComparisonNumber of trianglesWhy
a < hno solutionside a is too short to reach the base
a = hone solutiona just touches — a right triangle
h < a < btwo solutionsa crosses the base twice
a ≥ bone solutionone crossing lands behind vertex A, so it is rejected

∠A is right or obtuse:

ComparisonNumber of trianglesWhy
a ≤ bno solutionthe side opposite the largest angle must be the largest side
a > bone solution∠B must be acute, so only one value works
Study Tip · ∠A is acute. The altitude h is compared to a because h is the minimum distance from ∠C to AB. Anything shorter than h simply cannot reach. Remember sin A = opposite / hypotenuse = h/b.

Worked Examples

Find the value of x to the nearest tenth.
m∠A = 58°, a = 9, m∠C = 35°, c = x
1sin A / a = sin C / cLaw of Sines
2sin 58° / 9 = sin 35° / xm∠A = 58°, a = 9, m∠C = 35°, c = x
3x sin 58° = 9 sin 35°Multiplication Property of Equality
4x = (9 sin 35°) / sin 58°Divide each side by sin 58°
5x ≈ 5.1622 / 0.8480 ≈ 6.0872Use a calculator
x ≈ 6.1
Check: 35° < 58°, so the side opposite 35° must be shorter than the side opposite 58°. Indeed 6.1 < 9 ✓
Trap: do not pair 9 with 35°. The 9 sits opposite ∠A, so it belongs in the ratio with sin 58°.

Check (multiple choice): a triangle has angles 18° and 141° with the side between them equal to 13; find x, the side opposite 141°.
Third angle = 180° − (18° + 141°) = 21°, so 13 is opposite 21°.
x = (13 sin 141°) / sin 21° ≈ 8.181 / 0.35837 ≈ 22.8 → choice D.
A survey triangle has a 106° angle opposite the 146.9-ft side and a 24° angle opposite the unknown side x. Find x to the nearest tenth.
1sin 106° / 146.9 = sin 24° / xLaw of Sines
2x sin 106° = 146.9 sin 24°Multiplication Property of Equality

In △MNP, m∠N = 32°, n = 7, and p = 4. Determine whether △MNP has no solution, one solution, or two solutions. Then solve the triangle.
1∠N acute and n > pone solution exists
2sin 32° / 7 = sin P / 4Law of Sines
3(4 sin 32°) / 7 = sin P ≈ 0.30281Multiply each side by 4
4m∠P = sin⁻¹(0.30281) ≈ 17.627°Use the sin⁻¹ function
5m∠M = 180° − (32° + 17.627°) ≈ 130.373°Triangle Angle-Sum Theorem
6m = (7 sin 130.373°) / sin 32° ≈ 5.333 / 0.52992Law of Sines, solve for m
m∠P ≈ 18°,   m∠M ≈ 130°,   m ≈ 10.1
Why is the obtuse partner rejected? The obtuse twin of 17.627° is 162.373°, and 32° + 162.373° = 194.373° > 180°. Impossible — so only one triangle survives.
In △RST, m∠R = 95°, r = 10, and s = 12. Determine whether △RST has no solution, one solution, or two solutions.
1∠R is obtuse, so ∠R is the largest angleonly one angle can be ≥ 90°
2the largest angle must face the longest sideAngle-Side Relationships
3but r = 10 < 12 = scontradiction
Because ∠R is obtuse and 10 < 12, there is no solution.
Algebraic check: sin S = (12 sin 95°)/10 ≈ 1.1954. A sine can never exceed 1, so no such angle S exists ✓ (See the figure above: the arc of radius 10 never reaches ray RS.)
Think About It! If ∠R were acute and r < s, you could no longer answer instantly — you would compute h = s sin R and compare it with r to get 0, 1, or 2 triangles.
In △ABC, m∠A = 32°, a = 15, and b = 18. Determine whether △ABC has no solution, one solution, or two solutions. Then solve the triangle.
1h = b sin A = 18 sin 32°b = 18 and m∠A = 32°
2h ≈ 9.5Use a calculator
39.5 < 15 < 18, so h < a < bthere are TWO solutions

Case 1 — ∠B is acute

sin B / 18 = sin 32° / 15
sin B = (18 sin 32°) / 15
sin B ≈ 0.6359
m∠B ≈ 39.487° → 39°

m∠C ≈ 180° − (32° + 39°) = 109°

sin C / c = sin 32° / 15
c = (15 sin 109°) / sin 32°
c ≈ 26.8

Case 2 — ∠B is obtuse

Find an obtuse B with sin B ≈ 0.6359.
m∠B ≈ 180° − 39° = 141°
(Postulate 9.1)

m∠C ≈ 180° − (32° + 141°) = 7°

sin C / c = sin 32° / 15
c = (15 sin 7°) / sin 32°
c ≈ 3.4
One solution: m∠B ≈ 39°, m∠C ≈ 109°, c ≈ 26.8
Another solution: m∠B ≈ 141°, m∠C ≈ 7°, c ≈ 3.4
Watch Out! The calculator's sin−1 returns only 39°. It will never tell you about 141°. Whenever you are in the SSA case and h < a < b, you must add the second case by hand.

Common mistakes
  • Pairing a side with the wrong angle — always use the angle opposite the side.
  • Forgetting the obtuse case when h < a < b. sin−1 hides it.
  • Keeping the obtuse case when the three angles would exceed 180°.
  • Calculator in radian mode instead of degree mode.
  • Rounding an intermediate angle and then propagating the error into the next step.
  • Trying to start an SSS or SAS problem with the Law of Sines.
  • Accepting a result with sin B > 1 — that is the algebraic signal of "no triangle".
