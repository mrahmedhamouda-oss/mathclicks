Core Concepts

1. What a trapezoid is

A trapezoid is a four-sided figure in which exactly one pair of opposite sides never meet — they run in the same direction forever. That one pair does all the work: it creates the bases, the legs, and the base angles.

Formal definition. A trapezoid is a quadrilateral with exactly one pair of parallel sides.

Bases — the two parallel sides of the trapezoid.
Legs — the two nonparallel sides.
Base angle — an angle formed by a base and one of the legs. A trapezoid has two pairs of base angles, one pair at each base.
Isosceles trapezoid — a trapezoid whose legs are congruent.
Study Tip: the word exactly matters. A parallelogram has two pairs of parallel sides, so a parallelogram is not a trapezoid under this definition.

2. Theorems about isosceles trapezoids

Congruent legs force a kind of mirror symmetry across the trapezoid. Everything below is a consequence of that symmetry.

Theorem 7.21
If a trapezoid is isosceles, then each pair of base angles is congruent.
If trapezoid FGHJ is isosceles with bases GH and FJ, then ∠G ≅ ∠H and ∠F ≅ ∠J.
Theorem 7.22 (converse)
If a trapezoid has one pair of congruent base angles, then it is an isosceles trapezoid.
If ∠L ≅ ∠M, then trapezoid KLMP is isosceles.
Theorem 7.23
A trapezoid is isosceles if and only if its diagonals are congruent.
If trapezoid QRST is isosceles, then QS ≅ RT. Likewise, if QS ≅ RT, then QRST is isosceles.
Watch Out! Base angles are congruent within a pair, not across pairs. In the figure above, ∠G ≅ ∠H and ∠F ≅ ∠J, but ∠G is supplementary to ∠F, because a leg is a transversal cutting the two parallel bases.

3. The midsegment of a trapezoid

Cut both legs exactly in half and join those two midpoints. The segment you get lies halfway between the bases — so its length is halfway between the two base lengths.

The midsegment of a trapezoid (also called the median) is the segment that connects the midpoints of the legs of the trapezoid.

Theorem 7.24 — Trapezoid Midsegment Theorem
The midsegment of a trapezoid is parallel to each base and its length is one half the sum of the lengths of the bases.
If UR is the midsegment of trapezoid PQST with bases PQ and TS:
UR = ½(PQ + TS)   ↔   2·UR = PQ + TS
!
Because 2·UR = PQ + TS, you can find a missing base just as easily as a missing midsegment: PQ = 2·UR − TS.
Study Tip: the midsegment endpoints are midpoints, so on the coordinate plane you find them with the Midpoint Formula: M = ((x1+x2)/2, (y1+y2)/2) applied to each leg.

4. What a kite is

A kite is what you get when you glue two isosceles triangles together along a common base: two short sides next to each other, then two long sides next to each other.

A kite is a convex quadrilateral with exactly two distinct pairs of adjacent congruent sides. Unlike a parallelogram, the opposite sides of a kite are neither congruent nor parallel.

Theorem 7.25
If a quadrilateral is a kite, then its diagonals are perpendicular.
Theorem 7.26
If a quadrilateral is a kite, then exactly one pair of opposite angles is congruent.
✓
Perpendicular diagonals split a kite into four right triangles. That is why every kite length problem turns into the Pythagorean Theorem: a2 + b2 = c2.
Watch Out! The congruent pair of opposite angles is the pair between the short side and the long side (the two vertices where a short side meets a long side). The two vertices where two short sides meet, or two long sides meet, are not congruent to each other.

5. Proving a figure is a trapezoid on the coordinate plane

Find the slope of all four sides with m = (y2 − y1)/(x2 − x1).
Show that exactly one pair of opposite sides has equal slopes → that pair is parallel, so the figure is a trapezoid.
Find the lengths of the two legs with d = √((x2−x1)2 + (y2−y1)2).
Equal legs → isosceles trapezoid. Unequal legs → trapezoid that is not isosceles.

ABCD is an isosceles trapezoid with bases AB and CD. AB = 3x − 2, CD = 3x + 9, AD = 4x + 5, BC = 5x − 6.
(A) Prove x = 11.  (B) Find m∠A if m∠C = 72°.  (C) Find the perimeter.

Part A — Prove x = 11

2AD = BCdefinition of congruent segments

45 = x − 6subtract 4x from each side
511 = x  ∴  x = 11add 6; symmetric property

Part B — Find m∠A when m∠C = 72°

1m∠D = m∠C = 72°Thm 7.21: base angles ≅
2m∠A + m∠D = 180°Consecutive Interior Angles (AB ∥ CD)
3m∠A + 72 = 180substitution
4m∠A = 108°solve

Part C — Perimeter

1P = AB + BC + CD + ADperimeter of ABCD

4P = 15(11) + 6 = 165 + 6x = 11

Check: with x = 11, AD = 4(11)+5 = 49 and BC = 5(11)−6 = 49 ✓ equal legs. AB = 31, CD = 42, so P = 31 + 49 + 42 + 49 = 171 ✓
Quadrilateral QRST has vertices Q(−8, −4), R(0, 8), S(6, 8), T(−6, −10). Show that QRST is a trapezoid and determine whether it is isosceles.

Slopes of QR and ST

QR = (8 − (−4))/(0 − (−8)) = 12/8 = 3/2
ST = (−10 − 8)/(−6 − 6) = −18/−12 = 3/2
Equal → QR ∥ ST

Slopes of QT and RS

QT = (−10 − (−4))/(−6 − (−8)) = −6/2 = −3
RS = (8 − 8)/(6 − 0) = 0/6 = 0
Not equal → QT is not ∥ RS
1Exactly one pair of opposite sides is parallel∴ QRST is a trapezoid
2QT = √((−6−(−8))2 + (−10−(−4))2) = √(4 + 36) = √40Distance Formula on leg QT

Common mistakes to avoid
  • Calling a parallelogram a trapezoid. A trapezoid has exactly one pair of parallel sides.
  • Setting the two different base angles equal. Angles at opposite ends of a leg are supplementary, not congruent.
  • Adding instead of averaging: the midsegment is ½(b₁+b₂), not b₁+b₂.
  • Forgetting to double the midsegment when solving for a base.
  • Assuming both kite diagonals are bisected. Only the diagonal joining the two "short-side" vertices... in fact only one diagonal is bisected, the one connecting the vertices between unequal sides.
  • Claiming all four angles of a kite come in congruent pairs. Exactly one pair is congruent (Theorem 7.26).
  • Using whole diagonals in the Pythagorean Theorem instead of half-diagonals.
