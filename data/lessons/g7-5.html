Core Concepts

Today's Vocabulary

Two special parallelograms sit at the bottom of the quadrilateral family tree. Everything you already know about parallelograms is still true — these shapes just add extra guarantees.

rhombus (plural rhombi) — a parallelogram with all four sides congruent.
square — a parallelogram with all four sides congruent and all four angles congruent.
Study Tip: A rhombus is a "pushed-over square." It keeps the equal sides but gives up the right angles. A square is a rhombus that also happens to have four right angles — so a square is always a rhombus, but a rhombus is only sometimes a square.

Properties of a rhombus

A rhombus is a parallelogram, so it inherits all parallelogram properties: opposite sides parallel and congruent, opposite angles congruent, consecutive angles supplementary, and diagonals that bisect each other. On top of that, it gets two brand-new theorems.

Theorem 7.15 — Diagonals of a Rhombus
If a parallelogram is a rhombus, then its diagonals are perpendicular.
In rhombus ABCD with diagonals meeting at E: AC ⊥ BD, so m∠AEB = 90°.
Theorem 7.16 — Diagonals of a Rhombus
If a parallelogram is a rhombus, then each diagonal bisects a pair of opposite angles.
In rhombus ABCD: AC bisects ∠DAB and ∠DCB; BD bisects ∠ABC and ∠ADC.

Why Theorem 7.15 works: the diagonals already bisect each other (parallelogram), so the four small triangles share two pairs of congruent sides; because all four sides of the rhombus are congruent too, the triangles are congruent by SSS. The four angles at the centre are congruent and add to 360°, so each one is 360 ÷ 4 = 90°.

Properties of a square

A square is a parallelogram, a rectangle, and a rhombus all at once, so every property of each of those shapes applies to it.

In square ABCD with diagonals meeting at E:
  AB ≅ BC ≅ CD ≅ DA   (rhombus)
  m∠A = m∠B = m∠C = m∠D = 90°   (rectangle)
  AC ≅ BD   (rectangle: diagonals congruent)
  AC ⊥ BD   (rhombus: diagonals perpendicular)
  AE = EC = BE = ED = ½AC   (diagonals bisect each other)

Because the diagonals of a square are congruent and bisect each other, all four half-diagonals are equal. That single fact solves most square problems in one line.

Tests: how to prove a parallelogram is a rhombus or a square

The theorems above go one way (rhombus → property). These four theorems go the other way (property → rhombus).

Theorem 7.17
If the diagonals of a parallelogram are perpendicular, then the parallelogram is a rhombus.
Theorem 7.18
If one diagonal of a parallelogram bisects a pair of opposite angles, then the parallelogram is a rhombus.
Theorem 7.19
If two consecutive sides of a parallelogram are congruent, then the parallelogram is a rhombus.
Theorem 7.20
If a quadrilateral is both a rectangle and a rhombus, then it is a square.
Watch Out! — Common Misconception: Theorems 7.17–7.19 only apply if you already know the quadrilateral is a parallelogram. A kite also has perpendicular diagonals but is not a rhombus. Always establish "parallelogram" first, then apply the test.
1
Only two consecutive sides need to be congruent to force a rhombus (Thm 7.19) — the parallelogram properties supply the other two.
2
Diagonals ⊥ → rhombus. Diagonals ≅ → rectangle. Diagonals ⊥ and ≅ → square.
3
The diagonals cut a rhombus into four congruent right triangles, so s² = (d1/2)² + (d2/2)².

Coordinate-geometry test

Given four vertices, use slopes and distances:

Show it is a parallelogram: opposite sides have equal slopes (or the diagonals share a midpoint).
Slopes of the two diagonals: if the product is −1, the diagonals are perpendicular → rhombus.
Slopes of two consecutive sides: if the product is −1, the sides are perpendicular → rectangle.
If both step 2 and step 3 hold, it is a square (Theorem 7.20).
Study Tip — Make a Graph: plot the points first with the same scale on both axes. The picture tells you what to expect and catches slope-arithmetic slips.

Common mistakes to avoid
  • Assuming the diagonals of a rhombus are congruent. They are perpendicular, not equal — unless the rhombus happens to be a square.
  • Confusing a half-diagonal with a side. In a square with diagonal d, the half-diagonal is d/2 but the side is d/√2.
  • Applying Theorems 7.17–7.19 before establishing that the figure is a parallelogram.
  • Using the full vertex angle where the diagonal has already halved it (Example 2: use 37.5°, not 75°).
  • Stopping at "rhombus" on a coordinate problem — always check the side slopes too, in case it is really a square.
  • Writing "a square is a rhombus" as false. It is true; a rhombus is only sometimes a square.
