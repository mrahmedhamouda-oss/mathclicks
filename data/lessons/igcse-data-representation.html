Three graphs carry almost all the marks in this topic: the scatter diagram, the histogram and the cumulative frequency curve. Each has one idea that examiners test again and again — correlation is not cause, frequency is area, and the curve tells you "how many are less than or equal to". Drive all three below.

◎ What you will be able to do

  • Describe correlation with strength, direction and meaning in context.
  • Draw and use a line of best fit, and know when an estimate is unreliable.
  • Build a histogram using frequency density when class widths are unequal.
  • Read a frequency — and part of a class — back off a histogram.
  • Draw a cumulative frequency curve against the upper class boundary.
  • Estimate the median, the quartiles, the IQR and any percentile from the curve.
  • Turn those five numbers into a box-and-whisker diagram.

1 Scatter diagrams and correlation Interactive

A scatter diagram plots two variables against each other for the same individuals, to see whether they are related.

CorrelationWhat the points doHow to describe it
Strong positiveClose to a line sloping up"As $x$ increases, $y$ increases"
Weak positiveLoosely sloping upSame, but the link is less clear
Strong negativeClose to a line sloping down"As $x$ increases, $y$ decreases"
No correlationNo pattern at all"There is no relationship between $x$ and $y$"
✎
A full-mark description gives strength and direction and says what it means in context: "Strong positive correlation — students who revised for longer tended to score higher marks."
The line of best fit
  • One straight line, drawn with a ruler, following the trend of the points.
  • Roughly the same number of points above it as below it.
  • It does not have to pass through the origin, but it should pass through the mean point.
  • Never join the points dot to dot, and never draw a curve.
TermMeaningReliability
InterpolationEstimating inside the range of the dataReliable
ExtrapolationEstimating outside the range of the dataUnreliable — the pattern may not continue
Lab 1 — scatter, correlation and the line

Pick a dataset and slide the reading line. The lab describes the correlation, draws the line of best fit through the mean point, and warns you the moment your estimate becomes extrapolation.

!
Correlation is not cause. Ice cream sales and sunburn cases correlate strongly, but ice cream does not cause sunburn — hot weather causes both. If a question asks whether one thing causes the other, the answer is almost always "not necessarily".

2 Histograms Interactive

In a histogram it is the area of a bar, not its height, that represents the frequency. So when class widths are unequal, the height must be the frequency density.

frequency density $=\dfrac{\text{frequency}}{\text{class width}}$  ·  frequency $=$ frequency density $\times$ class width
✎
Three rules for drawing one. Bars touch (the data is continuous) · the vertical axis is labelled frequency density · each bar spans exactly its class, from lower bound to upper bound.
Lab 2 — the histogram builder

Change any frequency and watch the densities and the bar heights follow. The last box estimates how many values lie in part of a class, by assuming they are spread evenly across it.

!
The classic error is plotting frequency instead of frequency density. With unequal widths that makes the wide classes look far more common than they are — and scores zero. Note too that the modal class is the one with the greatest frequency density, which need not be the one with the largest frequency.

Cumulative frequency is a running total: how many values are less than or equal to each upper class boundary.

From the curve to a box plot

The five numbers a box-and-whisker diagram needs come straight off the curve: the lowest value, $Q_1$, the median, $Q_3$ and the highest value. Median left of centre with a long right tail means positive skew; median right of centre with a long left tail means negative skew.

✍️ Worked examples

Example 1
Describing correlation
 ⌄

A scatter diagram plots the age of a car against its value. The points slope downwards in a tight band. Describe the correlation and explain what it means.

1

Direction: the points slope downwards, so the correlation is negative.

2

Strength: the points lie in a tight band, so it is strong.

3

Context: say what it means for cars, not for "$x$ and $y$".

Answer. Strong negative correlation — as the age of a car increases, its value decreases.
!
"Negative correlation" alone often scores only one of the two marks. The interpretation in context is usually worth the second.
Example 2
Using a line of best fit
 ⌄

A scatter diagram shows hours of revision against test score for ten students, for revision times from $1$ to $10$ hours. Estimate the score of a student who revised for $6$ hours, and comment on estimating the score for $20$ hours.

1

Draw a vertical line up from $h=6$ to the line of best fit.

2

Draw a horizontal line across to the vertical axis and read the value — about $62$.

3

$20$ hours is far outside the data, which only reaches $10$ hours.

Answer. About $62\%$. The estimate for $20$ hours would be extrapolation and is unreliable — the relationship may not continue, and a score cannot exceed $100\%$ anyway.
✎

Example 4
Reading a frequency back off a histogram
 ⌄

A bar covers $40

1

Frequency is the area: $0.6\times(70-40)=0.6\times30=18$.

2

Part of a class: assume the values are spread evenly, so use the same height over the narrower width: $0.6\times10=6$.

Answer. Frequency $18$; about $6$ values lie between $40$ and $50$
!
Reading the height $0.6$ as the frequency is the most common error in the topic — the frequency is the area.
Example 5
Median and quartiles from a curve
 ⌄

A cumulative frequency curve shows the marks of $100$ students. Estimate the median, the quartiles and the interquartile range.

1

Median: read across from $100\div2=50$ → about $36$.

2

Lower quartile: read across from $100\div4=25$ → about $26$.

3

Upper quartile: read across from $3\times100\div4=75$ → about $47$.

4

IQR: $47-26=21$.

Answer. Median $\approx36$ · $Q_1\approx26$ · $Q_3\approx47$ · IQR $\approx21$
✎
Different from a list. With raw data the median is at position $\frac{n+1}{2}$; on a curve you read across from $\frac{n}{2}$, because the curve is a continuous model. Lab 3 holds exactly this data.
Example 6
A percentile, and reading backwards
 ⌄

Using the same curve, estimate the 90th percentile and find how many students scored more than $45$ marks.

1

90th percentile: read across from $\frac{90}{100}\times100=90$ → about $57$ marks, so $90\%$ scored $57$ or less.

2

More than 45: read up from $45$ to the curve, then across → about $71$ students scored $45$ or less.

3

Subtract from the total: $100-71=29$.

Answer. 90th percentile $\approx57$ marks · about $29$ students scored more than $45$
!
The curve always answers "how many are less than or equal to". For "more than", subtract from the total.
Example 7
Building a box plot from a curve
 ⌄

The $100$ marks range from $0$ to $70$, with $Q_1\approx26$, median $\approx36$ and $Q_3\approx47$. Describe the box-and-whisker diagram and comment on the shape.

1

The five values are the minimum, $Q_1$, the median, $Q_3$ and the maximum: $0,\ 26,\ 36,\ 47,\ 70$.

2

Draw the box from $Q_1$ to $Q_3$ with a line at the median, then whiskers out to the extremes.

3

The median sits slightly left of the centre of the box ($10$ above $Q_1$, $11$ below $Q_3$), so the middle half is close to symmetrical.

Answer. Box from $26$ to $47$ with the median at $36$, whiskers to $0$ and $70$ — roughly symmetrical through the middle half, with marks spread over the full range.

! Common mistakes to avoid

  • Describing correlation without context. Strength, direction and what it means.
  • Claiming cause from correlation. A third factor may be driving both variables.
  • Extrapolating without saying so. Outside the data, the estimate is unreliable — say it.
  • Plotting frequency instead of frequency density when the class widths differ.
  • Reading a bar's height as the frequency. Frequency is height × width, i.e. the area.
  • Plotting cumulative frequency against the midpoint. It goes against the upper boundary.
  • Using $\frac{n+1}{2}$ on a curve. Read across from $\frac{n}{2}$; the $+1$ belongs to raw lists.
  • Forgetting to subtract when a question asks "how many scored more than…".
