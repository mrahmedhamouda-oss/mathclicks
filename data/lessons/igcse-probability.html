Probability questions are won on two words: and means multiply, or means add. Everything else is bookkeeping — listing outcomes in a grid, tracking a tree stage by stage, or noticing that "given that" has shrunk the denominator. Build all three below with your own numbers.

◎ What you will be able to do

  • Calculate the probability of a single event, and use $P(\text{not }A)=1-P(A)$.
  • Work out an expected frequency, and estimate a probability from experiment.
  • List every outcome of two experiments in a sample space diagram.
  • Tell independent events from mutually exclusive ones, and know which rule each needs.
  • Build tree diagrams with and without replacement.
  • Use the complement for "at least one" questions.
  • Find a conditional probability from a Venn diagram or a two-way table.

1 Simple probability

$P(\text{event})=\dfrac{\text{number of favourable outcomes}}{\text{total number of equally likely outcomes}}$

Every probability lies between $0$ (impossible) and $1$ (certain).

✎
Two rules that solve half the questions. The probabilities of a complete set of outcomes add up to $1$, and therefore $P(\text{not }A)=1-P(A)$.
Expected frequency
expected frequency $=P(\text{event})\times n$

A game is won with probability $0.15$. In $400$ games you would expect to win $0.15\times400=60$ times.

Relative frequency

When the outcomes are not equally likely — a biased dice, a drawing pin — you estimate the probability from experiment.

relative frequency $=\dfrac{\text{number of successes}}{\text{number of trials}}$

A spinner landing on red $42$ times in $120$ spins gives an estimate of $\frac{42}{120}=0.35$. More trials give a more reliable estimate.

!
Write a probability as a fraction, decimal or percentage. A ratio like $3:5$, or "3 out of 5 chance" in words, scores zero.

2 Sample space diagrams Interactive

A sample space diagram lists every possible outcome of a combined experiment, so you can simply count. Two fair dice give $6\times6=36$ equally likely outcomes.

!
Order matters. $(2,5)$ and $(5,2)$ are two different outcomes. Counting them as one is the classic error — and it is exactly why a total of $7$ is far more likely than a total of $2$.
Lab 1 — the sample space grid

Set the two dice or spinners, choose how they combine, and pick an event. Every cell matching the event lights up, and the count becomes the probability.

✎
When to use one. A grid works for exactly two experiments with a manageable number of outcomes. For three or more stages, or for picks without replacement, use a tree.

IndependentOne event does not change the probability of the other$P(A\cap B)=P(A)\times P(B)$

✎
Worked through. If $P(A)=0.4$, $P(B)=0.7$ and they are independent: $P(A\cap B)=0.28$, so $P(A\cup B)=0.4+0.7-0.28=0.82$ and $P(\text{neither})=1-0.82=0.18$.
!
They are not the same thing. Mutually exclusive events with non-zero probabilities can never be independent — if one happens, the other definitely does not.

4 Tree diagrams Interactive

  • Each set of branches from a point is one stage, and those probabilities add to $1$.
  • Multiply along the branches for the probability of a complete path.
  • Add down the paths that satisfy the event.
  • All the final path probabilities add to $1$ — the fastest check there is.
Lab 2 — the tree diagram

Change what is in the bag and switch replacement on and off. Watch the second stage: with replacement it repeats the first, without replacement both the numerator and the denominator change.

✎
"At least one" — always use the complement. Instead of adding three paths, work out $P(\text{none})$ and subtract from $1$. It is faster and far less error-prone, and it is the only sensible approach with three or more stages.
!
Without replacement the denominator drops by $1$ and the numerator drops for whichever colour was taken. Forgetting the numerator is the commonest slip in the topic.

5 Venn diagrams and conditional probability Interactive

A Venn diagram of frequencies turns a probability question into simple counting. $P(A\mid B)$ is read "the probability of $A$ given that $B$ has happened" — and the key idea is that $B$ becomes the new total.

$P(A\mid B)=\dfrac{n(A\cap B)}{n(B)}=\dfrac{P(A\cap B)}{P(B)}$
Lab 3 — conditional probability

Fill in the four regions and compare the straight probabilities with the conditional ones. Notice that $P(A\mid B)$ and $P(B\mid A)$ share a numerator but not a denominator.

✎
Spot the wording. "Given that…", "A student who studies Physics is chosen…", "…is known to be…" all signal a conditional probability, and the denominator is no longer the grand total.

✍️ Worked examples

Example 1
Simple probability and the complement
 ⌄

A bag contains $4$ red, $5$ blue and $3$ green counters. One is taken at random. Find $P(\text{red})$, $P(\text{not green})$ and $P(\text{red or blue})$.

1

Total: $4+5+3=12$ counters.

2

$P(\text{red})=\frac{4}{12}=\frac13$.

3

Use the complement: $P(\text{not green})=1-\frac{3}{12}=\frac{9}{12}=\frac34$.

4

Red and blue are mutually exclusive, so add: $\frac{4}{12}+\frac{5}{12}=\frac34$.

Answer. $\frac13$, $\frac34$, $\frac34$
✎
"Not green" and "red or blue" describe the same counters, which is why they give the same probability.
Example 2
Using a sample space diagram
 ⌄

Two fair dice are thrown and the totals recorded. Find $P(\text{total}=7)$ and $P(\text{total}>9)$.

1

There are $6\times6=36$ equally likely outcomes.

2

Total $7$: $(1,6),(2,5),(3,4),(4,3),(5,2),(6,1)$ — six of them.

3

Total more than $9$: $(4,6),(5,5),(5,6),(6,4),(6,5),(6,6)$ — also six.

Answer. Both are $\frac{6}{36}=\frac16$
!
$(4,6)$ and $(6,4)$ are separate outcomes. Counting each unordered pair once gives the wrong denominator and the wrong answer.
Example 3
Independent events
 ⌄

$P(A)=0.4$ and $P(B)=0.7$, and $A$ and $B$ are independent. Find $P(A\cap B)$, $P(A\cup B)$ and $P(\text{neither})$.

1

Both: independent means multiply — $0.4\times0.7=0.28$.

2

At least one: $0.4+0.7-0.28=0.82$.

3

Neither: the complement of "at least one" — $1-0.82=0.18$.

Answer. $0.28$, $0.82$, $0.18$
✎
Check another way. Neither means "not $A$ and not $B$" $=0.6\times0.3=0.18$ ✓
Example 4
Tree diagram with replacement
 ⌄

A bag holds $5$ red and $3$ blue counters. One is taken, replaced, and a second taken. Find $P(\text{both red})$ and $P(\text{exactly one red})$.

1

The counter is replaced, so the second stage has the same probabilities as the first.

2

Both red: multiply along the top path — $\frac58\times\frac58=\frac{25}{64}$.

3

Exactly one red: two paths, R then B and B then R — $\frac{15}{64}+\frac{15}{64}=\frac{30}{64}=\frac{15}{32}$.

Answer. $P(\text{both red})=\frac{25}{64}$ · $P(\text{exactly one red})=\frac{15}{32}$
!
"Exactly one red" needs both orders. Giving only $\frac{15}{64}$ is the standard error.
Example 5
Tree diagram without replacement
 ⌄

The same bag, but the first counter is not replaced. Find $P(\text{both blue})$ and $P(\text{at least one red})$.

1

After the first counter is removed only $7$ remain.

2

Both blue: $\frac38\times\frac27=\frac{6}{56}=\frac{3}{28}$ — the blue count falls from $3$ to $2$ as well as the total from $8$ to $7$.

3

At least one red is the opposite of "no reds", which here is exactly "both blue": $1-\frac{6}{56}=\frac{50}{56}=\frac{25}{28}$.

Answer. $P(\text{both blue})=\frac{3}{28}$ · $P(\text{at least one red})=\frac{25}{28}$
✎
Check. The four paths give $\frac{20}{56}+\frac{15}{56}+\frac{15}{56}+\frac{6}{56}=1$ ✓ — Lab 2 does this check for you.
Example 6
Conditional probability from a Venn diagram
 ⌄

Of $40$ students, $25$ study Maths, $18$ study Physics and $10$ study both. A student who studies Physics is chosen at random. Find the probability that they also study Maths.

1

Fill the diagram. Both is $10$, so Maths only is $25-10=15$ and Physics only is $18-10=8$. Neither is $40-33=7$.

2

The new total. The student is known to study Physics, so the denominator is $n(P)=18$, not $40$.

3

Count the favourable ones. Of those $18$, exactly $10$ also study Maths.

Answer. $P(M\mid P)=\frac{10}{18}=\frac59$
!
Answering $\frac{10}{40}$ ignores the condition. The whole point of "given that" is that the total shrinks.
Example 7
Three stages and "at least one"
 ⌄

A biased coin has $P(\text{head})=0.6$. It is tossed three times. Find $P(\text{three heads})$ and $P(\text{at least one head})$.

1

Each toss is independent, so multiply.

2

Three heads: $0.6\times0.6\times0.6=0.216$.

3

At least one head via the complement: $P(\text{no heads})=0.4^{3}=0.064$, so $1-0.064=0.936$.

Answer. $0.216$ and $0.936$
✎
Adding the seven paths that contain at least one head gives the same answer and takes far longer. With three or more stages, always look for the complement first.

! Common mistakes to avoid

  • Writing a probability as a ratio such as $1:5$, or in words.
  • Adding when you should multiply. AND multiplies along a tree; OR adds completed paths.
  • Forgetting the second order for "one of each" or "exactly one" — there are always two paths.
  • Not changing the numerator on the second stage of a without-replacement tree.
  • Treating $(2,5)$ and $(5,2)$ as one outcome in a sample space diagram.
  • Using the grand total for a conditional probability. "Given that" shrinks the denominator.
  • Confusing $P(A\mid B)$ with $P(B\mid A)$. Different questions, different answers.
  • Confusing independent with mutually exclusive. One multiplies; the other has intersection $0$.
  • Doing "at least one" the long way instead of $1-P(\text{none})$.
  • Rounding partway through a tree. Keep the fractions exact until the last line.
