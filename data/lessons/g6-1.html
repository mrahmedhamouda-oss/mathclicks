Core Concepts

1. What a perpendicular bisector is

A perpendicular bisector is a line that does two jobs at once: it cuts a segment exactly in half, and it crosses that segment at a right angle. Miss either job and it is not a perpendicular bisector.

perpendicular bisector — a line, segment, or ray that passes through the midpoint of a segment and is perpendicular to that segment.
concurrent lines — three or more lines that intersect at one common point.
point of concurrency — the single point where concurrent lines meet.
circumcenter — the point of concurrency of the three perpendicular bisectors of the sides of a triangle.
If line ℓ is the ⊥ bisector of AB meeting it at M, then
  AM = MB   and   ℓ ⊥ AB  (m∠AMℓ = 90°)
Study Tip — Circum– The prefix circum– means "about" or "around." The circumcenter is the center of a circle drawn around a triangle so that the circle contains all three vertices.

2. The two theorems that do all the work

These two statements are converses of each other. One turns "on the bisector" into "equal distances." The other turns "equal distances" into "on the bisector." Almost every problem in this lesson is one of these two moves.

Theorem 6.1 — Perpendicular Bisector Theorem
Words: If a point is on the perpendicular bisector of a segment, then it is equidistant from the endpoints of the segment.
Symbols: If CD is a ⊥ bisector of AB, then AC = BC.
Theorem 6.2 — Converse of the Perpendicular Bisector Theorem
Words: If a point is equidistant from the endpoints of a segment, then it is on the perpendicular bisector of the segment.
Symbols: If AC = BC, then C lies on the ⊥ bisector of AB.

Why Theorem 6.1 works: drop the point C onto the midpoint D. Then AD ≅ DB (midpoint), ∠ADC and ∠BDC are both right angles, and CD ≅ CD. By SAS, △ADC ≅ △BDC, so AC ≅ BC by CPCTC.

Watch Out! One equal-length mark is not enough. To use the converse and conclude that a whole line is the perpendicular bisector, you need two different points that are each equidistant from the endpoints. Two such points determine the bisector line.

3. Perpendicular bisectors of a triangle

A triangle has three sides, so it has three perpendicular bisectors. Remarkably, they always meet at one point — they are concurrent. That point of concurrency is the circumcenter.

Theorem 6.3 — Circumcenter Theorem
The perpendicular bisectors of a triangle intersect at a point called the circumcenter that is equidistant from the vertices of the triangle.

So if P is the circumcenter of △ABC, then PA = PB = PC. That common distance is the radius of the circumcircle — the unique circle passing through all three vertices.

Watch Out! The circumcenter is equidistant from the vertices, not from the sides. (The point equidistant from the three sides is the incenter, from angle bisectors — a different lesson.)

4. The right-triangle trick you will use constantly

Draw a perpendicular bisector from the circumcenter P to a side. It hits the side at its midpoint and makes a right angle. That creates a right triangle whose legs are the distance P-to-side and half the side, with hypotenuse a radius.

(half a side)2 + (distance from center to that side)2 = (radius)2
Use the perpendicular bisector to halve the known side.
Apply the Pythagorean Theorem to get the radius r (a distance from the center to a vertex).
By the Circumcenter Theorem, that same r reaches every vertex.
Apply the Pythagorean Theorem again on a second side to find the missing half-length.
1
Midpoint + right angle. Both conditions, always. A line through the midpoint that is not perpendicular is only a bisector; a perpendicular line that misses the midpoint is only a perpendicular.
2
On the bisector ⇒ equidistant. Every point of the ⊥ bisector of AB is the same distance from A as from B.
3
Equidistant ⇒ on the bisector. The converse holds too, so the ⊥ bisector is the set of all points equidistant from the two endpoints.
4
Circumcenter: where the three ⊥ bisectors of a triangle meet; PA = PB = PC.
5
Setting up equations: two expressions for distances to the two endpoints are equal. Set them equal, solve, then substitute back — the question almost never asks for the variable itself.

Worked Examples

Line FG is the perpendicular bisector of EH. If EF = 4a − 15 and HF = a + 12, find EF.
1EF = HFPerpendicular Bisector Theorem
24a − 15 = a + 12Substitution
33a − 15 = 12Subtract a from each side
43a = 27  →  a = 9Add 15, then divide by 3
5EF = 4(9) − 15 = 36 − 15Substitute a = 9
EF = 21
Check: HF = 9 + 12 = 21, and 21 = 21 ✓. The two distances match, exactly as the theorem promises.
Watch Out! The question asks for EF, not a. Stopping at a = 9 is the most common lost mark on this type.
WX = WZ = 25.3, line WY ⊥ XZ, and ZY = 22.4. Find XY.
1WX = WZ (both 25.3)Given
2W is equidistant from X and ZDefinition of equidistant
3line WY is the ⊥ bisector of XZConverse of the ⊥ Bisector Theorem, with WY ⊥ XZ
4XY = YZDefinition of segment bisector
5XY = 22.4Substitution (ZY = 22.4)
XY = 22.4
Think about it: the 25.3 values never enter the arithmetic. Their only job is to prove that W is equidistant from the endpoints, which is what licenses the converse.

Find the point equidistant from P(−8, 2), Q(0, 12), and R(8, 2) — that is, the circumcenter of △PQR.
1P(−8, 2) and R(8, 2) share y = 2PR is horizontal; its midpoint is (0, 2)
2⊥ bisector of PR is the vertical line x = 0Perpendicular to a horizontal segment
3Center is (0, y); set (0,y)P = (0,y)QCircumcenter is equidistant from vertices
48² + (y − 2)² = (y − 12)²Distance formula, squared
564 + y² − 4y + 4 = y² − 24y + 144Expand
668 − 4y = −24y + 144  →  20y = 76Collect like terms
Circumcenter = (0, 3.8)
Check: distance to P = √(82 + 1.82) = √67.24 = 8.2; distance to Q = |12 − 3.8| = 8.2; distance to R = 8.2 ✓
Shortcut: if two vertices share an x-value, the ⊥ bisector of that side is a horizontal line y = average of the two y-values. If two vertices share a y-value, the ⊥ bisector is the vertical line x = average of the two x-values. Use those two easy lines first.

Practice 2

Line AD is the perpendicular bisector of BC, with BD = 3y − 4 and DC = y + 10. Find BC.
1BD = DCD is the midpoint of BC

Trap: the question asks for the whole segment BC, not the half BD. Double it.

Practice 4

D is the circumcenter of △ABC. M is the midpoint of AB, AB = 16, and DM = 6. Find DA and DC.
1AM = ½(16) = 8M is the midpoint
2∠DMA = 90°DM lies on the ⊥ bisector of AB

Practice 5

Find the coordinates of the circumcenter of the triangle with vertices A(−3, 1), B(5, 1), and C(5, 7).
1A and B share y = 1 → ⊥ bisector of AB is x = (−3 + 5)/2 = 1Vertical bisector of a horizontal side
2B and C share x = 5 → ⊥ bisector of BC is y = (1 + 7)/2 = 4Horizontal bisector of a vertical side
3The two bisectors meet at (1, 4)Point of concurrency
Circumcenter = (1, 4)
Check: to A: √(42+32) = 5; to B: √(42+32) = 5; to C: √(42+32) = 5 ✓ Note the triangle is right-angled at B, and the circumcenter landed on the midpoint of the hypotenuse AC — that always happens for a right triangle.

Common mistakes to avoid
  • Solving for the variable and stopping — the question usually wants a length.
  • Reporting a half-segment when the whole segment was asked for (or the reverse).
  • Claiming a line is a ⊥ bisector from a right angle alone, or from a midpoint alone. You need both.
  • Using only one equidistant point to conclude a whole line is the bisector — you need two.
  • Saying the circumcenter is equidistant from the sides. It is equidistant from the vertices.
  • Forgetting to take the positive square root only — lengths are never negative.
  • Rounding too early in a two-step Pythagorean problem; keep extra digits until the final answer.
