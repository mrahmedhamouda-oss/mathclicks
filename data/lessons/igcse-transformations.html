Four transformations, one question behind all of them: what stays the same, and what changes? Move a triangle around the grid below and watch the coordinate rule appear beside it — then use the same routine backwards to describe a transformation you are shown, which is where the marks really are.

◎ What you will be able to do

  • Reflect a shape in any of the standard mirror lines, and in $x=a$ or $y=b$.
  • Rotate a shape about the origin or about any other centre.
  • Translate a shape using a column vector.
  • Enlarge a shape by a positive, fractional or negative scale factor.
  • Find a centre of rotation and a centre of enlargement from a diagram.
  • Describe any single transformation fully — with every detail the mark scheme wants.

2 Reflection

  • Measure the perpendicular distance from a point to the mirror line.
  • Count the same distance across to the other side, at right angles to the line.
  • A point already on the mirror line does not move — those are the invariant points.

You must give the equation of the line, not a description. "Reflection in the vertical line" scores nothing; "reflection in $x=3$" scores the mark.

4 Translation

A translation is described by a single column vector: the top number is the movement in the $x$ direction, the bottom number the movement in the $y$ direction.

$\begin{pmatrix}x\text{-move}\\y\text{-move}\end{pmatrix}$  ·  right and up are positive, left and down are negative
✎
Finding the vector. Take one vertex and its image: $x$-component $=x_2-x_1$, $y$-component $=y_2-y_1$. Check with a second vertex.
!
Describe a translation with the vector only — never as a coordinate pair with a comma, and never as "5 left and 2 up" when the question asks for a full description.

5 Enlargement

An enlargement with scale factor $k$ and centre $C$ multiplies every distance from $C$ by $k$.

image distance from centre $=k\times$ object distance from centre
Scale factorWhat happens
$k>1$Larger, same side of the centre, same way up
$0Smaller — still called an enlargement — same side, same way up
$k<0$On the opposite side of the centre and turned upside down
  • To find the centre: draw a line through a vertex and its image, repeat for a second pair, and extend until they meet.
  • To find the scale factor: divide an image length by the matching object length.
✎
Areas. Under an enlargement of scale factor $k$, lengths multiply by $k$, areas by $k^{2}$ and volumes by $k^{3}$.

6 Describing a transformation fully Interactive

The identification routine
  • Is the image the same size? If not it is an enlargement — find $k$ from a pair of matching lengths, then the centre from two rays.
  • Same size, same way up, not turned? A translation — give the column vector.
  • Same size but mirrored? A reflection — find the mirror line and give its equation.
  • Same size, turned round? A rotation — give angle, direction and centre.
!
The single biggest exam trap. Name one transformation only. "Reflection and translation" scores zero even when both are true — a single transformation always exists, and that is what is being asked for.

✍️ Worked examples

Example 1
Reflecting in a diagonal line
 ⌄

Triangle A has vertices $(2,1)$, $(5,1)$ and $(2,3)$. Find the image after reflection in the line $y=x$.

1

Reflection in $y=x$ swaps the coordinates: $(x,y)\rightarrow(y,x)$.

2

Apply it to each vertex: $(2,1)\rightarrow(1,2)$, $(5,1)\rightarrow(1,5)$, $(2,3)\rightarrow(3,2)$.

Answer. $(1,2)$, $(1,5)$ and $(3,2)$
✎
Check. Each point and its image must be the same perpendicular distance from $y=x$, on opposite sides. Set Lab 1 to this reflection and compare.
Example 2
Rotating about a point that is not the origin
 ⌄

The point $P(5,2)$ is rotated $90°$ clockwise about $C(1,1)$. Find the image.

1

Shift the centre to the origin. Subtract $C$: $(5-1,\;2-1)=(4,1)$.

2

Rotate about the origin. $90°$ clockwise is $(x,y)\rightarrow(y,-x)$, so $(4,1)\rightarrow(1,-4)$.

3

Shift back. Add $C$ again: $(1+1,\;-4+1)=(2,-3)$.

Answer. $(2,-3)$
✎
Why it works. Rotating about $C$ is the same as moving $C$ to the origin, turning there, then moving everything back.
Example 3
Finding the centre of rotation
 ⌄

Triangle A has vertices $(1,1)$, $(3,1)$, $(1,2)$; its image B has vertices $(5,1)$, $(5,3)$, $(4,1)$. Describe fully the single transformation mapping A onto B.

1

Same size? Both have legs of $2$ and $1$, so it is not an enlargement.

2

Turned? The long leg is horizontal in A and vertical in B — a $90°$ turn, so a rotation.

3

First bisector. Join $(1,1)$ to $(5,1)$: midpoint $(3,1)$, segment horizontal, so the perpendicular bisector is $x=3$.

4

Second bisector. Join $(1,2)$ to $(4,1)$: midpoint $(2.5,1.5)$ and gradient $\frac{1-2}{4-1}=-\frac13$, so the perpendicular gradient is $3$ and the bisector is $y-1.5=3(x-2.5)$, i.e. $y=3x-6$.

5

Intersect. Putting $x=3$ gives $y=3$, so the centre is $(3,3)$.

6

Direction. From the centre, $(1,1)$ sits at $(-2,-2)$ and its image $(5,1)$ at $(2,-2)$. The rule $(x,y)\rightarrow(-y,x)$ does exactly that — anticlockwise.

Answer. Rotation of $90°$ anticlockwise about $(3,3)$
✎
Reproduce it in Lab 2: set the centre to $(3,3)$ with a $90°$ anticlockwise turn and watch the bisectors meet there.
Example 4
An enlargement with a fractional scale factor
 ⌄

Shape A has vertices $(2,2)$, $(8,2)$, $(2,5)$. Shape B has vertices $(2,2)$, $(4,2)$, $(2,3)$. Describe fully the transformation mapping A onto B.

1

Compare a pair of lengths. Horizontal side: $8-2=6$ in A, $4-2=2$ in B. So $k=\frac{2}{6}=\frac13$.

2

Confirm with the other side. Vertical side: $3$ in A and $1$ in B — again $\frac13$. ✓

3

Find the centre. The vertex $(2,2)$ has not moved, so it must be the centre.

Answer. Enlargement, scale factor $\frac13$, centre $(2,2)$
!
A shrinking image is still an enlargement in IGCSE. Never call it a reduction.
Example 5
A negative scale factor
 ⌄

Triangle T has vertices $(1,1)$, $(3,1)$, $(1,2)$. It is enlarged by scale factor $-2$, centre the origin. Find the image.

1

With centre the origin, an enlargement multiplies every coordinate by $k$.

2

$(1,1)\rightarrow(-2,-2)$, $(3,1)\rightarrow(-6,-2)$, $(1,2)\rightarrow(-2,-4)$.

Answer. $(-2,-2)$, $(-6,-2)$ and $(-2,-4)$
✎
Sense check. A negative scale factor puts the image on the far side of the centre and turns it upside down — which is exactly what these coordinates show.
Example 6
Areas under an enlargement
 ⌄

A shape of area $12\text{ cm}^{2}$ is enlarged by scale factor $2.5$. Find the area of the image.

1

Lengths multiply by $2.5$, so areas multiply by $2.5^{2}$.

2

$2.5^{2}=6.25$

3

$12\times6.25=75$

Answer. $75\text{ cm}^{2}$
!
Multiplying the area by $2.5$ (giving $30$) is the classic error. Areas scale by the square of the length scale factor.

! Common mistakes to avoid

  • Naming two transformations. "Reflection then translation" scores zero when one is asked for.
  • Describing a mirror line in words. Always give the equation: $x=3$, $y=-x$, and so on.
  • Leaving out the centre of a rotation or enlargement — it is worth a mark on its own.
  • Leaving out the direction of a rotation. Needed for $90°$ and $270°$, not for $180°$.
  • Writing a translation as a coordinate. It must be a column vector, not $(4,-3)$.
  • Calling a shrinking image a "reduction". It is an enlargement with $0
  • Scaling area by $k$ instead of $k^{2}$. Lengths use $k$, areas $k^{2}$, volumes $k^{3}$.
  • Forgetting that a negative scale factor inverts the shape and moves it to the other side of the centre.
