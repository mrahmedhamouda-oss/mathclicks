A scale drawing shrinks the real world onto paper; a bearing says which way to go once you are there. Together they answer every navigation question on the syllabus. Spin the compass dial below until three-figure bearings feel automatic, then chain two legs of a journey together and let the working appear.

◎ What you will be able to do

  • Read a scale written as a ratio or as a statement, and convert between the two.
  • Turn a map length into a real distance, and a real distance into a plan length.
  • Scale areas correctly — by the square of the length factor.
  • Measure, draw and write three-figure bearings.
  • Find a back bearing, and know when to add $180°$ and when to subtract it.
  • Solve navigation problems by scale drawing and by calculation.

1 Scales on maps and plans

FormExampleMeaning
Ratio (no units)$1:50\,000$1 unit on the map = 50 000 of the same units in real life
Statement (with units)1 cm represents 5 kmEvery centimetre drawn stands for 5 kilometres
✎
Key rule. In a ratio scale the units must be the same on both sides. $1:50\,000$ means 1 cm to 50 000 cm — you then convert 50 000 cm into a sensible unit.
The conversions you need
$1\text{ m}=100\text{ cm}\qquad 1\text{ km}=1000\text{ m}\qquad 1\text{ km}=100\,000\text{ cm}$

So $1:50\,000$ is the same as "1 cm represents 0.5 km", because $50\,000\div100\,000=0.5$.

2 Actual distances and areas Interactive

actual $=$ drawing $\times$ scale factor  ·  drawing $=$ actual $\div$ scale factor
  • Decide the direction: map → real (multiply) or real → map (divide).
  • Get both quantities into the same unit before you multiply or divide.
  • Do the calculation.
  • Convert the answer to a sensible unit and state that unit.
!
Real distances are always bigger than map distances, and plan lengths are always smaller than the real object. If your answer goes the wrong way you have multiplied where you should have divided.

Areas. If lengths are scaled by $n$, areas are scaled by $n^{2}$. With a scale of 1 cm : 5 km, an area of $1\text{ cm}^{2}$ on the map represents $5^{2}=25\text{ km}^{2}$.

3 Three-figure bearings Interactive

✎
The three rules of a bearing.
1 · Measured from North.   2 · Measured clockwise.   3 · Always written with three figures — $045°$, never $45°$.
Lab 2 — the bearing dial

Drag the slider and watch the clockwise arc grow from the North line. The panel gives the three-figure form, the nearest compass point, and the back bearing with the reason for adding or subtracting.

Bearing$000°$$045°$$090°$$135°$$180°$$225°$$270°$$315°$

Bearings run from $000°$ up to $360°$, so any value outside that range must be adjusted by adding or subtracting $360$.

Drawing a bearing
  • Mark the starting point and draw a North line vertically upwards from it.
  • Put the centre of the protractor on that point, with $0°$ along the North line.
  • Measure the angle clockwise from North and mark it.
  • Draw the line, then measure the required distance along it using the scale.
  • Label the point and the angle.
!
"The bearing of B from A" means the North line is drawn at A. The point after the word "from" is always where you are standing.

4 Back bearings

The back bearing is the reverse journey: the bearing of A from B, when you know the bearing of B from A.

Under $180°$ → add $180°$  ·  $180°$ or more → subtract $180°$

It works because the two North lines are parallel, so the angles at A and B are co-interior and add to $180°$.

!
Adding $180$ to a bearing of $250°$ gives $430°$, which is not a valid bearing. Subtract instead — the answer must lie between $000°$ and $360°$. Slide past $180°$ in Lab 2 and watch the rule flip.

5 Solving navigation problems Interactive

By accurate scale drawing
  • Write down the scale you are using.
  • Mark the starting point and draw its North line.
  • Measure each bearing with a protractor, drawing each leg to the correct scaled length.
  • Join the final point back to the start.
  • Measure that line and convert with the scale; measure its bearing from the appropriate North line.
By calculation

When the legs meet at a right angle, use Pythagoras and trigonometry. Otherwise use the sine or cosine rule.

✎
Useful angle facts. All North lines are parallel, so you can use: alternate angles (Z-shape) are equal · co-interior angles (C-shape) add to $180°$ · angles around a point add to $360°$.
Lab 3 — the two-leg journey

Set a distance and bearing for each leg. The sketch draws the path and the return line, and the working finds the interior angle at $Q$, the distance $PR$, and the bearing of $R$ from $P$. Try $8$ km on $060°$ then $6$ km on $150°$ for the classic right-angled case.

!
Watch out. Trigonometry gives you an angle inside a triangle, not a bearing. You almost always have to add it to, or subtract it from, another bearing to get the final answer.

✍️ Worked examples

Example 1
Map distance to actual distance
 ⌄

A map has a scale of $1:50\,000$. Two towns are $6$ cm apart on the map. Find the actual distance in kilometres.

1

The scale means 1 cm on the map represents 50 000 cm in reality.

2

Multiply: $6\times50\,000=300\,000$ cm.

3

Convert to km: $300\,000\div100\,000=3$.

Answer. $3$ km
✎
Faster route. Convert the scale once: $1:50\,000$ means 1 cm : 0.5 km. Then $6\times0.5=3$ km.
Example 2
Actual distance to plan length
 ⌄

A plan uses the scale "1 cm represents 250 m". A road is $1.75$ km long. How long is it on the plan?

1

Make the units match: $1.75$ km $=1750$ m.

2

Going from real to plan, so divide: $1750\div250=7$.

Answer. $7$ cm on the plan
!
Converting $1.75$ km to metres first is essential — dividing $1.75$ by $250$ gives $0.007$ cm, which is clearly wrong.
Example 3
Converting a scale and finding an area
 ⌄

A map has a scale of $1:25\,000$. (a) Write this as "1 cm represents … km". (b) A forest covers $8\text{ cm}^{2}$ on the map. Find its real area in $\text{km}^{2}$.

1

(a) $25\,000$ cm $=250$ m $=0.25$ km, so 1 cm represents 0.25 km.

2

(b) Lengths scale by $0.25$, so areas scale by $0.25^{2}=0.0625$.

3

$1\text{ cm}^{2}$ represents $0.0625\text{ km}^{2}$.

4

$8\times0.0625=0.5$

Answer. (a) 1 cm : 0.25 km · (b) $0.5\text{ km}^{2}$
Example 4
Back bearings
 ⌄

The bearing of B from A is $070°$. The bearing of D from C is $215°$. Find the bearing of A from B and the bearing of C from D.

1

$070°$ is less than $180°$, so add: $70+180=250$.

2

$215°$ is more than $180°$, so subtract: $215-180=35$, written as $035°$.

Answer. Bearing of A from B $=250°$ · bearing of C from D $=035°$
!
The three-figure rule still applies to the answer — write $035°$, not $35°$.
Example 5
Navigation with Pythagoras and trigonometry
 ⌄

A ship sails $8$ km from P on a bearing of $060°$ to Q, then $6$ km from Q on a bearing of $150°$ to R. Find (a) the distance PR, (b) the bearing of R from P.

1

Show the angle at Q is $90°$. The bearing turns from $060°$ to $150°$, a change of $150-60=90°$. So triangle PQR is right-angled at Q.

2

(a) Pythagoras: $PR^{2}=8^{2}+6^{2}=64+36=100$, so $PR=10$ km.

3

(b) Find the angle $QPR$ inside the triangle, opposite $6$ and adjacent $8$: $\tan(\angle QPR)=\dfrac{6}{8}=0.75$, giving $36.87°$.

4

Add it to the first bearing, because R lies further clockwise from P than Q does: $060°+36.87°=96.87°\approx097°$.

Answer. (a) $10$ km · (b) $097°$ (to the nearest degree)
!
Don't stop at $36.9°$ — that is the angle inside the triangle, not the bearing. The bearing is always measured from the North line at P. Reproduce the whole thing in Lab 3.
Example 6
Scale drawing to solve a journey
 ⌄

A walker leaves camp C and walks $4$ km on a bearing of $125°$ to a hut H. Describe how to find, by scale drawing, the distance and bearing of C from H. Use a scale of 1 cm : 1 km.

1

Mark C and draw a North line vertically upwards from it.

2

Measure $125°$ clockwise from that North line with a protractor.

3

Draw a line $4$ cm long along that direction (since 1 cm : 1 km) and label the end H.

4

Draw a second North line at H, parallel to the first.

5

Measure the angle clockwise from H's North line round to the line HC.

Answer. The distance is $4$ km (the same journey in reverse). The bearing of C from H is the back bearing: $125°+180°=305°$ — and the scale drawing should confirm that measurement.

! Common mistakes to avoid

  • Writing bearings with fewer than three figures. It is $045°$, never $45°$.
  • Measuring anticlockwise, or from another direction. Bearings are always clockwise from North.
  • Drawing the North line at the wrong point. "The bearing of B from A" needs the North line at A.
  • Giving a back bearing outside $000°$–$360°$. Subtract $180$ instead of adding when the bearing is $180°$ or more.
  • Mixing units in a scale calculation. Convert to a common unit before multiplying or dividing.
  • Scaling areas by the length factor. Areas scale by the square of it.
  • Giving a triangle angle as the final bearing. Combine it with the North line before answering.
