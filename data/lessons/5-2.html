Key Concepts

🔑 Today's Vocabulary

prime polynomial — a polynomial that cannot be written as a product of two polynomials with integral coefficients. Its only factors are 1 and itself.
quadratic form — an expression written as au² + bu + c, where u is some expression in x and a ≠ 0.

🔑 Sum and Difference of Two Cubes

Like quadratics, some cubic polynomials can be factored using polynomial identities.

Sum of Two Cubes:    a³ + b³ = (a + b)(a² − ab + b²)
Difference of Two Cubes:  a³ − b³ = (a − b)(a² + ab + b²)
ℹ Memory aid (SOAP): Same sign, Opposite sign, Always Positive. The binomial takes the same sign as the original, the middle term of the trinomial takes the opposite sign, and the last term is always positive.

🔑 Factoring Techniques — Choose by Number of Terms

Always look for a common factor first to simplify the expression. Then decide whether the remaining polynomial factors using one or more methods.

⚠ Order matters: When an expression is both a difference of squares and a difference of cubes (like 64x⁶ − y⁶), always do the difference of squares first — it factors more completely.

🔑 Solving Polynomial Equations by Factoring

Write the equation so that one side equals 0.
Factor the polynomial completely (GCF first, then a technique from the table).
Apply the Zero Product Property: if AB = 0 then A = 0 or B = 0. Set each factor equal to 0.
Solve each resulting equation and check your solutions in the original equation.

🔑 Quadratic Form

An expression in x can be written in quadratic form as au² + bu + c, where u is some expression in x and a ≠ 0.

Works when:  (highest exponent) = 2 × (middle exponent)

4x²⁰ + 6x¹⁰ + 15  →  u = 2x¹⁰   (since 20 = 2 × 10)  ✓
9x⁶ − 4x² − 12  →  6 ≠ 2 × 2   cannot be written in quadratic form  ✗
ℹ Why it helps: Once in quadratic form you factor a familiar quadratic in u, solve for u, then substitute back to solve for x. Remember to finish by solving for x — not u.

Try It Yourself

Factor and solve each one, then reveal. Problem 4 is quadratic form and problem 6 has a trap in it.

Problem 1

Solve 3x³ − 12x = 0.
13x(x² − 4) = 0GCF first — take out 3x
23x(x − 2)(x + 2) = 0x² − 4 is a difference of squares
3x = 0, x = 2, x = −2Zero Product Property on each factor
x = −2, 0 or 2
Never divide both sides by x to “simplify” — that throws away the solution x = 0. Factor it out instead and keep it.

Problem 2

Factor completely: x³ − 27.
1x³ − 3³Two terms, both perfect cubes → difference of cubes
2a³ − b³ = (a − b)(a² + ab + b²)Standard pattern with a = x, b = 3
(x − 3)(x² + 3x + 9)
Remembering the signs: the binomial copies the sign of the original, and the trinomial's middle term takes the opposite… no — simpler and safer: Same, Opposite, Always Positive. Binomial sign is the same (−), middle term is opposite (+3x), last term always positive (+9).

Problem 3

Factor by grouping: x³ + 2x² − 9x − 18.
1(x³ + 2x²) + (−9x − 18)Four terms → pair them up
2x²(x + 2) − 9(x + 2)Factor each pair — both give (x + 2)
3(x + 2)(x² − 9)Factor out the common bracket
4(x + 2)(x − 3)(x + 3)x² − 9 factors further — go all the way
(x + 2)(x − 3)(x + 3)
If the two brackets do not match after step 2, try pairing differently, or factor −1 out of the second pair. Matching brackets are the signal that grouping is working.

Problem 4

Solve x⁴ − 13x² + 36 = 0.
1let u = x², so u² − 13u + 36 = 0Quadratic form — the exponents 4 and 2 are in a 2:1 ratio
2(u − 4)(u − 9) = 0 → u = 4 or 9Factor the ordinary trinomial
3x² = 4 → x = ±2Substitute back — do not stop at u
4x² = 9 → x = ±3And the second branch
x = −3, −2, 2 or 3 — four solutions, as a degree-4 equation allows
Two traps: answering u = 4, 9 and forgetting to convert back to x, and forgetting the ± on each square root. Either one halves your marks.

Problem 5

Solve x³ = 4x² + 21x.
1x³ − 4x² − 21x = 0Get everything to one side — the Zero Product Property needs a 0
2x(x² − 4x − 21) = 0GCF is x
3x(x − 7)(x + 3) = 0Factor the trinomial: −7 × 3 = −21 and −7 + 3 = −4
x = 0, x = 7 or x = −3
Check x = 7: 343 on the left, and 4(49) + 21(7) = 196 + 147 = 343 on the right ✓

Problem 6

Solve x⁴ − 16 = 0 over the real numbers, then over the complex numbers.
1(x² − 4)(x² + 4) = 0Difference of squares with a = x², b = 4
2(x − 2)(x + 2)(x² + 4) = 0x² − 4 factors again; x² + 4 does not factor over the reals
3x² = −4 → x = ±2iThe remaining factor gives complex roots
Real: x = ±2. Complex: x = 2, −2, 2i, −2i — four roots in total
A sum of squares is not factorable over the reals. x² + 4 is not (x + 2)², and it is not (x + 2)(x − 2). Only the difference of squares factors. Over the complex numbers it does split, into (x + 2i)(x − 2i).

⚠ Common Mistakes to Avoid

⚠ Forgetting the GCF. 54x⁵ − 128x²y³ looks unfactorable until you pull out 2x². Check for a common factor before anything else.
⚠ Wrong sign in the cube identity. a³ + b³ = (a + b)(a² − ab + b²). The middle sign of the trinomial is the opposite of the binomial sign, and the last term is always positive.
⚠ Cubes before squares. Factoring 64x⁶ − y⁶ as a difference of cubes first gives an incomplete factorisation. Do the difference of squares first.
⚠ Solving for u and stopping. In quadratic form, u is a substitution. Replace u and solve for x, and remember that x² = negative gives imaginary solutions.
⚠ Assuming everything factors. If no technique applies and the coefficients are integral, the polynomial is prime — that is a valid answer.
