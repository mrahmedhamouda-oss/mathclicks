Core Concepts

1. What is a tangent?

A tangent just grazes a circle — it touches it once and then leaves. It never cuts through the inside.

tangent to a circle — a line or segment in the plane of a circle that intersects the circle in exactly one point and does not contain any points in the interior of the circle.
point of tangency — for a line that intersects a circle in one point, the point at which they intersect.
common tangent — a line or segment that is tangent to two circles in the same plane.
circumscribed angle — an angle with sides that are tangent to a circle.
circumscribed polygon — a polygon whose vertices lie outside the circle and whose sides are all tangent to the circle.
shortest distance — the shortest distance from a tangent to the center of a circle is the radius drawn to the point of tangency.

More than one line can be tangent to the same circle — in fact infinitely many, one at every point of the circle.

Theorem 10.11
In a plane, a line is tangent to a circle if and only if it is perpendicular to a radius drawn to the point of tangency.
If line ℓ is tangent to ⊙O at P, then ℓ ⊥ OP.
If ℓ ⊥ OP at P on ⊙O, then ℓ is tangent to ⊙O.

Because "if and only if" works both ways, this single theorem does two jobs: it gives you a right angle when you know a line is tangent, and it proves tangency when you can show the right angle exists.

2. Testing whether a segment is tangent

Because Theorem 10.11 hands you a right triangle, the Pythagorean Theorem becomes a tangency detector.

Identify the radius (call it r), the segment you suspect is tangent (call it t), and the segment from the exterior point to the center (call it d).
Test whether r² + t² = d².
If the equation is true, the triangle is a right triangle, the segment is ⊥ to the radius at the point on the circle, and by Theorem 10.11 the segment is tangent.
If the equation is false, the angle is not 90°, so the segment is not tangent.
r² + t² = d²  →  tangent   |   r² + t² ≠ d²  →  not tangent
Problem-Solving Tip — Solve a Simpler Problem: Sketch the right triangle without the circle and label it. Once the circle is gone you are just doing Pythagoras.
Theorem 10.12 — Tangent to a Circle Theorem
If two segments from the same exterior point are tangent to a circle, then they are congruent.
If PA and PB are tangent to ⊙O, then PA ≅ PB, so PA = PB.

Why it works: △OAP and △OBP are right triangles that share hypotenuse OP and have congruent legs OA ≅ OB (both radii). By HL they are congruent, so PA ≅ PB.

Theorem 10.13
If two segments or lines are tangent to a circle, then the circumscribed angle and the central angle that intercept the arc formed by the points of tangency are supplementary.
If QS and RS are tangent to ⊙P, then m∠P + m∠S = 180°.

Why it works: quadrilateral PQSR has right angles at Q and at R (Theorem 10.11). Its four angles add to 360°, and 90 + 90 = 180, so the remaining two angles — ∠P and ∠S — must add to 180°.

3. Circumscribed polygons

A circumscribed polygon wraps around a circle: every vertex sits outside the circle and every side is tangent to it. Each vertex therefore has two tangent segments coming out of it, and by Theorem 10.12 those two are equal. That single fact solves almost every perimeter problem.

Perimeter of a circumscribed polygon = 2 × (sum of one tangent length from each vertex)
Watch Out! — Identifying Circumscribed Polygons: Just because a circle is tangent to one or more of the sides of a polygon does not mean the polygon is circumscribed about the circle. Every side must be tangent, and the circle must sit inside.

4. Counting common tangents

How many lines can be tangent to two circles at once depends entirely on how the circles are positioned.

Position of the two circlesNumber of common tangents
Separate, neither inside the other4 (2 external + 2 internal)
Externally tangent (touching at one point, outside)3
Intersecting at two points2 (both external)
Internally tangent (touching at one point, one inside)1
One entirely inside the other (including concentric)0 — no common tangent
Think About It! Two circles have no common tangent exactly when one circle lies strictly inside the other without touching it.
1
Tangent line → instantly draw the radius to the point of tangency and mark a right angle.
2
Right angle → the Pythagorean Theorem r² + t² = d² is available.
3
Two tangents from the same exterior point are congruent. Set the two expressions equal.
4
Circumscribed angle + central angle = 180°.
5
Circumscribed polygon → the two tangent segments at each vertex are equal; the perimeter is twice their total.

Common mistakes to avoid:
  • Using the outside piece of a segment instead of the whole distance to the center. If the exterior part is 6 and the radius is 9, the hypotenuse is 15.
  • Putting the tangent length in the hypotenuse position. The hypotenuse always runs from the exterior point to the center.
  • Stopping after finding x when the question asked for a length or an angle measure.
  • Assuming a polygon is circumscribed because the circle touches some sides — every side must be tangent.
  • Confusing "supplementary" (adds to 180°) with "congruent" in Theorem 10.13.
  • Forgetting that intersecting circles have only 2 common tangents, not 4.
