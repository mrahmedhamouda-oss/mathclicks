Core Concepts

1. What a chord is

A chord is a segment whose two endpoints both sit on the circle. That is the whole definition — nothing about the centre. If a chord happens to pass through the centre it is the longest chord possible, and we give it a special name: a diameter.

If a chord is not a diameter, then its endpoints divide the circle into a minor arc (the shorter piece, less than 180°) and a major arc (the longer piece, more than 180°).

Chord — a segment with both endpoints on the circle.
Diameter — a chord that passes through the centre; length 2r.
Minor arc — the arc cut off by a chord that measures less than 180°.
Major arc — the remaining arc, measuring more than 180°.
Why arcs and chords travel together: draw the two radii to the endpoints of a chord. You always get an isosceles triangle whose apex angle is the central angle, and the central angle is exactly the measure of the minor arc. Change the chord and you change that angle; change the angle and you change the chord. That single picture is behind every theorem below.

2. Congruent arcs ↔ congruent chords

In one circle (or in two circles of equal radius), a longer chord always cuts off a bigger arc and a bigger arc always needs a longer chord. So equal chords and equal arcs come as a package.

Theorem 10.3
In the same circle or in congruent circles, two minor arcs are congruent if and only if their corresponding chords are congruent.
Example: arc FG ≅ arc HJ if and only if FG ≅ HJ.
⇔
Both directions work. You may start from equal arcs and conclude equal chords, or start from equal chords and conclude equal arcs. Watch the phrase “in the same circle or in congruent circles” — the radii must match.
Watch Out! Two circles of different size can have chords of the same length that cut off completely different arc measures. The theorem is worthless without the “same or congruent circles” condition.

3. Bisecting an arc

If a line, segment, or ray divides an arc into two congruent arcs, then it bisects the arc. The point where it meets the arc is the arc’s midpoint.

Study Tip — Arc Bisectors: in the figure of Theorem 10.4, FH is an arc bisector of arc JG whenever arc JF ≅ arc FG. An arc bisector splits the arc into two equal-measure pieces, exactly the way an angle bisector splits an angle.

4. A diameter (or radius) perpendicular to a chord

Drop a perpendicular from the centre onto a chord and it does double duty: it cuts the chord in half and it cuts the arc in half.

Theorem 10.4
If a diameter (or radius) of a circle is perpendicular to a chord, then it bisects the chord and its arc.
Example: If diameter AB is perpendicular to chord XY at Z, then XZ ≅ ZY and arc XB ≅ arc BY.
Perpendicular from centre → chord is split in half
chord = 2 × (half-chord)    arc = 2 × (half-arc)
r² = d² + (chord/2)²   where d = distance from centre to chord
√
The half-chord, the distance d, and the radius form a right triangle. Almost every numeric chord problem is really the Pythagorean Theorem in disguise.

5. The perpendicular bisector of a chord

Turn Theorem 10.4 around. If a line is both perpendicular to a chord and cuts it in half, that line has no choice but to run straight through the centre.

Theorem 10.5
The perpendicular bisector of a chord contains a diameter (or radius) of the circle.
Example: If AB is a perpendicular bisector of chord XY, then AB is a diameter of ☉C.
Why: every point on the perpendicular bisector of XY is equidistant from X and Y. The centre is equidistant from X and Y as well (both distances are radii), so the centre must lie on that bisector.
◎
This is how you find a lost centre: draw any two chords, construct both perpendicular bisectors, and the point where they cross is the centre.

6. Chords equidistant from the centre

“Distance from the centre to a chord” always means the perpendicular distance. Chords that sit the same distance from the centre are the same length — and the closer a chord creeps toward the centre, the longer it gets (the diameter, at distance 0, is the longest of all).

Theorem 10.6
In the same circle or in congruent circles, two chords are congruent if and only if they are equidistant from the centre.
Example: FG ≅ JH if and only if LX = LY.
half-chord = √(r² − d²)    chord = 2√(r² − d²)
d = √(r² − (chord/2)²)
Read the diagram: are the small squares (right angles) at the feet of the segments from the centre? If yes, those segments are the distances.
Equal distances → set the two chord expressions equal to each other.
Equal chords → set the two distance expressions equal to each other.
Solve the linear equation, then substitute back to get the quantity actually asked for.
Watch Out! After you solve for x, re-read the question. Many problems ask for the chord length or the distance, not for x. Finishing at x = 5 when the question wanted FG = 16 is the most common lost mark in this lesson.

Worked Examples

JK ≅ LM and m(arc LM) = 75°. Find m(arc JK).
1JK ≅ LMGiven
2arc JK ≅ arc LMThm 10.3: congruent chords → congruent arcs
3m(arc JK) = m(arc LM)Definition of congruent arcs
4m(arc JK) = 75°Substitution
m(arc JK) = 75°
Check: both chords subtend a central angle of 75°, and in the drawing the two chords really are the same length — consistent.
Trap: do not subtract from 180° or 360°. Congruent arcs are equal, not supplementary.

2DE = FGDefinition of congruent segments

Every formula from this lesson

r² = d² + (c/2)² c = 2√(r² − d²) d = √(r² − (c/2)²) chord = 2 × half-chord arc = 2 × half-arc diameter = 2r minor arc + major arc = 360°
Common mistakes to avoid
  • Using the diameter as the hypotenuse instead of the radius.
  • Forgetting to double the half-chord: the perpendicular gives you half of the chord, not all of it.
  • Solving for x and stopping, when the question asked for a length or an arc measure.
  • Applying Theorem 10.3 or 10.6 across circles of different radii — the circles must be congruent.
  • Assuming a segment from the centre to a chord is the distance when no right angle is marked.
  • Treating congruent arcs as supplementary (subtracting from 180°) instead of simply equal.
★
The one picture to memorise: centre → perpendicular → chord. That perpendicular splits the chord in half, splits the arc in half, passes through the centre, and creates a right triangle with legs d and c/2 and hypotenuse r. Four theorems, one diagram.
