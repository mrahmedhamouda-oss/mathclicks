Core Concepts

Why we need a second law

The Law of Sines needs a complete pair — an angle and the side directly across from it. If you are handed two sides and the angle squeezed between them (SAS), or all three sides and no angles at all (SSS), you never get such a pair, so the Law of Sines is useless. When the Law of Sines cannot be used to solve a triangle, the Law of Cosines may apply.

You can use the Law of Cosines to find the length of the third side of a triangle when the measures of two sides and the included angle are known, or to find the angle measures of a triangle if the lengths of all three sides are known.

Theorem 9.11 · Law of Cosines
If △ABC has lengths a, b, and c, representing the lengths of the sides opposite the angles with measures A, B, and C, then
a2 = b2 + c2 − 2bc·cos A
b2 = a2 + c2 − 2ac·cos B
c2 = a2 + b2 − 2ab·cos C
Read the pattern once and you never have to memorise three formulas: the side you are solving for sits alone on the left, and the angle on the right is the one opposite it. The other two sides appear twice — squared, and again in the product 2(side)(side).

The same law, rearranged to hunt for angles

Solve a2 = b2 + c2 − 2bc cos A for cos A and you get the angle form. This is the version you use for SSS.

cos A = (b2 + c2 − a2) / (2bc)  ∴  m∠A = cos−1[(b2 + c2 − a2) / (2bc)]

Notice the numerator: the squared side that stands alone is the one being subtracted, and it is the side opposite the angle you want.

Which law do I reach for?

ASA — two angles and the included side. Third angle by Angle-Sum, then Law of Sines.
AAS — two angles and a non-included side. You already have a complete pair → Law of Sines.
SSA — two sides and a non-included angle. Complete pair exists → Law of Sines (ambiguous case).
SAS — two sides and the included angle. No pair → Law of Cosines.
SSS — three sides, no angles. No pair → Law of Cosines.
Right triangle — sine, cosine, tangent and the Pythagorean Theorem are fastest, but either law still works.

Method — SAS: find the missing side

Name the unknown side with the letter matching the given angle. If ∠C is known, the missing side is c.
Write c2 = a2 + b2 − 2ab cos C.
Substitute the two known side lengths and the angle. Keep the cosine unevaluated for now.
Simplify the constants: c2 = (a2+b2) − (2ab) cos C.
Take the positive square root of each side, then use a calculator in degree mode.

Method — SSS: find a missing angle

Choose which angle you want; write the equation whose lone left-hand side is the side opposite that angle.
Substitute all three lengths.
Simplify both constant pieces, then isolate the cosine term.
Divide by the coefficient of the cosine. Watch the signs — a negative result is legal and means the angle is obtuse.
Apply cos−1 and round.
1
The Law of Cosines works on every triangle — acute, right or obtuse. Nothing about it assumes a right angle.
2
The Pythagorean Theorem is just the Law of Cosines with C = 90°: since cos 90° = 0, the term −2ab cos C vanishes and c2 = a2 + b2.
3
cos A < 0 ⇔ A is obtuse. So if a2 > b2 + c2, angle A is obtuse; if they are equal, A is right.
4
A triangle has at most one obtuse angle, and it is always opposite the longest side. That single fact rescues you from the ambiguous case.
Study Tip · SSS: When given all three side lengths of a triangle, you must use the Law of Cosines to find the first angle measure. Then you can use either the Law of Cosines or the Law of Sines to find a second angle measure. If you plan to use the Law of Cosines for the second angle, the order does not matter. But if you want to use the Law of Sines for the second angle, you should find the largest angle first with the Law of Cosines.
Watch Out! Rounding. When you round a numerical solution and then use it in later calculations, your answers may become inaccurate. Wait until after you have completed all of your calculations to round.
Watch Out! The inverse sine lies. sin−1 on a calculator only ever returns an acute value. If the angle you are chasing is actually obtuse, the calculator hands you its supplement. cos−1 has no such flaw — it returns values from 0° to 180° and gets obtuse angles right automatically. That is why the Law of Cosines is the safe tool for the largest angle.
Study Tip · Obtuse Angles: There are also values for sin A, cos A, and tan A when m∠A ≥ 90°. Values of the ratios for these angles can be found by using the trigonometric functions on your calculator. It is good practice to solve for smaller angles first, and then use the Triangle Angle-Sum Theorem to find the measure of the largest, third angle.

Worked Examples

Find the value of x to the nearest tenth. In △ABC, AC = 2.9, CB = 0.9, and m∠C = 44°. AB = x.

We are given the measures of two sides and their included angle. Use the Law of Cosines to write an equation. The unknown side is opposite ∠C, so it plays the role of c.

1c2 = a2 + b2 − 2ab cos CLaw of Cosines
2x2 = 0.92 + 2.92 − 2(0.9)(2.9) cos 44°Substitution
3x2 = 9.22 − 5.22 cos 44°Simplify
4x = √(9.22 − 5.22 cos 44°)Take the positive square root of each side
5x ≈ √(9.22 − 3.755) = √5.465Use a calculator
x ≈ 2.3
Check: 2.3 lies between 2.9 − 0.9 = 2.0 and 2.9 + 0.9 = 3.8, so the Triangle Inequality is satisfied. ✓ Also, 44° is acute, so x should be shorter than the 2.9 side — and it is.
Watch Out! 2(0.9)(2.9) cos 44° is one product. Do not compute (9.22 − 5.22) first and then multiply by cos 44°. Multiplication comes before subtraction.
Find the value of x to the nearest whole number. In △MNP, MN = 9, MP = 11, NP = 5, and m∠P = x°.
1p2 = m2 + n2 − 2mn cos PLaw of Cosines
292 = 52 + 112 − 2(5)(11) cos x°Substitution
381 = 146 − 110 cos x°Simplify
4−65 = −110 cos x°Subtract 146 from each side
5−65/−110 = cos x°Divide each side by −110
6x = cos−1(0.5909)Use the inverse cosine function
7x ≈ 53.78Use a calculator
x ≈ 54
Check: 9 is the middle-length side, so ∠P should be the middle-sized angle. cos−1 of a positive number is acute — consistent with a 54° result. ✓
GOLF · The tee box is 378 yards from the hole. To avoid a water hazard, Nhat turns 32° and hits a shot 261.5 yards up the fairway. Find the distance from Nhat's ball to the hole, to the nearest yard.

Because we know the measures of two sides of the triangle (378 and 261.5) and the included angle (32°), use the Law of Cosines to find the remaining distance x.

1x2 = 261.52 + 3782 − 2(261.5)(378) cos 32°Law of Cosines
2x2 = 211,266.25 − 197,694 cos 32°Simplify
3x = √(211,266.25 − 197,694 cos 32°)Take the positive square root of each side
4x ≈ √(211,266.25 − 167,654) = √43,612Use a calculator
x ≈ 209 yards
Study Tip · Rounding: Do not round 197,694 cos 32° to a few digits and then subtract — carry the full calculator value all the way to the square root, and round only at the very end.
Solve △ABC. Round to the nearest degree. AB = 8, AC = 11, BC = 7.

Because 72 + 82 ≠ 112 (49 + 64 = 113, not 121), this is not a right triangle. All three sides are given (SSS), so decide which angle you want first, then use the Law of Cosines. Here a = 7, b = 11, c = 8.

1a2 = b2 + c2 − 2bc cos ALaw of Cosines
272 = 112 + 82 − 2(11)(8) cos ASubstitute
349 = 185 − 176 cos ASimplify
4−136 = −176 cos ASubtract 185 from each side
5−136/−176 = cos ADivide each side by −176

But b = 11 is the longest side, so ∠B must be the largest angle — and 86° would be smaller than the third angle. The calculator returned the acute twin; the true angle is its supplement, 180° − 86° = 94°. Use the Law of Cosines for the largest angle and the trap disappears.

Solve △FGH. Round angle measures to the nearest degree and side measures to the nearest tenth. FH = 3, HG = 2, and m∠H = 90°.

Common mistakes to avoid
  • Pairing the wrong angle with the lone side. The angle in the cosine is always opposite the side by itself.
  • Subtracting before multiplying: 9.22 − 5.22 cos 44° means 9.22 − (5.22 · cos 44°).
  • Forgetting that −2ab cos C adds when C is obtuse, because cos C is then negative.
  • Dropping the square root at the end of an SAS problem — you solved for a², not a.
  • Using the Law of Sines to find a large angle after an SSS start; sin−1 only returns acute values.
  • Rounding intermediate values and then continuing to calculate.
  • Reaching for the Law of Cosines when a complete angle–side pair already exists — the Law of Sines is far less work.
  • Calculator in radian mode. Every angle here is in degrees.
