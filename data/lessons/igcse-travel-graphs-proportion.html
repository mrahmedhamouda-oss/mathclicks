Distance–time and speed–time graphs are the two most heavily tested graph types in Paper 2 and Paper 4 — and the two most often confused. Build your own journeys with the sliders below, watch the gradients and areas update, and the difference between them stops being something you have to remember.

◎ What you will be able to do

  • Calculate speed, distance and time — with the units matching.
  • Interpret distance–time graphs, including stops and curved sections.
  • Interpret speed–time graphs: gradient for acceleration, area for distance.
  • Recognise direct proportion and solve real-life proportion problems.

$\text{average speed}=\dfrac{\text{total distance}}{\text{total time}}$

Stops still count in the total time. You cannot find an average speed by averaging two speeds unless equal times were spent at each.

2 Distance–time graphs Interactive

✎
Key rule. On a distance–time graph, gradient = speed. Time goes along the bottom; distance from the starting point goes up the side.
Section of graphWhat is happening
Straight line sloping upMoving away from the start at constant speed
Steeper lineMoving faster
Horizontal lineStationary — the distance is not changing
Straight line sloping downReturning towards the start
Curve getting steeperSpeeding up (accelerating)
Curve getting shallowerSlowing down
!
Watch out. A horizontal section does not mean "travelling at a steady speed" — it means not moving at all. And a downward slope does not mean negative distance; it means heading back.
Lab 2 — build your own journey

Set the outward speed and time, the length of the stop, and the return speed. Watch how the average speed for the whole journey drops as soon as you add a stop — even though neither travelling speed changed.

Curved distance–time graphs

If the graph is a curve, the speed is changing. To find the speed at one particular moment, draw a tangent at that point and find its gradient. Two points on the curve would give you the average speed over an interval instead.

3 Speed–time graphs Interactive

✎
Two key rules. Gradient = acceleration  ·  Area under the graph = distance travelled.
Section of graphWhat is happening
Line sloping upAccelerating (speed increasing)
Horizontal lineConstant speed, zero acceleration
Line sloping downDecelerating (speed decreasing)

CurveThe acceleration itself is changing
$\text{acceleration}=\dfrac{\text{change in speed}}{\text{time taken}}\quad(\text{m/s}^2)$ — a negative answer means deceleration

To get the distance, split the region under the graph into triangles, rectangles and trapezia: triangle $=\tfrac12 bh$, rectangle $=bh$, trapezium $=\tfrac12(a+b)h$. Add them up — or treat the whole shape as one trapezium if it is one.

Lab 3 — the speed–time graph builder

!
Watch out. On a speed–time graph the gradient is acceleration, not speed. Reading the gradient when the question asks for distance is the most common error in this whole topic.

4 Proportional relationships Interactive

Two quantities are in direct proportion if one is always a fixed multiple of the other — doubling one doubles the other.

$y\propto x\ \rightarrow\ y=kx\ \rightarrow\ \dfrac{y}{x}=k$ (constant)

The graph is a straight line through the origin, and the gradient is the constant $k$.

MethodHow it worksBest when…
UnitaryFind the value of one unit, then multiply upThe numbers divide neatly
Scale factorFind how many times bigger the new amount is, then apply the same factorThe two amounts are simple multiples
AlgebraicWrite $y=kx$, find $k$, then use the formulaSeveral answers are needed, or the numbers are awkward
Lab 4 — is it direct proportion?

Type any pairs of values. The lab works out $y\div x$ for every column: constant means direct proportion, and the line through the origin is drawn for you.

!
Watch out. A straight line that does not pass through the origin — a taxi fare with a fixed charge, for example — is not direct proportion, even though it is linear.

✍️ Worked examples

Example 1
Speed, distance and time with unit conversion
 ⌄

(a) A train travels 315 km in 2 h 30 min. Find its average speed in km/h. (b) Convert 72 km/h into m/s.

1

(a) Convert the time first: $2\text{ h }30\text{ min}=2.5\text{ h}$.

2

$\text{speed}=315\div2.5=126\text{ km/h}$.

3

(b) $72\text{ km}=72\,000\text{ m}$ and $1\text{ hour}=3600\text{ s}$.

4

$72\,000\div3600=20\text{ m/s}$ — or simply $72\div3.6=20$.

Answer. (a) 126 km/h · (b) 20 m/s. Check both in Lab 1.
Example 2
Reading a distance–time graph
 ⌄

A journey graph shows: out to 100 km over the first 2 hours, flat from 2 h to 3 h, then back to 0 km by 5 h. Find the outward speed, describe the middle section, find the return speed and the average speed for the whole journey.

Outward leg (0 → 2 h): 100 km in 2 h, so $\text{speed}=100\div2=50$ km/h.

2 h to 3 h: the line is horizontal, so the distance stays at 100 km — the traveller is stationary for 1 hour.

Return leg (3 h → 5 h): 100 km back in 2 h, so again 50 km/h. (The gradient is $-50$; the sign only shows direction.)

Average speed: total distance $=100+100=200$ km in a total time of 5 h, so $200\div5=40$ km/h.

Answer. 50 km/h out · stopped for 1 h · 50 km/h back · 40 km/h average. Set Lab 2 to 50 / 2 h / 1 h / 50 to reproduce it exactly.
!
The average (40) is lower than the travelling speed (50) because the stopped hour still counts in the total time.
Example 3
Reading a speed–time graph
 ⌄

A car accelerates from rest to 20 m/s in 10 s, holds 20 m/s for 30 s, then decelerates to rest over 20 s. Find the acceleration, the deceleration, and the total distance.

Acceleration (0 → 10 s): $a=(20-0)\div10=2\text{ m/s}^2$.

Deceleration (40 → 60 s): $a=(0-20)\div20=-1\text{ m/s}^2$, i.e. a deceleration of $1\text{ m/s}^2$.

PieceShapeArea
0–10 sTriangle$\tfrac12\times10\times20=100$ m
10–40 sRectangle$30\times20=600$ m
40–60 sTriangle$\tfrac12\times20\times20=200$ m
Answer. Total $=100+600+200=900$ m.
Check as one trapezium: parallel sides 30 s and 60 s, height 20 m/s → $\tfrac12(30+60)\times20=900$ m ✔ — this is exactly what Lab 3 shows with the default settings.
Example 4
Speed at an instant from a curve
 ⌄

A distance–time graph curves upwards. At $t=3$ s a tangent is drawn passing through $(1,0)$ and $(5,24)$. Find the speed at $t=3$.

The speed at an instant is the gradient of the tangent at that point. Using the two given points on the tangent:

$\text{gradient}=\dfrac{24-0}{5-1}=\dfrac{24}{4}=6$

Attach the units from the axes: distance in metres, time in seconds.

Answer. The speed at $t=3$ s is $6$ m/s.
!
Don't use two points on the curve — that gives the average speed over an interval, not the speed at that instant.
Example 5
Real-life direct proportion
 ⌄

5 kg of rice costs \$12.50. (a) Find the cost of 8 kg. (b) How much rice can be bought for \$30?

1

Unitary method. Cost of 1 kg: $12.50\div5=\$2.50$, so $k=2.5$ and $C=2.5m$.

2

(a) $C=2.5\times8=\$20.00$.

3

(b) $30=2.5m$, so $m=30\div2.5=12$ kg.

Answer. (a) \$20.00 · (b) 12 kg.
Alternative. Scale factor: $8\div5=1.6$, so the cost is $12.50\times1.6=\$20$.
Example 6
Is it direct proportion?
 ⌄

Decide whether each table shows direct proportion.
Table A: $(2,7)\ (4,14)\ (6,21)$  ·  Table B: $(2,9)\ (4,15)\ (6,21)$.

Table A: $7/2=3.5$, $14/4=3.5$, $21/6=3.5$ — the ratio is constant.

Table B: $9/2=4.5$, $15/4=3.75$, $21/6=3.5$ — not constant. The steps are even ($+6$ for every $+2$ in $x$), so this is linear, $y=3x+3$ — a straight line, but it cuts the y-axis at 3, not at the origin.

Answer. A is direct proportion, $y=3.5x$. B is linear but not proportional. Load Set A and Set B in Lab 4 to see both verdicts.

! Common mistakes to avoid

  • Writing 2 h 30 min as 2.30. It is 2.5 hours — convert minutes by dividing by 60.
  • Mixing units. Metres with hours gives a meaningless speed. Convert first.
  • Reading a flat section of a distance–time graph as constant speed. It means stopped.
  • Using the gradient of a speed–time graph to find distance. Distance is the area; the gradient gives acceleration.
  • Forgetting stops when calculating average speed. Use total distance ÷ total time, including waiting time.
  • Assuming every straight-line graph is proportional. It must pass through the origin.
