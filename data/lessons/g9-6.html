Core Concepts

1. Indirect measurement — measuring what you cannot reach

Sometimes you cannot put a tape measure on the thing you want to measure — a drone in the sky, the top of a cliff, the far side of a canyon. Indirect measurement means measuring something you can reach (a distance along the ground, your own eye height, an angle) and then using similar figures, proportions, or trigonometry to find the thing you cannot reach.

In this lesson the tool is trigonometry: build a right triangle whose angle you know and whose one side you know, then use sine, cosine, or tangent — or an inverse ratio — to get the missing part.

indirect measurement — finding a measure by using similar figures, proportions, or trigonometric ratios instead of measuring the object directly.
angle of elevation — the angle formed by a horizontal line and an observer's line of sight to an object above the horizontal line.
angle of depression — the angle formed by a horizontal line and an observer's line of sight to an object below the horizontal line.
line of sight — the straight segment from the observer's eye to the object. In the right triangle it is always the hypotenuse.

2. Both angles start at a HORIZONTAL line

This is the single most common place to lose points. An angle of elevation and an angle of depression are always measured from a horizontal line — never from a vertical wall, pole, or building.

elevation  →  you look UP from the horizontal
depression  →  you look DOWN from the horizontal
Watch Out! If a problem says "the angle between the pole and the line of sight is 62°", that is not an angle of elevation. The angle of elevation would be 90° − 62° = 28°, because the pole is vertical and the elevation angle is measured from the horizontal.

3. Elevation and depression are equal (alternate interior angles)

Look at the observer's horizontal line and the object's horizontal line. Both are horizontal, so they are parallel. The line of sight is a transversal cutting them. Therefore the angle of elevation from the lower point and the angle of depression from the upper point are alternate interior angles — and alternate interior angles are congruent.

Alternate Interior Angles Theorem (applied here)
Because the two horizontal lines are parallel and the line of sight is a transversal, m∠(elevation) = m∠(depression). You may transfer a depression angle down to the object as an equal elevation angle — and that is usually the move that puts the angle inside your right triangle where you can use it.
!
A depression angle marked at the top of a figure is almost never inside the triangle. Slide it down: the equal elevation angle at the bottom vertex is inside the triangle.

4. Choosing the right ratio

Label the right triangle from the angle you know: the side across from it is opposite, the side touching it (that is not the hypotenuse) is adjacent, and the line of sight is the hypotenuse.

sin θ = opposite / hypotenuse
cos θ = adjacent / hypotenuse
tan θ = opposite / adjacent
Draw the figure. Put in the horizontal dashed line at the observer.
Mark the known angle and the known side; call the unknown x.
Decide which two sides are involved (opp, adj, hyp) → that picks sin, cos, or tan.
Write the equation, then solve. If x is in the denominator, multiply by x first, then divide.
If the angle is unknown, use an inverse: θ = tan−1(opp/adj), etc.
Study Tip — Units of Measure. Convert everything to one unit before you compute. 66 in. must become 5.5 ft before you add it to a distance measured in feet.

5. Eye height matters

The right triangle sits on the observer's eye-level horizontal, not on the ground. If an object is 102 ft above the ground and the observer's eyes are 6 ft above the ground, the vertical leg of the triangle is 102 − 6 = 96 ft. At the end, add the eye height back if the question asks for a height above the ground.

√
Subtract eye height going in; add eye height coming out.

6. Area of a triangle from two sides and the included angle

The usual area formula needs a height. When you do not know the height but you know two sides and the angle between them, trigonometry supplies the height for you.

Key Concept · Area of a Triangle
To find the area of a triangle when the height is not known, you can use Area = ½ab sin C, where a and b are side lengths and C is the included angle.

Why it works: drop the height h from the vertex where side a meets the third side. In that right triangle, sin C = h/a, so h = a sin C. Then Area = ½·b·h = ½·b·(a sin C) = ½ab sin C.

Watch Out! The angle must be between the two sides you multiply. If you are given two sides and a non-included angle, first find the included angle using 180° − (sum of the other two angles).

Worked Examples

DRONES  Rakeem is flying his drone at the park. He spots the drone at an angle of elevation that he estimates to be 30°. The remote control tells Rakeem that his drone is 102 feet above the ground. If Rakeem is 6 feet tall, how far is he from the drone to the nearest foot?
1BC = 102 − 6 = 96subtract eye height
2sin A = BC / ABsin = opposite / hypotenuse
3sin 30° = 96 / xm∠A = 30°, BC = 96, AB = x
4x · sin 30° = 96multiply each side by x
5x = 96 / sin 30° = 96 / 0.5divide each side by sin 30°
x = 192  →  Rakeem is 192 feet from his drone
Check: 192 · sin 30° = 192(0.5) = 96 ✓  The hypotenuse (192) is longer than both legs, as it must be.
Trap: using 102 instead of 96 gives 204 ft. The 6 ft of Rakeem's height is below the horizontal line of sight, so it is not part of the triangle.
SIGHTSEEING  Pedro looks out a window 30 feet from the ground and spots a fire hydrant on the ground at an estimated angle of depression of 40°. What is the horizontal distance from Pedro to the hydrant to the nearest foot?
1AB ∥ DC, so m∠BAC = m∠ACDAlternate Interior Angles Theorem
2m∠ACD = 40°definition of congruent angles
3tan C = AD / DCtan = opposite / adjacent
4tan 40° = 30 / xm∠C = 40°, AD = 30, DC = x
5x · tan 40° = 30multiply each side by x
6x = 30 / tan 40° ≈ 30 / 0.8391 ≈ 35.75divide each side by tan 40°
x ≈ 36 feet
Check: 35.75 · tan 40° ≈ 30.0 ✓  Since 40° < 45°, the horizontal leg should be longer than the vertical leg — and 35.75 > 30. ✓
MALL  Wei is estimating the height of the second floor in the mall. She sights the second floor at a 10° angle of elevation. She then steps forward 50 feet, until she is 5.5 feet from the wall, and sights the second floor again. If Wei's line of sight is 66 inches above the ground, at what angle of elevation does she sight the second floor?
166 in. = 5.5 ftconvert to one unit
2BD = 50 + 5.5 = 55.5she stepped 50 ft closer
3tan 10° = AB / BD = AB / 55.5tan = opposite / adjacent in △ABD
455.5 · tan 10° = ABmultiply each side by 55.5
5tan y° = AB / BC = AB / 5.5tan = opposite / adjacent in △ABC
65.5 · tan y° = ABmultiply each side by 5.5
75.5 · tan y° = 55.5 · tan 10°both equal AB — substitute
8tan y° = (55.5 · tan 10°) / 5.5 ≈ 1.7793divide each side by 5.5
9y = tan−1(1.7793) ≈ 60.66inverse tangent
y ≈ 60.66°
Follow-up: AB = 5.5 tan 60.66° ≈ 9.8 ft, so the second floor is about 9.8 + 5.5 = 15.3, about 15 feet above the ground. Remember to add Wei's 5.5 ft eye height back in.
The big idea: two different triangles share the same unknown side AB. Write an expression for AB from each triangle, set them equal, and the shared side disappears.

Common mistakes to avoid
  • Measuring the angle of elevation or depression from a vertical line instead of the horizontal.
  • Putting the depression angle inside the triangle without moving it — it lives outside the triangle, at the top.
  • Forgetting to subtract the observer's eye height from the object's height above the ground.
  • Mixing units: 66 in. is 5.5 ft, not 66 ft.
  • Multiplying when the unknown is in the denominator (tan 40° = 30/x gives x = 30 / tan 40°, not 30 · tan 40°).
  • Using a non-included angle in ½ab sin C.
  • Leaving the calculator in radian mode — it must be in degree mode.
