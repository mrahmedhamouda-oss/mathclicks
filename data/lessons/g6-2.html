Core Concepts

1. What an angle bisector is

An angle bisector is a line, segment, or ray that cuts an angle into two congruent angles. Nothing more, nothing less — it splits the opening exactly in half.

If ray BD bisects ∠ABC, then
∠ABD ≅ ∠DBC   and   m∠ABD = m∠DBC = ½·m∠ABC

Turning that around: m∠ABC = 2 · m∠ABD. Every problem in this lesson is built on those two sentences.

Angle bisector — a ray (or line or segment) that divides an angle into two congruent angles.
Equidistant — the same distance from. Distance from a point to a line always means the perpendicular distance.
Incenter — the point where the three angle bisectors of a triangle meet.
Point of concurrency — the single point three or more lines all pass through.

2. Distance from a point to a side means perpendicular distance

Before the theorems make sense you must accept one convention: the distance from a point to a line is measured along the perpendicular. Any slanted segment is longer. That is why every diagram in this lesson is covered in little right-angle squares — they are not decoration, they are what makes the lengths count as distances.

Theorem 6.4 — Angle Bisector Theorem
Words: If a point is on the bisector of an angle, then it is equidistant from the sides of the angle.
Symbols: If ray BF bisects ∠DBE, FD ⊥ BD, and FE ⊥ BE, then DF = FE.

Why it works: triangles BDF and BEF share side BF, have congruent halves of the bisected angle, and both contain a right angle. By AAS they are congruent, so the corresponding sides DF and FE must match.

3. The converse runs the logic backwards

Theorem 6.4 goes bisector → equal distances. Theorem 6.5 goes equal distances → bisector. That second direction is how you prove something is a bisector.

Theorem 6.5 — Converse of the Angle Bisector Theorem
Words: If a point in the interior of an angle is equidistant from the sides of the angle, then it is on the bisector of the angle.
Symbols: If FD ⊥ BD, FE ⊥ BE, and DF = FE, then ray BF bisects ∠DBE.
Watch out! The converse needs three conditions, not one: the point must be interior to the angle, and both segments must be perpendicular to the sides. Two equal slanted segments prove nothing at all.

4. The incenter

A triangle has three angles, so it has three angle bisectors. They are always concurrent — all three cross at one single point, and that point sits inside the triangle every time.

Theorem 6.6 — Incenter Theorem
Words: The angle bisectors of a triangle intersect at a point called the incenter that is equidistant from the sides of the triangle.
Symbols: If P is the incenter of △ABC, then PD = PE = PF, where D, E, F are the feet of the perpendiculars to the three sides.

Because the incenter is the same perpendicular distance from all three sides, a circle centred there with that radius touches all three sides exactly once — the inscribed circle. That is the picture worth memorising.

Study Tip: Incenter and circumcenter are easy to swap. The incenter comes from angle bisectors and is equidistant from the sides. The circumcenter comes from perpendicular bisectors and is equidistant from the vertices.

5. The three things to memorise

1
On the bisector ⇒ equal perpendicular distances to the two sides. Set the two expressions equal and solve.
2
Equal perpendicular distances from an interior point ⇒ that point is on the bisector, so the two half-angles are congruent.
3
Incenter ⇒ each vertex angle is twice its half-angle, and the three whole angles still add to 180°.
Read the diagram and find every right-angle square. Only those segments are distances.

Ray RT is the angle bisector of ∠QRS. QT ⊥ RQ, ST ⊥ RS, QT = 4x + 8 and TS = 9x − 7. Find QT.
1QT = STAngle Bisector Theorem
24x + 8 = 9x − 7Substitution
3−5x = −15Simplify
4x = 3Simplify
5QT = 4(3) + 8Substitute x = 3
QT = 20
Check: TS = 9(3) − 7 = 27 − 7 = 20. Both distances are 20, exactly as the theorem promises. ✓
Trap: the question asked for QT, not x. Stopping at x = 3 loses the mark.

SP = 17

P is the incenter of △XYZ. Ray XP bisects ∠LXK, ray YP bisects ∠KYJ, and ray ZP bisects ∠LZJ. If m∠PXK = 24.6° and m∠PYJ = 25.1°, find m∠LZP.
1m∠LXK = 2 · m∠PXK = 2(24.6) = 49.2Definition of angle bisector
2m∠KYJ = 2 · m∠PYJ = 2(25.1) = 50.2Definition of angle bisector
3m∠LXK + m∠KYJ + m∠LZJ = 180Triangle Angle-Sum Theorem

A charging station must be equidistant from the three roads joining towns A(0, 0), B(2, 7) and C(9, 1). Find the approximate location of the station.
1Distance to a road = perpendicular distanceDefinition of distance to a line
2The point equidistant from all three sides is the incenterIncenter Theorem

Practice 4

I is the incenter of △DEF. If m∠IDE = 21° and m∠IEF = 32°, find m∠DFE.
1m∠D = 2(21) = 42DI bisects ∠D
2m∠E = 2(32) = 64EI bisects ∠E
3m∠F = 180 − 42 − 64Triangle Angle-Sum Theorem

Practice 5

I is the incenter of △ABC and m∠BIC = 130°. Find m∠A.
1m∠IBC + m∠ICB = 180 − 130 = 50Triangle Angle-Sum in △BIC
2½m∠B + ½m∠C = 50BI and CI are bisectors
3m∠B + m∠C = 100Multiply by 2
4m∠A = 180 − 100Triangle Angle-Sum Theorem
m∠A = 80°
Shortcut: for any incenter, m∠BIC = 90 + ½m∠A. Here 130 = 90 + ½(80). ✓

Practice 6

I is the incenter of △XYZ. The perpendicular distances to two of the sides are IA = 3a + 2 and IB = a + 10. Find the distance from I to the third side.
1IA = IBIncenter Theorem
23a + 2 = a + 10Substitution
32a = 8  →  a = 4Simplify
4IA = 3(4) + 2 = 14Substitution
The distance to the third side is also 14
Why: the incenter is equidistant from all three sides, so the third distance equals the other two. ✓

Common mistakes
  • Using two segments that are not perpendicular to the sides. No right-angle mark, no theorem.
  • Forgetting the point must be interior to the angle when you apply the converse.
  • Solving for x and stopping, when the question asked for a length or an angle measure.
  • Treating a given half-angle as the whole vertex angle in the Triangle Angle-Sum Theorem — double it first.
  • Confusing incenter (bisectors, equidistant from sides) with circumcenter (perpendicular bisectors, equidistant from vertices).
  • Assuming equal segments from an interior point to the two sides always means a bisector — only when they are perpendicular.
