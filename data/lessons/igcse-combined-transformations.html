Do one transformation, then another — and the whole journey can always be replaced by a single transformation. That single one is what the exam asks for. Build any pair below and the lab names the equivalent transformation for you, so you can test the two results that carry most of the marks: parallel mirrors give a translation of $2d$, and crossing mirrors give a rotation of $2\theta$.

◎ What you will be able to do

  • Carry out a sequence of transformations in the order given.
  • Replace a sequence with the single transformation that has the same effect.
  • Use the parallel-mirror rule ($2d$) and the intersecting-mirror rule ($2\theta$).
  • Combine two translations, two rotations or two enlargements about a common centre.
  • Write down the inverse of a transformation, and of a whole sequence.
  • Set out reasoning that earns the "show that" and "justify" marks.

1 One transformation after another Interactive

A question says something like "Shape A is reflected in the $y$-axis. The image is then rotated $90°$ clockwise about the origin." You carry them out strictly in the order written, using the image of one as the object of the next.

✎
The reliable method. Track the coordinates of every vertex through the sequence, one transformation at a time, in a small table. It is faster than redrawing and far harder to get wrong.
!
Order matters. Reflecting then translating is almost never the same as translating then reflecting. Never swap the order for convenience.
The one big idea
any sequence of reflections, rotations and translations can be replaced by a single transformation

Your job in the exam is usually to find that single transformation and describe it fully.

Lab 1 — the sequence machine

Set two transformations. A is the object, B the halfway image and C the finish; the panel gives the combined coordinate rule and names the single transformation that replaces the pair. Switch to the second tab to test whether the order matters.

2 Two reflections — the two results worth memorising Interactive

Parallel mirrors
Reflecting in two parallel lines a distance $d$ apart gives a translation of $2d$, perpendicular to the mirrors, from the first towards the second.
Intersecting mirrors
Reflecting in two lines that cross at an angle $\theta$ gives a rotation of $2\theta$ about the point where they cross.

The perpendicular case ties the two together: mirrors at $\theta=90°$ give a rotation of $180°$, which is why reflecting in the $x$-axis and then the $y$-axis is a half turn about the origin:

$(x,y)\rightarrow(x,-y)\rightarrow(-x,-y)$  — a rotation of $180°$ about $(0,0)$
Lab 2 — two mirrors

Slide the mirrors and read off the result. A is the object, B the first image and C the second. Watch the translation double the gap, and the rotation double the angle.

!
Swapping the order of two parallel mirrors reverses the translation: $x=4$ then $x=1$ gives $6$ units to the left, not the right.

3 Combining two of the same kind

CombinationSingle equivalent transformation
Translation by $\mathbf{u}$, then by $\mathbf{v}$Translation by $\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v}$ — just add the column vectors
Rotation of $a°$ then $b°$, same centreRotation of $(a+b)°$ about that centre (clockwise counts as negative)
Enlargement $k_1$ then $k_2$, same centreEnlargement of scale factor $k_1k_2$ about that centre
Reflection in a line, then the same lineThe identity — every point is back where it started
!
Only when the centres match. Two rotations about different centres still combine into a single rotation through the sum of the angles, but the centre moves and has to be found from the coordinates.
✎
Angles add. $90°$ clockwise followed by $180°$ about the same centre is $-90°+180°=+90°$, i.e. $90°$ anticlockwise.

4 Finding the single equivalent transformation

  • Apply the sequence to every vertex and write down the final coordinates.
  • Compare the finish with the original: same size? same way up? mirrored? turned?
  • Use the identification routine — enlargement, translation, reflection or rotation.
  • Find the missing details (vector, mirror line, centre, angle) from the coordinates.
  • Check every vertex against your proposed transformation before writing the answer.
✎
Count the reflections. An even number leaves the shape the right way round, so the answer is a translation or a rotation. An odd number leaves it mirrored, so the answer is a reflection. Lab 1 shows this count for you.

5 Inverse transformations Interactive

The inverse undoes a transformation and returns every point to its starting position.

TransformationIts inverse
Translation by $\mathbf{v}$Translation by $-\mathbf{v}$
Rotation of $\theta$ clockwise about $C$Rotation of $\theta$ anticlockwise about the same $C$
Reflection in a line $L$Reflection in the same line $L$ — it is its own inverse
Enlargement, scale factor $k$, centre $C$Enlargement, scale factor $\frac1k$, centre the same $C$
✎
Reversing a sequence. To undo "P then Q" you undo Q first, then P — like taking off your shoes before your socks.
Lab 3 — undo it

Pick a transformation, or a sequence of two, and the lab writes down the inverse in the right order — then checks that everything really does return to the start.

6 Justifying a geometrical solution

When a question says show that, explain why or justify, the marks are for the reasoning, not the final statement. A complete justification has three parts.

  • State the evidence. The coordinates, lengths or angles you are relying on.
  • State the property. "Reflection preserves length", "the two triangles are congruent", "the perpendicular bisectors of matching vertices meet at the centre of rotation".
  • State the conclusion, in the language of the question.
✎
Model wording. "Each side of the image has the same length as the matching side of the object, so the transformation is congruent; the image is turned rather than mirrored, so it is a rotation. The perpendicular bisectors of $(1,1)$–$(5,1)$ and $(1,2)$–$(4,1)$ meet at $(3,3)$, so the centre of rotation is $(3,3)$."
!
"It looks like a rotation" and "I used tracing paper" earn no reasoning marks. Refer to lengths, coordinates or named properties.

✍️ Worked examples

Example 1
Two reflections in parallel lines
 ⌄

A shape is reflected in $x=2$, and the image is then reflected in $x=7$. Describe fully the single equivalent transformation.

1

The mirrors are parallel, so the result is a translation perpendicular to them.

2

The gap is $7-2=5$.

3

The translation is $2\times5=10$, from the first mirror towards the second — to the right.

Answer. Translation by $\begin{pmatrix}10\\0\end{pmatrix}$
✎
Algebraic check. $(x,y)\rightarrow(4-x,\,y)\rightarrow\big(14-(4-x),\,y\big)=(x+10,\,y)$. ✓
Example 2
Two reflections in perpendicular lines
 ⌄

$P(4,-3)$ is reflected in the $x$-axis, and the image is then reflected in the $y$-axis. Find the final point and describe the single equivalent transformation.

1

Reflection in the $x$-axis: $(4,-3)\rightarrow(4,3)$.

2

Reflection in the $y$-axis: $(4,3)\rightarrow(-4,3)$.

3

Overall the rule is $(x,y)\rightarrow(-x,-y)$ — a half turn.

Answer. $(-4,3)$; the single transformation is a rotation of $180°$ about the origin
✎
Why. The mirrors meet at $90°$, and two reflections in lines at $\theta$ give a rotation of $2\theta=180°$.
Example 3
Combining rotations about the same centre
 ⌄

$R$ is a rotation of $120°$ clockwise about $(1,2)$; $S$ is a rotation of $210°$ anticlockwise about $(1,2)$. Describe fully "$R$ followed by $S$".

1

Take anticlockwise as positive: $R=-120°$ and $S=+210°$.

2

Same centre, so the angles add: $-120+210=+90$.

Answer. Rotation of $90°$ anticlockwise about $(1,2)$
!
This shortcut works only because both rotations share a centre. With different centres you must work through the coordinates.
Example 4
A rotation followed by a reflection
 ⌄

Triangle A has vertices $(2,1)$, $(5,1)$, $(2,3)$. It is rotated $90°$ anticlockwise about the origin to give B, and B is reflected in the $x$-axis to give C. Describe fully the single transformation mapping A onto C.

1

Rotation. $(x,y)\rightarrow(-y,x)$ gives B: $(-1,2)$, $(-1,5)$, $(-3,2)$.

2

Reflection in the $x$-axis. $(x,y)\rightarrow(x,-y)$ gives C: $(-1,-2)$, $(-1,-5)$, $(-3,-2)$.

3

Combine the rules. $(x,y)\rightarrow(-y,x)\rightarrow(-y,-x)$.

4

The rule $(x,y)\rightarrow(-y,-x)$ is a reflection in $y=-x$.

Answer. Reflection in the line $y=-x$
✎
The sequence contained exactly one reflection — an odd number — so the single transformation had to be a reflection. Reproduce it in Lab 1.
Example 5
A reflection followed by a translation
 ⌄

A shape is reflected in the $y$-axis, and the image is translated by $\begin{pmatrix}6\\0\end{pmatrix}$. Describe fully the single equivalent transformation.

1

Reflection in the $y$-axis: $(x,y)\rightarrow(-x,y)$.

2

Translation $6$ to the right: $(-x,y)\rightarrow(6-x,\,y)$.

3

Compare with reflection in $x=a$, which is $(x,y)\rightarrow(2a-x,\,y)$.

4

So $2a=6$, giving $a=3$.

Answer. Reflection in the line $x=3$
✎
Sense check. $(1,5)$ maps to $(5,5)$, and $3$ is exactly halfway between $1$ and $5$. ✓
Example 6
Undoing a sequence
 ⌄

$T$ is a translation by $\begin{pmatrix}3\\-2\end{pmatrix}$ and $E$ is an enlargement of scale factor $4$, centre $(0,0)$. Describe the transformations that undo "$T$ followed by $E$".

1

To reverse a sequence, undo the last step first — so start with $E$.

2

$E^{-1}$ is an enlargement of scale factor $\frac14$, centre $(0,0)$.

3

$T^{-1}$ is a translation by $\begin{pmatrix}-3\\2\end{pmatrix}$.

Answer. Enlarge by $\frac14$ about the origin, then translate by $\begin{pmatrix}-3\\2\end{pmatrix}$
!
Doing the inverses in the original order — translate, then shrink — does not return the shape to its start, because the enlargement would scale the translation too. Test it in Lab 3.

! Common mistakes to avoid

  • Doing the transformations in the wrong order. The first named one acts on the original shape; the second acts on its image.
  • Answering with two transformations. A sequence of congruent transformations always reduces to one — find it.
  • Adding rotation angles when the centres differ. The shortcut needs a common centre.
  • Forgetting to double the distance between parallel mirrors — the translation is $2d$, not $d$.
  • Forgetting to double the angle between intersecting mirrors — the rotation is $2\theta$, not $\theta$.
  • Reversing a sequence in the same order. To undo "P then Q" apply $Q^{-1}$ first, then $P^{-1}$.
  • Giving no reasoning in a "show that" question. Write both images down and state explicitly that they differ.
  • Ignoring the reflection count. Even means translation or rotation; odd means reflection. Use it as a check.
