Core Concepts

1. Three lengths do not automatically make a triangle

Hand someone three sticks and they may or may not be able to close them into a triangle. If two of the sticks are far too short, they simply cannot reach each other across the long one — the shape never closes. In order for three segments to form a triangle, a special relationship must exist among their lengths.

Theorem 6.11 · Triangle Inequality Theorem
The sum of the lengths of any two sides of a triangle must be greater than the length of the third side.

For a triangle with sides a, b, and c, all three of these statements must be true at the same time:

a + b > c
a + c > b
b + c > a

If even one of the three fails, no triangle exists.

2. Testing a set of three lengths

Write the three lengths down.
Form all three pairs and add each pair.
Compare each sum to the remaining (third) length.
If every sum is strictly greater than the third length, a triangle is possible. If any sum is less than or equal to the third length, it is impossible.
Study Tip — the one-check shortcut: order the lengths so that a ≤ b ≤ c. Then a + c > b and b + c > a are automatically true, because you are adding the longest side to something positive. Only a + b > c — the two shortest against the longest — can ever fail, so that is the only inequality you truly have to check.
Watch Out! The inequality is strict. If a + b = c exactly, the two short sides lie flat along the long one and the "triangle" collapses to a segment with zero height. Equality means no triangle. Example: 3, 5, 8 fails because 3 + 5 = 8, not > 8.

3. Finding the range for a missing third side

When the lengths of two sides of a triangle are known, the third side can be any length in a range of values — not just one value. Let the known sides be p and q and let x be the unknown third side. The three inequalities give

p + q > x  →  x < p + q
p + x > q  →  x > q − p
q + x > p  →  x > p − q

The last two collapse into one statement: x must exceed the positive difference of the two known sides. So the answer is always a compound inequality:

| p − q |  <  x  <  p + q
Study Tip: the compound inequality 4 < x < 12 is read "x is between 4 and 12." Both endpoints are excluded, so the least possible whole-number value is 5 and the greatest is 11.

4. Key facts to memorise

1
Two sides added must be strictly greater than the third — "greater than or equal to" is not good enough.
2
Check the two smallest against the largest. That single test decides everything.
3
Missing third side: difference < x < sum of the two known sides.
4
The range is open at both ends: <, never ≤.
5
Three points are the vertices of a triangle only if they are not collinear. For collinear points the two shorter distances add up to exactly the longest one.

5. Why the theorem is true (the proof idea)

To prove AC + BC > AB in △ABC, extend BC past C to a point D so that CD ≅ AC. Then △ACD is isosceles, so ∠CAD ≅ ∠D. Since ∠BAD is larger than ∠CAD, it is larger than ∠D, and by the Angle–Side relationship of Lesson 6-5 the side opposite the larger angle is longer: BD > AB. But BD = BC + CD = BC + AC. Therefore AC + BC > AB.

Think About It! This is Exercise 22 in the textbook — the auxiliary segment CD is the whole trick.

6. Vocabulary

Triangle Inequality Theorem — the sum of any two side lengths of a triangle is greater than the third side length.
Compound inequality — two inequalities joined into one statement, such as 4 < x < 12.
Degenerate triangle — the flat "triangle" you get when a + b = c; it has zero area and is not a triangle.
Collinear points — points lying on one line; they can never be the vertices of a triangle.

Common mistakes to avoid:
  • Using ≥ instead of >. Equality gives a flat, degenerate figure — not a triangle.
  • Checking only one pair when the sides are not in order. The shortcut only works after you sort them.
  • Writing the range as p − q < x < p + q without taking the positive difference.
  • Giving one number for the third side. The answer is always a range.
  • Including the endpoints when asked for the least or greatest whole number: for 4 < x < 12 the answers are 5 and 11, not 4 and 12.
  • Forgetting to solve back for the variable when a side is written as an expression like x − 2.
  • Assuming three plotted points must form a triangle — collinear points do not.
