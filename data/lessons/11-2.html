Core Concepts

1. The four words you need before any formula

Every regular polygon fits perfectly inside one circle — each vertex touches the circle. That circle is what gives the polygon a center, a radius and an apothem.

Center of a regular polygon — the center of the circle circumscribed about the polygon.
Radius of a regular polygon — the radius of the circumscribed circle: a segment from the center to a vertex.
Apothem — a perpendicular segment between the center of the polygon and a side of the polygon.
Central angle — an angle with its vertex at the center whose sides pass through two consecutive vertices.
Composite figure — a figure that can be separated into basic regions (triangles, rectangles, trapezoids, circles, regular polygons).
Decomposing into triangles — splitting an n-gon into n congruent isosceles triangles by drawing all n radii.
Watch Out! The radius goes to a corner; the apothem goes to the middle of a side. They are never the same length — the apothem is always shorter, because the perpendicular distance to a line is the shortest distance.

2. The central angle: 360/n

Drawing all n radii chops the full turn around the center into n equal pieces, and a full turn is 360°. So each slice gets the same share.

Central Angle of a Regular Polygon
The measure of each central angle of a regular n-gon is 360°/n.
Example: a regular octagon has 360°/8 = 45°; a regular pentagon has 360°/5 = 72°.
Study Tip: The apothem bisects the central angle (it is the altitude of an isosceles triangle drawn to the base). So the acute angle you actually use in trigonometry is 180°/n, not 360°/n.

3. Why the area formula works

Split the regular n-gon into n congruent isosceles triangles. Each triangle has base s (one side of the polygon) and height a (the apothem).

area of ONE triangle = ½bh = ½sa
area of ALL n triangles = n · ½sa = ½a(ns)
but ns = P, the perimeter  ∴  A = ½aP
Key Concept · Area of a Regular Polygon
The area A of a regular n-gon with side length s is one half the product of the apothem a and the perimeter P.
A = ½a(ns)   or   A = ½aP

4. Finding a missing apothem, radius or side

Drop the apothem to a side. You get a right triangle whose legs are a and s/2, whose hypotenuse is the radius r, and whose angle at the center is 180°/n.

tan(180°/n) = (s/2) / a  →  a = s / (2·tan(180°/n))
sin(180°/n) = (s/2) / r  →  r = s / (2·sin(180°/n))
cos(180°/n) = a / r  →  a = r·cos(180°/n)
Pythagorean check: r² = a² + (s/2)²
Find the central angle 360°/n, then halve it to get 180°/n.
Halve the side to get the leg s/2.
Use tangent (or a special right triangle) to get the apothem a.
Compute the perimeter P = ns.
Substitute into A = ½aP and round only at the end.
Study Tip: Two shortcuts worth memorising, both from 30-60-90 triangles:
equilateral triangle → A = (√3/4)s²  ·  regular hexagon → a = (√3/2)s, r = s, A = (3√3/2)s².

5. Composite figures

A composite figure is just several basic figures glued together. Break it apart, find each piece, then add (Area Addition Postulate) — or, when a piece has been cut out, find the big region and subtract the hole.

area of figure = area of piece₁ + area of piece₂ + …
area of figure = area of enclosing region − area of removed region
Study Tip · Drawing Figures: To solve area problems by subtracting, redraw the basic shapes that are being removed. Seeing the "hole" as its own triangle or rectangle makes the missing measures obvious.
1
Central angle of a regular n-gon = 360°/n. The apothem bisects it into two angles of 180°/n.
2
A = ½aP. Apothem × perimeter ÷ 2 — nothing else.
3
The apothem is perpendicular to a side and bisects it, so its foot is the midpoint of that side.
4
Radius > apothem, always. r² = a² + (s/2)².
5
Composite figure = add the parts, or subtract the hole. Never mix the two by accident.

Common mistakes to avoid
  • Using the full central angle 360°/n in the trig ratio instead of the half-angle 180°/n.
  • Using the whole side s as the opposite leg instead of s/2.
  • Substituting the side length for P in A = ½aP. P is the whole perimeter.
  • Confusing radius and apothem. a reaches the middle of a side; r reaches a vertex; r > a.
  • Assuming a = (√3/2)s for every polygon — that is a hexagon-only shortcut, so the statement is only sometimes true.
  • Rounding the apothem too early; carry full precision until the final line.
  • In a composite figure, adding a region that was actually cut out.
