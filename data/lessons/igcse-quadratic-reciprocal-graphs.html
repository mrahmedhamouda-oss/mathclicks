1 Quadratic graphs Interactive

Every quadratic gives a smooth, symmetrical curve called a parabola.

$y=ax^{2}+bx+c\qquad (a\neq 0)$
✎
Key rule. The sign of $a$ decides which way the curve opens. $a>0$ → opens upwards (∪), the turning point is a minimum. $a<0$ → opens downwards (∩), the turning point is a maximum. The size of $|a|$ controls how narrow it is.
Lab 1 — the parabola explorer

Move $a$, $b$ and $c$ and watch every feature update at once. Try to answer these as you go: which slider slides the curve up and down? which one moves the line of symmetry? what happens the moment the discriminant goes negative?

2 Drawing a quadratic from a table Interactive

  • Build the table. Substitute each $x$ value — work out $x^{2}$, then the $x$ term, then the constant, in separate rows.
  • Plot the points accurately, using the scales printed on the axes.
  • Join them with a smooth curve — freehand, never with a ruler, never as straight segments.
  • Extend the curve to the edges of the grid and label it with its equation.
!
Watch out. Squaring a negative gives a positive: $(-3)^{2}=9$, not $-9$. This is the single most common table error. Also the vertex often sits between two whole-number $x$-values, so the lowest plotted point may not be the true turning point.
Lab 2 — table trainer

Fill in every $y$-value, press Check, then press Plot my points to see which of your points really sit on the curve.

3 Roots, intercepts & turning points

y-intercept — where the curve crosses the $y$-axis. Put $x=0$, so it is always $(0,\,c)$: just read off the constant.
Roots (x-intercepts) — where $y=0$. These are the solutions of $ax^{2}+bx+c=0$. There can be 2, 1 (repeated) or none.
Line of symmetry — the vertical line $x=\dfrac{-b}{2a}$. If you know the two roots, it is exactly halfway between them.
Turning point (vertex) — substitute the line-of-symmetry $x$ back in to get $y$. Or complete the square: $y=a(x-h)^{2}+k$ has vertex $(h,k)$.

4 Reciprocal graphs Interactive

Curves of the form $y=\dfrac{a}{x}$ have two separate branches and are called a hyperbola.

  • $a>0$ → branches in the 1st and 3rd quadrants (top-right and bottom-left).
  • $a<0$ → branches in the 2nd and 4th quadrants (top-left and bottom-right).
  • The curve never touches either axis and never crosses from one branch to the other.
  • It has rotational symmetry of order 2 about the origin.
✎
Asymptote. A line the curve approaches ever more closely but never actually reaches. For $y=\dfrac{a}{x}$ they are $x=0$ (the y-axis) and $y=0$ (the x-axis). For $y=\dfrac{a}{x}+k$ the whole curve slides up by $k$, so the horizontal asymptote becomes $y=k$.
Lab 4 — reciprocal explorer

Change $a$ to flip the branches between quadrants, and change $k$ to slide the curve — watch the horizontal asymptote and the table of values move with it. Look at what happens to $y$ as $x$ gets close to $0$.

!
Watch out. Never join the two branches through $x=0$. The function is undefined there — leave a clear gap.

5 Solving quadratics from a graph Interactive

The solutions are always the $x$-coordinates of intersection points.

  • Case A — $ax^{2}+bx+c=0$. Read off where the curve cuts the x-axis.
  • Case B — $ax^{2}+bx+c=k$. Draw the horizontal line $y=k$ and read the $x$-coordinates of the crossings.
  • Case C — the equation doesn't match your curve. Rearrange so that one side is exactly the equation of your drawn curve. Whatever is left on the other side is the line you must draw.
Case C worked
You drew $y=x^{2}-2x-3$ and must solve $x^{2}-3x-1=0$.
Add $x$ and add $2$ to both sides: $x^{2}-2x-3=x+2$.
So draw the line $\mathbf{y=x+2}$ and read the x-coordinates of the intersections.
Lab 5 — slide the line

The curve is fixed at $y=x^{2}-2x-3$. Move the line and watch the solution count change from 2 → 1 (tangent) → 0. Use the presets to see Case A, Case B and Case C.

!
Watch out. The answer is the $x$-coordinate of the intersection — not the $y$-coordinate, and not the coordinate pair. Give graph readings to 1 d.p. unless told otherwise, and always give both solutions.

Example 1
Draw and interpret a quadratic
 ⌄

Complete a table of values for $y=x^{2}-2x-3$ for $-2\le x\le 4$, draw the graph, and state the roots, the y-intercept, the line of symmetry and the turning point.

x−2−101234
$x^2$41014916

$-3$−3−3−3−3−3−3−3
y50−3−4−305

Roots: the curve meets the x-axis at $x=-1$ and $x=3$. Check by factorising: $x^{2}-2x-3=(x+1)(x-3)=0$. ✔

y-intercept: the constant term, so $(0,-3)$.

Line of symmetry: $x=\dfrac{-b}{2a}=\dfrac{2}{2}=1$ — also the midpoint of $-1$ and $3$.

Turning point: put $x=1$ in: $y=1-2-3=-4$.

Answer. Roots $x=-1,\ x=3$ · y-intercept $(0,-3)$ · line of symmetry $x=1$ · minimum turning point $(1,-4)$.
Example 2
Turning point by completing the square
 ⌄

Find the coordinates of the turning point of $y=x^{2}-6x+5$ and say whether it is a maximum or a minimum.

1

Halve the coefficient of $x$: $-6\div 2=-3$.

2

$(x-3)^{2}$ expands to $x^{2}-6x+9$ — that is $9$ too much, so subtract $9$.

3

$y=(x-3)^{2}-9+5=(x-3)^{2}-4$.

4

Compare with $y=a(x-h)^{2}+k$: here $h=3$, $k=-4$. Since $a=1>0$ the parabola opens upwards.

Answer. Minimum turning point at $(3,-4)$. Check: $x=\frac{-b}{2a}=\frac{6}{2}=3$, then $y=9-18+5=-4$. ✔
!
The bracket is $(x-3)^{2}$ but the x-coordinate is $+3$ — the sign flips.

Example 4
Solving a quadratic from a graph
 ⌄

Using the graph of $y=x^{2}-2x-3$, solve (a) $x^{2}-2x-3=0$ and (b) $x^{2}-2x-3=2$.

(a) Read where the curve meets the x-axis (the line $y=0$): $x=-1$ or $x=3$.

(b) Draw the horizontal line $y=2$ and read the x-coordinates of the two crossings: $x\approx-1.4$ or $x\approx3.4$. Set the preset y = 2 in Lab 5 to see it.

Check. Algebraically $x^{2}-2x-5=0$, so $x=\dfrac{2\pm\sqrt{24}}{2}=1\pm\sqrt{6}$, i.e. $x=3.449\ldots$ or $-1.449\ldots$ ✔ — the 1 d.p. graph readings agree.
Example 5
Which extra line should you draw?
 ⌄

The curve $y=x^{2}-2x-3$ is drawn. Which straight line should be drawn to solve $x^{2}-3x-1=0$?

1

Write the equation to solve: $x^{2}-3x-1=0$.

2

Make the left-hand side match the drawn curve — add $x$ and add $2$ to both sides: $x^{2}-2x-3=x+2$.

3

The left side is the curve; the right side is the line to draw.

Answer. Draw $y=x+2$ (preset 3 in Lab 5). Exact solutions $x=\dfrac{3\pm\sqrt{13}}{2}$, i.e. $x\approx3.30$ or $x\approx-0.30$.
Example 6
Linear, quadratic or neither?
 ⌄

Classify each set: A $(1,5)(2,8)(3,11)(4,14)$ · B $(1,2)(2,5)(3,10)(4,17)$ · C $(1,12)(2,6)(3,4)(4,3)$.

A: first differences $+3,+3,+3$ → constant → linear, $y=3x+2$.

B: first differences $+3,+5,+7$; second differences $+2,+2$ → constant → quadratic, $y=x^{2}+1$.

C: neither set of differences is constant, but $x\times y=12$ every time → reciprocal, $y=\dfrac{12}{x}$.

Answer. A linear · B quadratic · C reciprocal. Type each set into Lab 6 to see the differences appear.

! Common mistakes to avoid

  • Joining points with straight lines. Curves are drawn freehand and smooth — no rulers, no sharp corner at the vertex.
  • Sign errors when squaring negatives. $(-4)^{2}=16$. Set the table out in separate rows.
  • Joining the two branches of a reciprocal graph. There is no point at $x=0$ — leave the gap.
  • Letting a curve touch its asymptote. It gets closer and closer, but never meets it.
  • Giving the y-coordinate as the solution. Solutions of an equation are always $x$-values.
  • Assuming the lowest table value is the turning point. The vertex often lies between two plotted x-values — use $x=\frac{-b}{2a}$.
