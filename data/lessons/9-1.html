Core Concepts

1. What a rational expression is

A rational expression is nothing more exotic than a fraction whose top and bottom are polynomials instead of plain numbers. If you can write it as polynomial ÷ polynomial, it is rational.

Definition. A rational expression is a ratio of two polynomial expressions:
  P(x) / Q(x)   where P and Q are polynomials and Q ≠ 0.
rational expression — a ratio of two polynomial expressions, e.g. (x² − 9)/(x + 4).
complex fraction — a rational expression whose numerator and/or denominator is itself a rational expression.

Not rational expressions: (x + 1)/√(x + 3) (the bottom is not a polynomial), or anything with a variable exponent. But (x² + 1)/3 is rational — a constant is a degree-0 polynomial.

2. Simplifying works exactly like reducing a numerical fraction

To reduce 35/40 you divide top and bottom by the GCF. Rational expressions use the identical process — you just have to factor first to see what the GCF is.

Numbers

35/40
= (5 · 7)/(5 · 8)
= 7/8     GCF = 5

Expressions

(x² + 7x + 10)/(x² − x − 6)
= [(x + 5)(x + 2)]/[(x − 3)(x + 2)]
= (x + 5)/(x − 3)   GCF = x + 2
Factor the numerator completely (GCF first, then trinomial / difference of squares).
Factor the denominator completely the same way.
Before cancelling, read off the zeros of the ORIGINAL denominator — those are your excluded values.
Eliminate every factor that appears in both top and bottom (each pair becomes 1).
Write what is left. That is simplest form.
You may only cancel FACTORS, never terms. In (x + 5)/(x + 3) you cannot cross out the x's — they are terms joined by +, not factors joined by ·. Cancelling is really dividing by 1, and (x+5)/(x+3) has no common factor to divide by.

3. Excluded values — the trap of this whole lesson

Division by zero is undefined, so any value of the variable that makes a denominator zero must be thrown out. The subtlety: cancelling a factor hides a restriction, but it does not remove it.

!
Excluded-Value Rule. State restrictions from the ORIGINAL denominator, in factored form, BEFORE you cancel anything. Set every factor of that original denominator equal to 0 and solve. Every one of those values stays excluded, even if the factor disappears during simplification.
If the original expression is P/Q and Q = a₁a₂a₃… in factored form,
then by the Zero Product Property the expression is undefined when
  a₁ = 0 or a₂ = 0 or a₃ = 0 …

Right — restrictions from the original

(x² − 4)/(x² − x − 6)
= [(x−2)(x+2)]/[(x−3)(x+2)]
x ≠ 3 and x ≠ −2
= (x − 2)/(x − 3)

Wrong — restrictions read after cancelling

… = (x − 2)/(x − 3)
x ≠ 3 only  ✗
The value x = −2 has been lost. The
original expression is 0/0 there.
Study Tip — Checking: because you are only simplifying, the original and the answer must agree for every allowed value of x. Pick a convenient number that is not excluded, substitute into both, and confirm you get the same value.

4. Multiplying rational expressions

Multiply straight across — numerator times numerator, denominator times denominator. In practice you factor first and cancel before multiplying, so the numbers never get large.

Key Concept · Multiplying Rational Expressions
Words: To multiply rational expressions, multiply the numerators and multiply the denominators.
Symbols: For all rational expressions a/b and c/d with b ≠ 0 and d ≠ 0,
(a/b) · (c/d) = ac/bd

5. Dividing rational expressions

Dividing by a fraction is multiplying by its reciprocal — flip the divisor, then follow the multiplication rule.

Key Concept · Dividing Rational Expressions
Words: To divide rational expressions, multiply the dividend by the reciprocal of the divisor.
Symbols: For all rational expressions a/b and c/d with b ≠ 0, c ≠ 0, and d ≠ 0,
(a/b) ÷ (c/d) = (a/b) · (d/c) = ad/bc
Why division needs one extra restriction. Multiplication requires only b ≠ 0 and d ≠ 0. Division also requires c ≠ 0, because c moves down into the denominator when you flip the divisor — and a divisor of 0 is meaningless in the first place.

6. Factoring out −1 — when two factors are opposites

Expressions like 6x − 5y and 5y − 6x look different but are opposites. Pull a −1 out of one of them and a common factor appears.

a − b = −1(b − a)   ∴   (a − b)/(b − a) = −1

So (6x − 5y) = −1(5y − 6x), and the pair cancels to leave a factor of −1 out front. It does not matter whether you factor the −1 out of the numerator or the denominator — the results are equivalent, since the single minus sign can be written in front of the whole fraction.

7. Complex fractions

A complex fraction is a fraction stacked on a fraction. The main fraction bar simply means “divide.” Rewrite it as a division problem and you are back to rule 5.

  (a/b)
−−−−  =  (a/b) ÷ (c/d) = (a/b) · (d/c) = ad/bc
  (c/d)
★
Three memorised moves: (1) factor everything, (2) flip only the divisor — never the dividend, (3) restrictions come from the original bottoms, including the numerator of the divisor once it is flipped.

★
The one rule to carry out of this lesson: restrictions come from the ORIGINAL denominator in factored form, stated before any cancelling. Simplifying changes the way an expression looks — it never changes which values are illegal.
Common mistakes to avoid:
  • Cancelling terms instead of factors: (x + 5)/(x + 3) ≠ 5/3.
  • Reading restrictions off the simplified expression — cancelled factors still exclude values.
  • Flipping the dividend instead of the divisor when dividing.
  • Forgetting that dividing needs c ≠ 0 as well as b ≠ 0 and d ≠ 0.
  • Treating a² − b² as (a − b)².
  • Missing that a − b and b − a are opposites, so they cancel to −1 — not to 1.
  • Multiplying everything out before factoring — you lose the very factors you need to cancel.
