Core Concepts

1. What a rectangle actually is

A rectangle is not a brand-new shape — it is a parallelogram that has been forced to stand up straight. Every property a parallelogram owns, a rectangle inherits, and then it adds one more of its own.

rectangle — a parallelogram with four right angles.
Consequence — every rectangle is a parallelogram, but a parallelogram need not be a rectangle.

From the definition alone you already know all of the following:

  • All four angles are right angles.
  • Opposite sides are parallel and congruent.
  • Opposite angles are congruent.
  • Consecutive angles are supplementary.
  • Diagonals bisect each other.

In addition, the diagonals of a rectangle are congruent. That last fact is the one that separates rectangles from ordinary parallelograms.

2. The two theorems of this lesson

Theorem 7.13 · Diagonals of a Rectangle
If a parallelogram is a rectangle, then its diagonals are congruent.
In symbols: if ▭ABCD is a rectangle, then AC ≅ BD.

Why it works: in a rectangle, △ABC and △BAD share leg AB, have BC ≅ AD (opposite sides of a parallelogram) and both contain a right angle. By SAS the triangles are congruent, so their hypotenuses AC and BD are congruent.

Theorem 7.14 · Converse
If the diagonals of a parallelogram are congruent, then the parallelogram is a rectangle.
Read the converse carefully. Theorem 7.14 only applies to a parallelogram. Congruent diagonals inside a random quadrilateral prove nothing — an isosceles trapezoid also has congruent diagonals and is definitely not a rectangle.

3. Half-diagonals: the four equal radii

Diagonals of a rectangle are congruent and they bisect each other. Put those two facts together and every one of the four half-diagonals has the same length.

If P is the intersection of diagonals AC and BD:
AP = PC = BP = PD = ½AC = ½BD

So the four triangles formed by the diagonals are all isosceles, and the vertices of a rectangle all lie the same distance from P.

1
A whole diagonal is twice a half-diagonal: AC = 2·AP.
2
The two whole diagonals are equal: AC = BD.
3
A diagonal splits a corner's right angle into two angles that add to 90°.

4. Angles created by a diagonal

Draw diagonal AC in rectangle ABCD. At vertex A the 90° corner ∠DAB is cut into ∠DAC and ∠CAB, so those two angles are complementary. The same happens at every vertex.

m∠DAC + m∠CAB = 90°   m∠BAC + m∠ACB = 90°

The second equation is the one that shows up most often: in right triangle ABC the right angle is at B, so the two acute angles must add to 90°. Alternate interior angles give you a bonus: ∠ACD ≅ ∠BAC, because AB ∥ DC.

Watch out! Two angles formed at the same vertex by a diagonal are complementary (sum 90°). Two angles formed at opposite ends of the same diagonal are congruent, not complementary. Identify which vertex each angle lives at before you write an equation.

5. Proving a parallelogram is a rectangle

Show the quadrilateral is a parallelogram first — both pairs of opposite sides congruent, or both pairs parallel, or diagonals bisecting each other.
Show the diagonals are congruent.
Conclude “rectangle” by Theorem 7.14.

There is a shortcut that skips step 2 entirely:

Study Tip · Right Angles. Recall from Theorem 7.6 that if a parallelogram has one right angle, then it has four right angles. One 90° corner in a parallelogram is already enough to call it a rectangle.
Study Tip · Rectangles and Parallelograms. All rectangles are parallelograms, but all parallelograms are not necessarily rectangles.

6. Rectangles on the coordinate plane

Given four vertices, you can prove “rectangle” with nothing but algebra.

Distance Formula:  d = √[(x2 − x1)2 + (y2 − y1)2]
Slope Formula:  m = (y2 − y1) / (x2 − x1)

Distance-Formula route

  • Both pairs of opposite sides congruent → parallelogram.
  • Both diagonals congruent → rectangle.

Slope-Formula route

  • Opposite sides have equal slopes → parallelogram.
  • Consecutive sides have slopes whose product is −1 → right angle → rectangle.
Common error. Showing only that the four sides are congruent proves a rhombus, not a rectangle. Showing only that opposite sides are congruent proves a parallelogram. You must add the diagonal or the slope check.

7. Think About It

The two diagonals of a rectangle create four right triangles — △ABC, △BCD, △CDA and △DAB — each with legs equal to the two sides of the rectangle and hypotenuse equal to a diagonal. All four are congruent, so the number of congruent pairs you can list is C(4, 2) = 6.

Common mistakes to avoid
  • Using Theorem 7.14 without first establishing that the figure is a parallelogram.
  • Setting two angles at opposite ends of a diagonal equal to 90°. They are congruent, not complementary.
  • Setting a half-diagonal equal to a whole diagonal — remember the factor of 2.
  • Concluding “rectangle” from four congruent sides. That is a rhombus.
  • Forgetting that the diagonals of a rectangle are not perpendicular and do not bisect the corner angles (unless the rectangle is a square).
  • Rejecting no solutions in an algebra problem — a negative length is not acceptable.
