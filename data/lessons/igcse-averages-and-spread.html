An average tells you where the data sits; a measure of spread tells you how tightly it sits there. Neither is any use alone — and the marks in a comparison question come from quoting one of each, in context. Type your own data into the labs below and watch what a single outlier does to the mean and to the range.

◎ What you will be able to do

  • Classify data as qualitative or quantitative, discrete or continuous.
  • Organise data into frequency tables, grouped tables and stem-and-leaf diagrams.
  • Find the mean, median, mode and range of a list.
  • Find the mean, median and mode from a frequency table.
  • Calculate an estimate of the mean from grouped data, and give the modal class.
  • Find quartiles and the interquartile range.
  • Compare two datasets the way the mark scheme wants.

Bias. A good sample is random — every member of the population has an equal chance of being chosen — and large enough. Asking only your friends, only people in one place, or only those willing to reply gives a biased sample, and no amount of careful calculation afterwards can fix it.

2 Organising data

A tally and frequency table turns a messy list into something you can calculate with. When data is continuous, or there are too many different values, group it into classes that do not overlap and cover every possible value.

4 Averages from a frequency table Interactive

mean $=\dfrac{\Sigma fx}{\Sigma f}$

Here $x$ is the value, $f$ its frequency, $\Sigma f$ the total number of items and $\Sigma fx$ the total of all the values.

!
Mode versus frequency. The mode is the value with the highest frequency, not the frequency itself. Writing the frequency is a guaranteed lost mark.
Grouped data — an estimate of the mean

Once data is grouped the individual values are lost, so each class is represented by its midpoint. That is why the answer is only an estimate.

estimated mean $=\dfrac{\Sigma(f\times\text{midpoint})}{\Sigma f}$
What is askedWhat you give
Modal classThe class with the highest frequency
Class containing the medianCount to the middle value using cumulative frequency
Lab 2 — the frequency-table calculator

Switch between discrete values and grouped classes. The table builds $f\times x$ and the running total for you, and the panel names the mode or the modal class.

!
Dividing by the number of classes instead of $\Sigma f$ is a frequent error. You always divide by the number of items.

5 Quartiles and the interquartile range

ValueMeaningHow to find it from an ordered list
$Q_1$A quarter of the way throughThe median of the lower half
$Q_2$HalfwayThe middle value
$Q_3$Three quarters of the way throughThe median of the upper half
IQRSpread of the middle half$Q_3-Q_1$
✎
Why the IQR beats the range. The range uses only the two most extreme values, so a single outlier can double it. The IQR describes the middle $50\%$ and ignores the extremes.
!
With an odd number of values, leave the median itself out of both halves before finding the quartiles.

✍️ Worked examples

Example 1
The four measures from a list
 ⌄

Find the mean, median, mode and range of $4,\ 7,\ 3,\ 7,\ 9,\ 2,\ 7,\ 5$.

1

Order first: $2,\ 3,\ 4,\ 5,\ 7,\ 7,\ 7,\ 9$.

2

Mean: total $=44$ and there are $8$ values, so $44\div8=5.5$.

3

Median: $n=8$, so position $\frac{8+1}{2}=4.5$ — average the 4th and 5th: $\frac{5+7}{2}=6$.

4

Mode: $7$ appears three times. Range: $9-2=7$.

Answer. Mean $5.5$ · median $6$ · mode $7$ · range $7$
Example 2
Working backwards from a mean
 ⌄

The mean of six numbers is $14$. Five of them are $10$, $12$, $15$, $17$ and $11$. Find the sixth.

1

Turn the mean back into a total: total $=$ mean $\times$ how many $=14\times6=84$.

2

Add the five known values: $10+12+15+17+11=65$.

3

The missing number is the difference: $84-65=19$.

Answer. $19$
✎
The key move. Whenever a question gives you a mean, immediately write down the total. Almost every "find the missing value" question turns on that one step.
Example 3
Averages from a frequency table
 ⌄

Goals scored in $25$ matches: $0$ goals in $7$ matches, $1$ in $9$, $2$ in $5$, $3$ in $3$, $4$ in $1$. Find the mean, median and mode.

1

$\Sigma f=7+9+5+3+1=25$.

2

$\Sigma fx=(0\times7)+(1\times9)+(2\times5)+(3\times3)+(4\times1)=32$.

3

Mean: $32\div25=1.28$.

4

Median: position $\frac{25+1}{2}=13$. The cumulative frequencies are $7$, $16$, … so the 13th value is in the group $x=1$.

5

Mode: the largest frequency is $9$, at $x=1$.

Answer. Mean $1.28$ · median $1$ · mode $1$
✎
A mean of $1.28$ goals is perfectly fine even though you cannot score $0.28$ of a goal — a mean need not be a possible data value.

Example 5
Quartiles from an even-sized list
 ⌄

Find the median, quartiles and IQR of $5,\ 7,\ 8,\ 10,\ 11,\ 13,\ 14,\ 16,\ 17,\ 19,\ 22,\ 25$.

1

Already ordered, $n=12$. Median: average the 6th and 7th: $\frac{13+14}{2}=13.5$.

2

Lower half $5,7,8,10,11,13$ — its median is $\frac{8+10}{2}=9$, so $Q_1=9$.

3

Upper half $14,16,17,19,22,25$ — its median is $\frac{17+19}{2}=18$, so $Q_3=18$.

4

IQR: $18-9=9$.

Answer. $Q_1=9$ · median $=13.5$ · $Q_3=18$ · IQR $=9$
✎
With an even $n$ the list splits cleanly in two. With an odd $n$, leave the median out of both halves. Paste this list into Lab 1 to check.
Example 6
Combining two means
 ⌄

In a class of $30$, the $18$ girls have a mean height of $155$ cm and the $12$ boys a mean of $162$ cm. Find the mean height of the whole class.

1

Convert each mean into a total: girls $18\times155=2790$ cm; boys $12\times162=1944$ cm.

2

Add the totals: $2790+1944=4734$ cm.

3

Divide by the total number of students: $4734\div30=157.8$.

Answer. $157.8$ cm
!
Averaging the two means — $\frac{155+162}{2}=158.5$ — is wrong, because there are more girls than boys. Always go back to totals.
Example 7
Comparing two datasets properly
 ⌄

Two machines fill bottles. Machine P has median $52$ ml and IQR $5$ ml; Machine Q has median $52$ ml and IQR $2$ ml. Compare them, and say which you would choose.

1

Average: the medians are equal at $52$ ml, so on average the two machines fill to the same volume.

2

Spread: Machine Q has the smaller IQR ($2$ ml against $5$ ml).

3

In context: a smaller spread means the volumes are closer together, so Q is more consistent.

Answer. Both machines have the same median volume of $52$ ml, but Machine Q is more consistent because its interquartile range is smaller — so choose Machine Q.

! Common mistakes to avoid

  • Not ordering the list before finding the median or the quartiles.
  • Giving the frequency instead of the value when asked for the mode.
  • Dividing by the number of classes instead of by $\Sigma f$.
  • Forgetting the word "estimate" for a mean from grouped data — it often carries its own mark.
  • Averaging two means when the groups are different sizes. Go back to totals.
  • Comparing only the averages. One average and one measure of spread, both in context.
  • Quoting a bare number as a comparison. "Class B has the higher median" needs the "so…" that follows.
  • Trusting the range when there is an outlier — use the IQR instead.
