Core Concepts

What a parallelogram is

A parallelogram is a four-sided figure whose two pairs of opposite sides never meet — they run in exactly the same direction. That single idea is the definition; everything else in this lesson is a consequence of it.

parallelogram — a quadrilateral with both pairs of opposite sides parallel.
symbol — write ▱ABCD and read it “parallelogram ABCD”. Name the vertices in order around the figure.
consecutive angles — two angles that share a side, such as ∠A and ∠B.
opposite angles — two angles that share no side, such as ∠A and ∠C.
In ▱ABCD:   BC ∥ AD  and  AB ∥ DC   by definition
Study Tip — include a figure. Theorems are stated in general terms. In a proof it is much easier to keep track of which segment or angle you are talking about if you first sketch the parallelogram and label it.

The four side-and-angle theorems

These four statements all begin the same way: “If a quadrilateral is a parallelogram, then…” They tell you what you are allowed to conclude once you already know the figure is a parallelogram.

Theorem 7.3 — Opposite Sides
If a quadrilateral is a parallelogram, then its opposite sides are congruent.
In ▱ABCD: AB ≅ CD and BC ≅ AD.
Theorem 7.4 — Opposite Angles
If a quadrilateral is a parallelogram, then its opposite angles are congruent.
In ▱ABCD: ∠A ≅ ∠C and ∠B ≅ ∠D.
Theorem 7.5 — Consecutive Angles
If a quadrilateral is a parallelogram, then its consecutive angles are supplementary.
In ▱ABCD: m∠A + m∠B = 180, m∠B + m∠C = 180, m∠C + m∠D = 180, m∠D + m∠A = 180.

Why? AB ∥ DC, and AD is a transversal, so ∠A and ∠D are same-side interior angles — and same-side interior angles are supplementary.
Theorem 7.6 — One Right Angle
If a parallelogram has one right angle, then it has four right angles.

Why? If m∠A = 90, then m∠C = 90 (opposite angles) and m∠B = 180 − 90 = 90 (consecutive angles), and likewise m∠D = 90.
Watch Out! Theorems 7.3 through 7.6 apply only if you already know the figure is a parallelogram. A quadrilateral drawn to look slanted is not enough — the problem must say ▱, or you must be able to prove both pairs of opposite sides are parallel.

The two diagonal theorems

Draw both diagonals of a parallelogram and they cross at the exact centre of the figure. Draw just one diagonal and it slices the parallelogram into two identical triangles.

Theorem 7.7 — Diagonals Bisect
If a quadrilateral is a parallelogram, then its diagonals bisect each other.
If the diagonals of ▱ABCD meet at E, then AE ≅ CE and BE ≅ DE.

∴ The intersection point is the midpoint of both diagonals — that is what makes the Midpoint Formula so useful on the coordinate plane.
Theorem 7.8 — Diagonal Separates
If a quadrilateral is a parallelogram, then each diagonal separates the parallelogram into two congruent triangles.
In ▱ABCD with diagonal AC: △ABC ≅ △CDA (by SSS, since the diagonal is shared).
Careful — diagonals bisect, they are not congruent. In a general parallelogram AC ≠ BD. Only special parallelograms (rectangles) have congruent diagonals. Bisecting each other and being equal in length are two different things.

Memorise these five

1
Opposite sides are parallel and congruent.
2
Opposite angles are congruent: set the two expressions equal.
3
Consecutive angles are supplementary: set the sum equal to 180.
4
Diagonals bisect each other: each half of one diagonal equals the other half.
5
Perimeter of a parallelogram: P = 2ℓ + 2w, because opposite sides are congruent.
Talk About It. Every parallelogram is a quadrilateral, but not every quadrilateral is a parallelogram. “Parallelogram” is a subset of “quadrilateral” — a trapezoid with only one pair of parallel sides is a quadrilateral that fails the test.

Practice 5 — coordinate geometry

▱ABCD has A(−3, 4), B(5, 4), C(7, −2), D(−1, −2). Verify the diagonals bisect each other and give the intersection point.

1mid AC = ((−3 + 7)/2, (4 + (−2))/2)Midpoint Formula

Why this works: two segments bisect each other exactly when they share a midpoint. Equal midpoints is the whole test.

Common mistakes to avoid
  • Setting consecutive angles equal instead of adding them to 180 (and the reverse for opposite angles).
  • Assuming the diagonals are congruent. They bisect each other, but AC ≠ BD unless the parallelogram is a rectangle.
  • Stopping at the variable. If the question asks for a measure, substitute the value back in.
  • Reporting a half-diagonal when the whole diagonal was requested.
  • Using Theorems 7.3–7.8 on a quadrilateral that has not been established as a parallelogram.
  • Misreading the vertex order: in ABCD, AB is opposite CD — not opposite BC.
