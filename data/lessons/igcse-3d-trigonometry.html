There is no new mathematics in this lesson. Every 3D question is solved by finding a right-angled triangle hidden inside the solid, redrawing it flat, and using Pythagoras or SOH CAH TOA on it. The hard part is spotting the triangle — so build the solids below, rotate the ideas around, and watch which triangle each question needs.

◎ What you will be able to do

  • Find lengths inside cuboids, pyramids, prisms and cones.
  • Find the angle between a line and a plane, and between two planes.
  • Tell the slant edge from the slant height — and half a side from half a diagonal.
  • Chain two or more triangles together in a multi-step problem.
  • Carry full accuracy through the working and round only at the end.

1 The core strategy

✎
The one big idea. Every 3D problem is solved by finding a right-angled triangle inside the shape, redrawing it flat on its own, and working on it in 2D.
  • Sketch the solid and mark every length you are given.
  • Identify the triangle containing what you want. It usually needs a vertical edge, a horizontal edge and a sloping line.
  • Redraw that triangle separately — flat, large, all lengths labelled. This step alone prevents most errors.
  • Check it is right-angled and say where. Vertical edges always meet the horizontal base at $90°$.
  • Apply Pythagoras or a trig ratio.
  • Repeat if necessary — many questions need a length from one triangle before a second triangle can be solved.
!
Never round an intermediate length. Store it in the calculator and use the full value in the next stage, or the final angle can be out by a whole degree. Better still, keep surds exact — write $\sqrt{125}$, not $11.18$.

2 Cuboids and the space diagonal Interactive

The longest straight line that fits inside a cuboid runs corner to opposite corner. It is found in two stages: first the diagonal across the base, then the diagonal through the solid.

Lab 1 — the cuboid explorer

Set the three edges, then click through the four stages. Watch how the base diagonal $AF$ becomes one side of the second triangle $AFG$.

Stage 1: $AF^{2}=AB^{2}+BF^{2}$  ·  Stage 2: $AG^{2}=AF^{2}+FG^{2}$

For a cuboid $8\times6\times5$: $AF^{2}=8^{2}+6^{2}=100$, so $AF=10$; then $AG^{2}=10^{2}+5^{2}=125$, so $AG=\sqrt{125}=11.2$ cm.

✎
The shortcut. Combining both stages, for a cuboid with edges $l$, $w$, $h$: $$\text{space diagonal}=\sqrt{l^{2}+w^{2}+h^{2}}$$ Here $\sqrt{64+36+25}=\sqrt{125}=11.2$ cm — the same answer in one line.
!
Use the shortcut only for a cuboid. In any other solid you must work through the triangles one at a time.

3 Angles in three dimensions

Angle between a line and a plane

Drop a perpendicular from the end of the line down onto the plane, then join the foot of that perpendicular back to the start of the line. The angle you want lies between the original line and this projection.

For the cuboid above: $\tan(\angle GAF)=\dfrac{FG}{AF}=\dfrac{5}{10}=0.5\Rightarrow 26.6°$
!
The angle between a line and a plane is always the smallest such angle, so it lies between $0°$ and $90°$. An answer above $90°$ means you used the wrong triangle.
Angle between two planes

Find the line where the two planes meet. From a point on that line, draw a line in each plane at right angles to it. The angle between those two lines is the angle between the planes.

For a pyramid, the angle between a triangular face and the base is measured using the slant height — the perpendicular from the apex to the midpoint of a base edge.

Angle between two lines

Two lines only form an angle if they meet, or can be made to meet. Redraw the triangle containing both; if it has no right angle, use the cosine rule.

4 Pyramids and cones Interactive

In a square-based pyramid the apex $V$ sits directly above the centre $M$ of the base. Almost every question comes down to choosing between two lengths:

  • $MN=\dfrac{s}{2}$ — half a side, used for anything involving a face.
  • $MA=\dfrac{s\sqrt2}{2}$ — half the diagonal, used for anything involving an edge.
Lab 2 — the pyramid explorer

Set the base and the height, then switch between the four questions. Look at how the same pyramid gives two different angles depending on whether you go to a corner or to the midpoint of a side.

You want…Use the triangle…Because
Slant edge $VA$$VMA$$MA$ is half the base diagonal; $VM$ is vertical
Slant height $VN$$VMN$$MN$ is half a base side; $VM$ is vertical
Angle between edge $VA$ and the base$VMA$$\tan=\dfrac{VM}{MA}$
Angle between face $VAB$ and the base$VMN$$\tan=\dfrac{VM}{MN}$
Angle at the apex, e.g. $\angle AVB$$AVB$Isosceles — split it in half at $N$
!
Slant edge and slant height are different lengths. The edge runs to a corner; the height runs to the midpoint of a side. Mixing them up is the classic pyramid error — and it changes the answer by several degrees.
Cones

A cone works the same way, with a single right-angled triangle made from the radius $r$, the vertical height $h$ and the slant height $l$:

$l^{2}=r^{2}+h^{2}$

5 Multi-step reasoning

Long 3D questions are just short ones chained together. Work backwards from what is asked.

  • Write down exactly what the question wants.
  • Name the triangle that contains it, and list that triangle's three sides.
  • Mark which of them you already know. Any you don't becomes a mini-question of its own.
  • Solve the mini-questions first, then come back to the main triangle.
✎
An example of the chain. "Find the angle between $VC$ and the base" → need triangle $VMC$ → need $MC$ → $MC$ is half the base diagonal → find the diagonal with Pythagoras on the base → work back up.
Accuracy discipline
  • Keep every intermediate result at full calculator accuracy, or in surd form.
  • Round only at the very end — lengths to 3 s.f., angles to 1 d.p.
  • Put units on every length and the degree symbol on every angle.
  • Sense-check: the space diagonal must be the longest line in a cuboid, and an angle with the base must be under $90°$.

✍️ Worked examples

Example 1
Space diagonal of a cuboid
 ⌄

A cuboid measures $8$ cm by $6$ cm by $5$ cm. Find the length of the longest straight line that fits inside it.

1

The longest line is the space diagonal, corner to opposite corner.

2

Base diagonal first — right-angled triangle on the base: $AF^{2}=8^{2}+6^{2}=64+36=100$, so $AF=10$ cm.

3

Then the space diagonal — the vertical edge is perpendicular to the base: $AG^{2}=10^{2}+5^{2}=125$, so $AG=\sqrt{125}$.

4

$\sqrt{125}=11.180\ldots$

Answer. $11.2$ cm (3 s.f.)
✎
One-line check. $\sqrt{8^{2}+6^{2}+5^{2}}=\sqrt{125}=11.18$ ✔
Example 2
Angle between a diagonal and the base
 ⌄

For the same $8\times6\times5$ cuboid, find the angle between the space diagonal $AG$ and the base.

1

The projection of $AG$ onto the base is $AF$, so the required angle is $\angle GAF$.

2

Redraw triangle $AFG$ flat: $AF=10$ (horizontal), $FG=5$ (vertical), right angle at $F$.

3

Opposite and adjacent are known → use tan: $\tan(\angle GAF)=\dfrac{5}{10}=0.5$

4

$\angle GAF=\tan^{-1}(0.5)=26.565\ldots°$

Answer. $26.6°$ (1 d.p.)
!
Use the exact base diagonal of $10$, not a rounded value — and be sure the angle is at $A$, not at $G$.
Example 3
Slant edge of a pyramid
 ⌄

A pyramid has a square base of side $6$ cm and a vertical height of $8$ cm. Find the length of a slant edge $VA$.

1

The apex sits directly above the centre $M$ of the square base.

2

Base diagonal: $AC=\sqrt{6^{2}+6^{2}}=\sqrt{72}=6\sqrt2\approx8.485$ cm.

3

Half of it: $MA=3\sqrt2\approx4.2426$ cm.

4

Triangle $VMA$ is right-angled at $M$: $VA^{2}=VM^{2}+MA^{2}=8^{2}+(3\sqrt2)^{2}=64+18=82$

5

$VA=\sqrt{82}=9.055\ldots$

Answer. $9.06$ cm (3 s.f.)
✎
Neat trick. $(3\sqrt2)^{2}=9\times2=18$ exactly — keeping the surd avoids any rounding error.
Example 4
Angle between a slant edge and the base
 ⌄

For the same pyramid (base $6$ cm, height $8$ cm), find the angle between the edge $VA$ and the base.

1

The projection of $VA$ onto the base is $MA$, so the angle wanted is $\angle VAM$.

2

In triangle $VMA$: opposite $VM=8$, adjacent $MA=3\sqrt2=4.2426$.

3

$\tan(\angle VAM)=\dfrac{8}{4.2426}=1.8856$

4

$\angle VAM=\tan^{-1}(1.8856)=62.06\ldots°$

Answer. $62.1°$ (1 d.p.)
Example 5
Angle between a face and the base
 ⌄

For the same pyramid, find the angle between the triangular face $VAB$ and the base.

1

The two planes meet along the edge $AB$. Let $N$ be the midpoint of $AB$.

2

$VN\perp AB$ (the face is isosceles) and $MN\perp AB$ in the base, so the required angle is $\angle VNM$.

3

$MN$ is half the side of the square: $MN=3$ cm.

4

Triangle $VMN$ is right-angled at $M$: $\tan(\angle VNM)=\dfrac{VM}{MN}=\dfrac{8}{3}=2.6667$

5

$\angle VNM=\tan^{-1}(2.6667)=69.44\ldots°$

Answer. $69.4°$ (1 d.p.)
!
This uses $MN=3$ (half a side), while Example 4 used $MA=4.24$ (half a diagonal). Choosing the wrong one is the most frequent mistake in pyramid questions — it gives $62.1°$ instead of $69.4°$. Compare them side by side in Lab 2.
Example 6
Slant height of a cone
 ⌄

A cone has radius $5$ cm and vertical height $12$ cm. Find the slant height and the angle the slant makes with the base.

1

The radius, the vertical height and the slant height form a right-angled triangle.

2

$l^{2}=r^{2}+h^{2}=5^{2}+12^{2}=25+144=169$, so $l=13$ cm.

3

For the angle at the base edge: opposite $12$, adjacent $5$, so $\tan\theta=\dfrac{12}{5}=2.4$

4

$\theta=67.38\ldots°$

Answer. Slant height $13$ cm (an exact 5–12–13 triangle) · angle $67.4°$ (1 d.p.)
Example 7
A multi-step problem
 ⌄

A vertical mast $VM$ stands at the corner $M$ of a rectangular field $MNPQ$, where $MN=40$ m and $NP=30$ m. The angle of elevation of the top $V$ from $P$ is $22°$. Find the height of the mast and the angle of elevation of $V$ from $N$.

1

Find $MP$, the diagonal of the field, using Pythagoras on the horizontal rectangle: $MP^{2}=40^{2}+30^{2}=2500$, so $MP=50$ m.

2

Find the height in right-angled triangle $VMP$, where the elevation from $P$ is $22°$: $\tan 22°=\dfrac{VM}{50}$, so $VM=50\tan 22°=20.20\ldots$ m.

3

Elevation from $N$ — a different triangle, $VMN$, with $MN=40$ m: $\tan(\text{angle})=\dfrac{20.20}{40}=0.5051$, giving $26.79\ldots°$.

Answer. Height $20.2$ m (3 s.f.) · elevation from $N$ is $26.8°$ (1 d.p.)
✎
Sense check. $N$ is closer to the mast than $P$ is ($40$ m against $50$ m), so the mast should look steeper from $N$ — and $26.8°>22°$ ✔
!
Use the unrounded $20.2033\ldots$ in the final step. Here rounding early still gives $26.8°$, but on other questions it changes the answer.

! Common mistakes to avoid

  • Working on the 3D picture directly. Redraw the triangle flat first — angles in a 3D sketch are distorted and cannot be trusted.
  • Confusing half a side with half a diagonal. Face angles use half a side; edge angles use half a diagonal.
  • Confusing slant edge with slant height. The edge goes to a corner, the height to a midpoint.
  • Rounding partway through. Carry full accuracy, or keep surds exact, until the final line.
  • Assuming a triangle is right-angled. Justify it — vertical edges meet the horizontal base at $90°$. If there is no right angle, use the sine or cosine rule.
  • Measuring the angle at the wrong vertex. The angle between a line and a plane sits where the line meets the plane.
  • Forgetting units and the degree symbol. Lengths in cm or m, angles in degrees.
