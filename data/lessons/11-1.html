Core Concepts

1. What "base" and "height" really mean

Every area formula on this page is really the same idea: how far across times how far up. The trap is that "how far up" is never a slanted side — it is always measured straight up, at a right angle to the base.

Base of a parallelogram — any side of the parallelogram. All four sides can serve as a base.
Altitude of a parallelogram — a perpendicular segment between any two parallel bases.
Height of a parallelogram — the length of an altitude of the parallelogram.
Height of a trapezoid — the perpendicular distance between the bases of the trapezoid.
Decomposition — the process of separating a figure into two or more nonoverlapping parts.
Diagonal (review) — a segment that connects any two nonconsecutive vertices of a polygon.
Study Tip — Heights of Figures: the height of a figure can be measured by extending a base. In parallelogram ABCD, the height that corresponds to base DC may land outside the figure, on the extension of DC. That is still a legal height.

2. Where the parallelogram formula comes from

Cut a right triangle off one end of a parallelogram and slide it to the other end. Nothing is added and nothing is lost — you get a rectangle with the same base and the same height. A rectangle's area is bh, so the parallelogram's area must be bh too.

Postulate 11.1 · Area Addition Postulate
The area of a region is the sum of the areas of its nonoverlapping parts.

This postulate is what makes decomposition legal: chop a figure into pieces, add the pieces' areas, and you have the whole.

Key Concept · Area of a Parallelogram
The area A of a parallelogram is the product of the base b and its corresponding height h.
A = bh
Watch Out! Units: area is measured in square units — square feet (ft2), square millimetres (mm2), square yards (yd2). If your answer has no exponent on the unit, you have written a length, not an area.

3. Where the trapezoid formula comes from

A trapezoid is a quadrilateral with exactly one pair of parallel sides; those parallel sides are the bases. Take a trapezoid, copy it, rotate the copy 180°, and fit the two together. The result is a parallelogram whose base is b1 + b2 and whose height is h. That parallelogram has area h(b1 + b2), and one trapezoid is exactly half of it.

Key Concept · Area of a Trapezoid
The area A of a trapezoid is one half the product of the height h and the sum of its bases, b1 and b2.
A = ½ h(b1 + b2)
Read it as an average: A = h · (b1+b2)/2. The trapezoid behaves like a rectangle whose width is the average of the two bases.

4. Rhombi and kites — use the diagonals

A rhombus is a parallelogram with all four sides congruent. A kite is a quadrilateral with exactly two pairs of consecutive congruent sides. In both figures the diagonals are perpendicular, so their areas are controlled by the diagonals rather than by a base and height.

Key Concept · Area of a Rhombus or Kite
The area A of a rhombus or kite is one half the product of the lengths of its diagonals, d1 and d2.
A = ½ d1d2
Watch Out! Half-diagonals: the diagonals of a rhombus bisect each other, so a figure labelled "6 mm" and "7 mm" from the centre really has d1 = 12 and d2 = 14. Double every half before you multiply. In a kite, only one diagonal is bisected.

A rhombus is also a parallelogram, so A = bh still works on it — use whichever information you are actually given.

5. When the height is missing

Right triangle in the corner? Use the Pythagorean Theorem: a2 + b2 = c2, where the slant side is the hypotenuse.
45° angle? In a 45°-45°-90° triangle the two legs are equal, so the height equals the horizontal offset.
30° or 60° angle? In a 30°-60°-90° triangle the legs are in ratio 1 : √3, short leg opposite the 30°.
Odd angle? Use trigonometry: h = (slant side) · sin(angle).
Trapezoid with two slant legs? Drop two perpendiculars to split it into right triangle + rectangle + right triangle.
Study Tip — Separating Figures: to solve some area problems you may need to draw in parallel and/or perpendicular lines to find information that is not provided.
1
Parallelogram: A = bh. The h must be perpendicular to the b you chose.
2
Trapezoid: A = ½h(b1 + b2). Only the two parallel sides are bases.
3
Rhombus or kite: A = ½d1d2, using full diagonals.
4
Working backwards from an area is just solving a one-step equation for the unknown length.

Worked Examples

Find the area of parallelogram ABCD. The slant side AD = 25 ft, the base DC = 23 ft, and the altitude from A meets the extension of DC 7 ft to the left of D.
1h2 + 72 = 252Pythagorean Theorem
2h2 = 625 − 49 = 576Simplify
3h = 24Solve (positive root)
4A = bhArea of a parallelogram
5A = (23)(24) = 552Solve
A = 552 ft2
The trap: 25 ft is the slant side, not the height. Using A = 23 × 25 = 575 is the single most common error on this figure. The height is the dashed segment, and it lands outside the parallelogram on the extension of DC — that is perfectly legal.
Check: 7-24-25 is a Pythagorean triple, and 72 + 242 = 49 + 576 = 625 = 252 ✓
Find the area of a parallelogram with base 17 yd, whose altitude cuts off a 45°-45°-90° corner triangle with a horizontal leg of 9 yd.
145°-45°-90° ⇒ leg = legSpecial right triangle
2h = 9 ydLeg opposite 45° = leg adjacent to 45°
3A = bhArea of a parallelogram
4A = (17)(9) = 153Solve
A = 153 yd2
General trig version: if the slant side has length s and makes an angle θ with the base, then h = s · sinθ and A = b · s · sinθ. The special right triangles are just the shortcut cases θ = 30°, 45°, 60°.
A raised garden bed is shaped like a trapezoid with h = 7 ft, b1 = 8 ft and b2 = 1.5 ft. Find its area, then decide how many 12-square-foot bags of mulch are needed.
1A = ½h(b1 + b2)Area of a trapezoid
2A = ½(7)(8 + 1.5)h = 7, b1 = 8, b2 = 1.5
3A = ½(7)(9.5) = 33.25Solve
433.25 ft2 × (1 bag / 12 ft2) = 2.77 bagsUnit analysis
5round up → 3 bags2 bags would not be enough
A = 33.25 ft2; 3 bags of mulch
Watch Out! When a count of physical items comes from an area, always round up, never to the nearest whole number.
Find the area of the isosceles trapezoid with bases 2 cm and 4 cm and legs of 3 cm each.
1x + 2 + x = 4Bottom base split by the two altitudes
22x = 2, so x = 1Solve
312 + h2 = 32Pythagorean Theorem on a corner triangle
4h = √(9 − 1) = √8 = 2√2Solve
5A = ½h(b1 + b2) = ½(√8)(4 + 2)Area of a trapezoid
6A = 3√8 = 6√2 ≈ 8.49Solve
A ≈ 8.49 cm2
Check by decomposition: rectangle = 2 · 2√2 = 4√2; two triangles = 2 · ½ · 1 · 2√2 = 2√2. Total = 6√2 ✓ (Area Addition Postulate)
Find the area of a rhombus in which the distance from the centre to a vertex is 6 mm along one diagonal and 7 mm along the other.
1d1 = 6 + 6 = 12 mmDiagonals of a rhombus bisect each other
2d2 = 7 + 7 = 14 mmSame reason
3A = ½d1d2Area of a rhombus
4A = ½(12)(14) = 84Solve
A = 84 mm2
The trap: using the half-diagonals directly gives ½(6)(7) = 21 — exactly one quarter of the truth. Doubling both halves multiplies the answer by 4.
Find the area of a kite whose diagonals measure 16 in. and 9 in.
1A = ½d1d2Area of a kite
2A = ½(16)(9)Substitute
3A = 72Solve
A = 72 in2
Why it works: the perpendicular diagonals cut the kite into four right triangles. Adding their areas gives ½d1d2 no matter where along d2 the crossing point sits — which is why the same formula covers both the rhombus and the kite.
One diagonal of a kite measures 55.88 centimetres. If the area of the kite is 92 square inches, what is the length of the other diagonal in inches, to the nearest tenth? (1 in. = 2.54 cm)
155.88 cm × (1 in. / 2.54 cm) = 22 in.Convert units first
2½d1d2 = AArea of a kite
3½(22)(x) = 92A = 92, d1 = 22, d2 = x
411x = 92, x = 92/11 ≈ 8.4Solve
The other diagonal is about 8.4 in.
Watch Out! Converting units: one measure is metric and the area is in square inches. Convert the length before you substitute — converting an area needs the square of the conversion factor, which is a far easier place to slip.
Check: ½(22)(8.4) = 92.4 ≈ 92 ✓ (small gap is the rounding of 8.36 to 8.4)

Common mistakes to avoid
  • Using a slant side as the height of a parallelogram or trapezoid.
  • Using a leg of a trapezoid as a base — only the parallel sides are bases.
  • Forgetting to double half-diagonals in a rhombus (this quarters the answer).
  • Forgetting the ½ in the trapezoid, rhombus and kite formulas (this doubles the answer).
  • Reporting the answer in linear units instead of square units.
  • Rounding a bag/tile/sheet count down instead of up.
  • Substituting a centimetre length into a square-inch equation without converting.
