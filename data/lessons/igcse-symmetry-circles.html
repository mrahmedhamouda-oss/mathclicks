A circle is symmetrical about every line through its centre, and that single fact generates the whole topic: the perpendicular from the centre bisects a chord, a tangent meets a radius at $90°$, and five angle theorems follow. Turn shapes to find their order of symmetry below, then drag points around a circle and watch each theorem hold.

◎ What you will be able to do

  • Count lines of symmetry and give the order of rotational symmetry.
  • Count planes of symmetry of a solid.
  • Use the chord and tangent properties to build a right-angled triangle.
  • Apply the five circle theorems.
  • Quote the reason the mark scheme is looking for — it carries its own mark.

1 Symmetry in two dimensions Interactive

A shape has line symmetry if a mirror line can be drawn so that one half is the exact reflection of the other. The order of rotational symmetry is the number of times a shape looks identical during one full turn.

Pick a shape: its mirror lines are drawn in, and the slider turns it. The dashed outline is where it started, so you can see exactly when it lands back on itself.

!
The parallelogram trap. It looks symmetrical but has no lines of symmetry — folding along a diagonal does not match the halves. It does have rotational symmetry of order $2$.
✎
Order is never zero. A shape with no rotational symmetry has order $1$, because it fits onto itself once in a full turn. Writing $0$ loses the mark.

3 Chords and tangents Interactive

These four results all follow from the symmetry of the circle, and each one turns a circle problem into a right-angled triangle problem.

PropertyWhat it gives you
The perpendicular from the centre bisects a chordA right-angled triangle: radius, half the chord, distance from the centre
Equal chords are equidistant from the centreAnd the converse — chords the same distance from the centre are equal
A tangent is perpendicular to the radius at the point of contactA right angle at the point of contact
The two tangents from an external point are equalAn isosceles triangle, and $OP$ bisects the angle between them
$r^{2}=d^{2}+\left(\tfrac{\text{chord}}{2}\right)^{2}$

Give it any two of the radius, the chord and the distance from the centre — or the radius and a tangent — and it draws the triangle to scale and sets out the Pythagoras.

!
Halve the chord first. Using the full chord as a leg asks you to square-root a negative number — the surest sign the working has gone wrong.
✎
Two tangents from a point $P$. $PA=PB$, triangle $APB$ is isosceles, $OP$ bisects angle $APB$, angle $OAP=$ angle $OBP=90°$, and therefore angle $AOB+$ angle $APB=180°$.

4 The five circle theorems Interactive

1 · Angle at the centre
The angle at the centre is twice the angle at the circumference, standing on the same arc.
2 · Angle in a semicircle
An angle drawn on a diameter is $90°$ — this is theorem 1 with a central angle of $180°$.
3 · Same segment
Angles standing on the same chord, from the same side, are equal.
4 · Cyclic quadrilateral
Opposite angles add to $180°$ — and all four vertices must be on the circle.
5 · the angle between a tangent and a chord $=$ the angle in the alternate segment

The "alternate segment" is the part of the circle on the other side of the chord from the angle you are measuring.

✍️ Worked examples

Example 1
Symmetry of a solid
 ⌄

Write down the number of planes of symmetry of (a) a cuboid with all edges different, (b) a cube, (c) a square-based pyramid.

1

Cuboid: one plane parallel to each pair of opposite faces, halfway between them — three pairs, so $3$. No diagonal plane works because the edges differ.

2

Cube: the same $3$, plus $6$ diagonal planes through pairs of opposite edges — $9$ in total.

3

Square-based pyramid: $2$ through opposite base edges and $2$ through the base diagonals — $4$.

Answer. (a) $3$ · (b) $9$ · (c) $4$
Example 2
Chord and radius with Pythagoras
 ⌄

A circle has radius $10$ cm and a chord of length $16$ cm. Find the distance from the centre to the chord.

1

The perpendicular from the centre bisects the chord, so half of it is $16\div2=8$ cm.

2

That gives a right-angled triangle with hypotenuse $10$ (the radius) and one leg $8$.

3

Pythagoras: $d^{2}=10^{2}-8^{2}=100-64=36$.

Answer. $6$ cm
!
Using the full chord of $16$ as a leg gives $\sqrt{100-256}$, which is impossible. Always halve the chord first — Lab 2 holds exactly this question.
Example 3
Tangent and radius
 ⌄

$T$ is on a circle with centre $O$ and radius $5$ cm, and $PT$ is a tangent of length $12$ cm. Find $OP$ and angle $OPT$.

1

The tangent is perpendicular to the radius at $T$, so triangle $OTP$ is right-angled at $T$.

2

$OP^{2}=5^{2}+12^{2}=169$, so $OP=13$ cm.

3

At $P$: opposite $=5$, adjacent $=12$, so $\tan(OPT)=\frac{5}{12}$ and angle $OPT=22.6°$.

Answer. $OP=13$ cm and angle $OPT=22.6°$ (1 d.p.)
Example 4
Angle at the centre
 ⌄

$A$, $B$ and $C$ lie on a circle with centre $O$. Angle $AOB=130°$ and $C$ is on the major arc. Find angle $ACB$, giving a reason.

1

Both angles stand on the same arc $AB$.

2

The angle at the centre is twice the angle at the circumference, so halve it: $130\div2$.

Answer. $65°$, because the angle at the centre is twice the angle at the circumference standing on the same arc
✎
Drag the slider in Lab 3 and watch the two measured angles stay in the ratio $2:1$ whatever the arc.
Example 5
Angle in a semicircle
 ⌄

$AB$ is a diameter and $C$ lies on the circle, with angle $CAB=35°$. Find angle $ABC$.

1

$AB$ is a diameter, so angle $ACB=90°$ (angle in a semicircle).

2

The angles of triangle $ABC$ add to $180°$.

3

Angle $ABC=180-90-35=55°$.

Answer. $55°$
✎
Spotting it. Whenever a triangle has one side passing through the centre, the opposite angle is $90°$. Look for the centre marked on the line.
Example 6
Cyclic quadrilateral
 ⌄

$ABCD$ is a cyclic quadrilateral with angle $DAB=78°$ and angle $ABC=92°$. Find angle $BCD$ and angle $CDA$.

1

Opposite angles of a cyclic quadrilateral add to $180°$.

2

$A$ and $C$ are opposite: angle $BCD=180-78=102°$.

3

$B$ and $D$ are opposite: angle $CDA=180-92=88°$.

Answer. angle $BCD=102°$ and angle $CDA=88°$
✎
Check. All four angles total $78+92+102+88=360°$, as they must for any quadrilateral ✓
Example 7
Two tangents from a point
 ⌄

$PA$ and $PB$ are tangents to a circle with centre $O$, touching at $A$ and $B$, and angle $APB=48°$. Find angle $AOB$ and angle $PAB$.

1

Angle $AOB$: $OAPB$ is a quadrilateral with right angles at $A$ and $B$ (tangent ⟂ radius), and its angles total $360°$: $360-90-90-48=132°$.

2

Angle $PAB$: tangents from an external point are equal, so $PA=PB$ and triangle $APB$ is isosceles. The base angles are equal: $(180-48)\div2=66°$.

Answer. angle $AOB=132°$ and angle $PAB=66°$
Example 8
Alternate segment theorem
 ⌄

$TB$ is a tangent to a circle at $T$, and the chord $TA$ makes an angle of $64°$ with the tangent. $B$ lies in the alternate segment. Find angle $TBA$, giving a reason.

1

Identify the chord: $TA$, drawn from the point of contact $T$.

2

The alternate segment is the part of the circle on the other side of $TA$ from the $64°$ angle — that is where $B$ lies.

3

The theorem says the two angles are equal.

Answer. angle $TBA=64°$, by the alternate segment theorem
!
There are two angles between the tangent and the chord at $T$ and they add to $180°$. Match the one on the opposite side of the chord from the segment containing $B$.

! Common mistakes to avoid

  • Saying a parallelogram has lines of symmetry. It has none, but order $2$.
  • Giving an order of rotational symmetry of $0$. The minimum is $1$.
  • Using the whole chord in Pythagoras. The perpendicular from the centre bisects it.
  • Halving the wrong angle. The angle at the centre is the larger one.
  • Applying the cyclic quadrilateral rule to adjacent angles. Only opposite pairs.
  • Using a theorem when not all the points are on the circle. Check first.
  • Picking the wrong angle in the alternate segment theorem. Match the opposite side of the chord.
  • Forgetting that two radii make an isosceles triangle — that gives you the base angles for free.
  • Giving no reason. "Give a reason" is a separate mark — quote the theorem by name.
