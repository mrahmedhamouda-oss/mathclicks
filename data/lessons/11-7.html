Find the volume of cylinders, cones, spheres, hemispheres and composite solids — from numbers, from algebraic expressions, and from a slant height.

Core Concepts

1. Volume of a Cylinder

Think of a cylinder as a stack of paper-thin circular discs, all exactly the same size. Each disc has area B = πr², and you stack them to a height h. That is exactly the same idea as a prism — area of the base, times how tall the stack is.

Key Concept · Volume of a Cylinder

Words: The volume V of a cylinder is V = Bh or V = πr²h, where B is the area of the base, h is the height of the cylinder, and r is the radius of the base.

V = Bh    or    V = πr²h
!
The height h is measured perpendicular to the base — never along a slanted edge.

2. Volume of a Cone

A cone is exactly one third of the cylinder that wraps around it. Put a cone and a cylinder side by side with the same base area B and the same height h: the cone holds one third as much.

Why? A pyramid is one third of the prism with the same base and height. Now take a pyramid and a cone with the same base area B and the same height h. By Cavalieri's Principle, if every horizontal slice of the two solids has the same area, the two solids have the same volume — and similar triangles show the slices do match. So the cone must obey the pyramid rule too.

Key Concept · Volume of a Cone

Words: The volume of a circular cone is V = (1/3)Bh, or V = (1/3)πr²h, where B is the area of the base, h is the height of the cone, and r is the radius of the base.

V = (1/3)Bh    or    V = (1/3)πr²h
Watch Out! · Volumes of Cones. The formula for the surface area of a cone only applies to right cones. However, the formula for the volume applies to oblique cones as well as right cones — as long as h is the perpendicular height.
Think About It! "The volume of a cone with radius r and height h equals the volume of a prism with height h." This is sometimes true — it happens only when the prism's base area B satisfies Bh = (1/3)πr²h, that is, when B = (1/3)πr².

3. Volume of a Sphere

Suppose a sphere with radius r contains infinitely many pyramids with vertices at the centre of the sphere. Each pyramid has height r and contributes V = (1/3)Br. The sum of the volumes of all the pyramids equals the volume of the sphere — and the sum of all their base areas is the surface area 4πr². So V = (1/3)(4πr²)(r) = (4/3)πr³.

Key Concept · Volume of a Sphere

Words: The volume V of a sphere is V = (4/3)πr³, where r is the radius of the sphere.

V = (4/3)πr³
great circle — a plane can intersect a sphere in a point or in a circle. If the circle contains the centre of the sphere, the intersection is called a great circle. It is the largest circle you can cut from the sphere, with the same radius r.
hemisphere — a great circle separates a sphere into two congruent halves, each called a hemisphere. Its volume is half a sphere: V = (1/2)·(4/3)πr³ = (2/3)πr³.
3
The sphere formula uses r3, not r2. If you ever get a "sphere volume" with only a square in it, you have used the surface-area formula by mistake.

4. Slant Height vs. Height (cones)

Problems often give a cone's slant height ℓ — the distance from the apex straight down the side to the rim — instead of the perpendicular height h. The radius, the height and the slant height form a right triangle, so use the Pythagorean Theorem first.

r² + h² = ℓ²  →  h = √(ℓ² − r²)  →  V = (1/3)πr²h
Watch Out! · Rounding. When you find the height of a container, you may be tempted to round that measure to the nearest tenth. Rounding at that stage will change the final calculation of the volume. Whenever possible, avoid rounding at intermediate steps. Keep h = √200 exact and wait to round until you have calculated the final answer.

5. Dimensions Given as Expressions

When the radius or height is written in terms of x, substitute the expression into the formula exactly as if it were a number — keep the parentheses.

Write the correct volume formula.
Substitute the expressions for r and h, keeping each inside parentheses.
Leave the answer in terms of x and π (factored form is fine), or expand if asked.
For a numeric answer, substitute the value of x into the expression from Part A, then simplify and round last.
Watch Out! · Multiple Expressions. The same volume can be written more than one way. For a cylinder with r = x − 5 and h = x, you could write V = π(x − 5)²x or, in expanded form, V = πx3 − 10πx² + 25πx. Both are correct. Remember (2x + 3)3 ≠ 8x3 + 27!

6. Composite Solids

A composite solid is built from two or more simple solids joined together. Find each piece separately, then add.

Break the solid into cylinders, cones, hemispheres and prisms.
Identify the radius and height of each piece — joined pieces usually share the same radius.
Compute each volume, keeping π exact.
Add (or subtract, if material was carved out) and round only at the very end.

Try It Yourself

Practice 1 · Cylinder from an expression

Find the volume of a cylinder with radius 2x mm and height (x − 2) mm. (a) in terms of x and π; (b) to the nearest tenth if x = 10.
1V = πr²h = π(2x)²(x − 2)Substitute
2= 4πx²(x − 2) = 4πx³ − 8πx²(2x)² = 4x²
3x = 10: 4π(1000) − 8π(100) = 3200πSubstitute x = 10
(a) V = 4πx³ − 8πx² mm³  ·  (b) V ≈ 10053.1 mm³

Practice 2 · Diameter written in terms of the height

A cylinder has a diameter that is 6 cm shorter than its height x. (a) Find V in terms of x and π; (b) find V to the nearest tenth if the height is 14 cm.
1d = x − 6, so r = (x − 6)/2Halve the diameter FIRST
2V = π[(x − 6)/2]²x = π(x − 6)²x / 4Volume of a cylinder
3x = 14: π(8)²(14)/4 = π(64)(14)/4 = 224πSubstitute x = 14
(a) V = π(x − 6)²x/4 cm³  ·  (b) V ≈ 703.7 cm³

Practice 3 · Cone from an expression

A cone has radius x ft and height (3x + 4) ft. (a) Find V in terms of x and π; (b) find V to the nearest tenth if x = 3.
1V = (1/3)πx²(3x + 4)Volume of a cone
2= πx³ + (4/3)πx²Distribute (1/3)πx²
3x = 3: (1/3)π(9)(13) = 39πSubstitute x = 3
(a) V = (1/3)πx²(3x + 4) ft³  ·  (b) V ≈ 122.5 ft³

Practice 4 · Sphere from an expression

A sphere has radius (5x − 0.5) in. (a) Find V in terms of x and π; (b) find V to the nearest tenth if x = 1.
1V = (4/3)π(5x − 0.5)³Volume of a sphere
2x = 1: r = 5(1) − 0.5 = 4.5Simplify the radius first
3V = (4/3)π(91.125) = 121.5π4.5³ = 91.125
(a) V = (4/3)π(5x − 0.5)³ in³  ·  (b) V ≈ 381.7 in³

Practice 5 · Composite solid

A solid consists of a cylinder of radius 9 m and height 14 m joined to a cone of radius 9 m and height 10 m. Find the exact volume in terms of π, then round to the nearest tenth.
1Cylinder: V = π(9)²(14) = 1134πV = πr²h
2Cone: V = (1/3)π(9)²(10) = 270πV = (1/3)πr²h
3Total = 1134π + 270π = 1404πAdd exact values
V = 1404π ≈ 4410.8 m³
Note: The cone's volume is exactly one third of a cylinder with the same radius and height 10 (which would be 810π), and 810π/3 = 270π. ✓

Common mistakes to avoid:
  • Forgetting the 1/3 on a cone — the answer comes out 3 times too big.
  • Using the diameter as the radius — that multiplies a sphere's volume by 8.
  • Using the slant height ℓ in place of the perpendicular height h.
  • Squaring instead of cubing in (4/3)πr³.
  • Rounding intermediate steps (e.g. √200 ≈ 14.1) instead of only the final answer.
  • Writing (2x + 3)³ as 8x³ + 27 — a binomial cube must be expanded properly.
  • Mixing units, or reporting a volume in square units instead of cubic units.
