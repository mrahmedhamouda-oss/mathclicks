Core Concepts

Where we are going

Everything you already know about square roots — splitting them apart, pulling perfect squares out, combining like terms — works exactly the same way for cube roots, fourth roots, and every other nth root. This lesson takes those square-root rules and extends them to radicals with any index.

like radical expressions — two radicals are like when both the index and the radicand are the same. Only like radicals can be added or subtracted. 33√(5x) and 83√(5x) are like; √5 and 3√5 are not.
conjugates — the pair a√b + c√d and a√b − c√d, where a, b, c, d are rational. Their product is always rational, which is what kills a radical in a denominator.

Splitting a radical across a product

A radical of a product is the product of the radicals — that is what lets you peel a perfect nth power out of a radicand and leave the rest inside.

Key Concept · Product Property of Radicals
Words: For any real numbers a and b and any integer n > 1,
n√(ab) = n√a · n√b,   if n is even and a ≥ 0, b ≥ 0, or if n is odd.
Examples: √12 · √3 = √36 = 6  and  3√4 · 3√16 = 3√64 = 4

Why the condition on n? If n is even, an nth root of a negative number is not a real number, so both factors must be non-negative before you are allowed to split. If n is odd, negatives are fine — 3√(−8) = −2.

Splitting a radical across a quotient

Key Concept · Quotient Property of Radicals
Words: For any real numbers a and b ≠ 0 and any integer n > 1,
n√(a/b) = n√a ÷ n√b,   if all of the roots are defined.
Examples: √(x4/25) = √x4 ÷ √25 = x2/5  and  3√(27/8) = 3√27 ÷ 3√8 = 3/2

What “simplest form” means

A radical expression is not finished until all four of these are true.

Key Concept · Simplest Form of Radical Expressions
A radical expression is in simplest form when the following conditions are met.
  • The index n is as small as possible.
  • The radicand contains no factors (other than 1) that are nth powers of an integer or polynomial.
  • The radicand contains no fractions.
  • No radicals appear in the denominator.
1
To simplify n√(radicand), factor the radicand into perfect nth powers times leftovers. Perfect nth powers walk out of the radical; leftovers stay in.
2
A variable power is a perfect nth power exactly when its exponent is a multiple of n: y12 = (y3)4 is a perfect 4th power, so 4√(y12) = |y3|.
3
Divide the exponent by the index: quotient = how many come out, remainder = how many stay in. 3√(b14) → 14 ÷ 3 = 4 r 2 → b43√(b2).
Study Tip · Absolute value. Use absolute value bars when an even root of an even power produces an odd power outside. But if the radicand itself already forces the variable to be non-negative, the bars are unnecessary. In √(75x12y7) the factor y7 must be non-negative for the square root to be real, so y ≥ 0 and y3 needs no bars.
Study Tip · Negative radicands. If a radicand has a negative constant, factor out −1 first. Then n√(−1) = −1 when n is odd, and = i when n is even.

Adding and subtracting radicals

Radicals combine exactly the way monomials do: 3q + 8q = 11q becomes 3√5 + 8√5 = 11√5. The radical is just the “variable part.”

a·n√c ± b·n√c = (a ± b)·n√c

Two radicals such as √18 and √32 may look unlike, but after simplifying, √18 = 3√2 and √32 = 4√2. So they are like radical expressions after all and can be combined.

Simplify every radical completely first.
Group terms that now have the same index and the same radicand.
Add or subtract the coefficients only. The radical never changes.
Leave unlike radicals side by side — that is a finished answer.
Watch Out! √a + √b ≠ √(a+b). Check: √9 + √16 = 3 + 4 = 7, but √25 = 5. Addition never goes inside a radical.

Multiplying radicals

Radicals with the same index multiply using the Product Property: multiply the coefficients, then multiply the radicands, then simplify what comes out.

c·n√a · d·n√b = cd·n√(ab)

For products with more than one term, use the Distributive Property or FOIL, exactly as with binomials:

(3√6 + 1)(3√2 − 3√7) = 3√12 − 3√42 + 3√2 − 3√7
Watch Out! You may only merge radicands when the indices match. √2 · 3√2 is not √4 or 3√4; it must be rewritten with rational exponents first.

Rationalizing the denominator

A radical in the denominator is not simplest form. Multiply the numerator and denominator by whatever makes the radicand an exact nth power — that is multiplying by 1, so the value never changes.

If the denominator is…Multiply numerator and denominator by…Example
√b √b 4/√7 = (4·√7)/(√7·√7) = 4√7/7
n√(bx) n√(bn−x) 3/5√2 = 3·5√(24)/5√(25) = 35√16/2
a√b + c√d (two terms) its conjugate a√b − c√d 1/(√5+2) = (√5−2)/(5−4) = √5 − 2

Why conjugates work: they are a difference of squares. (a√b + c√d)(a√b − c√d) = a2b − c2d, and every radical is gone because each one got squared.

Watch Out! · Rationalizing the Denominator. When deciding what to multiply by, make sure you raise the entire term under the radical to the power n − x. For 3√(7a) you must multiply by 3√(72a2), not just 3√(a2).

Worked Examples

Simplify each expression.   a. 3√(−27a6b14)    b. √(75x12y7)

Part a — index 3, so look for perfect cubes.

13√(−27a6b14) = 3√((−3)3 · (a2)3 · (b4)3 · b2)Factor into cubes.
2= 3√((−3)3) · 3√((a2)3) · 3√((b4)3) · 3√(b2)Product Property of Radicals
3= −3a2b4 · 3√(b2)Simplify.
3√(−27a6b14) = −3a2b4 3√(b2)
Exponent bookkeeping: 6 ÷ 3 = 2 exactly, so a2 comes out and nothing stays. 14 ÷ 3 = 4 remainder 2, so b4 comes out and b2 stays in. No absolute value bars — the index is odd.

Part b — index 2, so look for perfect squares.

1√(75x12y7) = √(52 · 3 · (x6)2 · (y3)2 · y)Factor into squares.
2= √(52) · √3 · √((x6)2) · √((y3)2) · √yProduct Property of Radicals
3= 5x6y3 √(3y)Simplify.
√(75x12y7) = 5x6y3 √(3y)
Think About It! Why are absolute value symbols not necessary around y3, even though it is an odd power produced by an even root? Because the original expression contains √y: for it to be a real number, y ≥ 0, and then y3 ≥ 0 automatically. (x6 is never negative either.)

Watch Out! The index 4 is even, so every odd power that comes out needs absolute value bars: 4√((y3)4) = |y3| and 4√(z4) = |z|. Textbooks often write the denominator as 2z assuming z > 0.

2= 12 · 5√(−1 · 2 · 5 · x2y6 · 16x4y4)Factor the constants.

4= 12 · 5√(−1) · 5√(25) · 5√5 · 5√(x5) · 5√x · 5√(y10)Product Property of Radicals

1A = ½(3√8 + 4)(7√8 + 6√3)Area of a triangle

Simplify 4x/(2√7 − 5).

The denominator has two terms, so multiply the numerator and denominator by the conjugate of 2√7 − 5, which is 2√7 + 5.

1= (4x(2√7 + 5)) ÷ ((2√7 − 5)(2√7 + 5))Multiply by the conjugate.
2= (8x√7 + 20x) ÷ ((2√7)2 + 10√7 − 10√7 − 25)FOIL
3= (8x√7 + 20x) ÷ (28 − 25)Middle terms cancel; (2√7)2 = 4 · 7
4= (8x√7 + 20x) ÷ 3Subtract.
(8x√7 + 20x)/3

Why the middle terms vanish

(A − B)(A + B)
= A2 + AB − AB − B2
= A2 − B2
Squaring kills both radicals.

Numeric check

2√7 − 5 ≈ 0.2915
Let x = 1: 4/0.2915 ≈ 13.72
(8√7 + 20)/3 ≈ (21.17+20)/3
≈ 13.72 ✓
Watch Out! The conjugate flips only the sign between the two terms. The conjugate of 2√7 − 5 is 2√7 + 5 — not −2√7 + 5 and not 5 − 2√7.

Common mistakes to avoid
  • Adding radicands: √a + √b ≠ √(a+b).
  • Combining unlike radicals: 12√(5x) + 2√3 cannot be shortened.
  • Merging different indices: √2 · 3√2 ≠ 3√4.
  • Forgetting absolute value bars when an even index produces an odd power outside.
  • Rationalizing by only part of the term: for 3√(7a) multiply by 3√(49a2), not 3√(a2).
  • Writing the conjugate of 2√7 − 5 as 5 − 2√7 instead of 2√7 + 5.
  • Stopping at 2|ab|4√4 — the index can still be reduced to 2|ab|√2.
