Core Concepts

1. The Pythagorean Theorem

In a right triangle the two short sides that form the right angle are called the legs, and the side opposite the right angle is the hypotenuse. The hypotenuse is always the longest side. The Pythagorean Theorem relates the lengths of the hypotenuse and legs of a right triangle.

Theorem 9.4 · Pythagorean Theorem
In a right triangle, the sum of the squares of the lengths of the legs is equal to the square of the length of the hypotenuse.
a2 + b2 = c2   ←  a, b are legs  ·  c is the hypotenuse
missing hypotenuse: c = √(a2 + b2)
missing leg:          a = √(c2 − b2)

Why the two forms differ: the theorem is one equation, but where the unknown sits changes the arithmetic. If the unknown is the hypotenuse you add two squares; if the unknown is a leg you subtract the leg's square from the hypotenuse's square.

Watch Out! c is never "the last number given" — it is the side opposite the right angle. Find the right-angle mark first, then look straight across from it.

2. Pythagorean Triples

A Pythagorean triple is a set of three nonzero whole numbers a, b, and c such that a2 + b2 = c2. Recognising one lets you write down the missing side with no square roots at all.

Common Pythagorean Triples
3, 4, 55, 12, 138, 15, 177, 24, 25
6, 8, 1010, 24, 2616, 30, 3414, 48, 50
9, 12, 1515, 36, 3924, 45, 5121, 72, 75
3x, 4x, 5x5x, 12x, 13x8x, 15x, 17x7x, 24x, 25x

Every row below the first is found by multiplying each number of the base triple by the same factor. That works because multiplying every side by x multiplies every square by x2, and the equation stays balanced.

Study Tip: to use a triple, divide the two known sides by their common factor. If 14 and 50 are given, both are 2 times something: 2·7 and 2·25. Since 7, 24, 25 is a triple, the missing side is 2·24 = 48.

3. The Converse — testing for a right triangle

The Pythagorean Theorem works in reverse too. If you know all three sides you can decide whether the triangle even is a right triangle.

Theorem 9.5 · Converse of the Pythagorean Theorem
If the sum of the squares of the lengths of the shortest sides of a triangle is equal to the square of the length of the longest side, then the triangle is a right triangle.

Side lengths also classify a triangle as acute or obtuse. In every case you compare c2 (longest side squared) with a2 + b2.

Theorem 9.6 · Acute test
If the square of the length of the longest side of a triangle is less than the sum of the squares of the lengths of the other two sides, then the triangle is an acute triangle.
If c2 < a2 + b2, then △ABC is acute.
Theorem 9.7 · Obtuse test
If the square of the length of the longest side of a triangle is greater than the sum of the squares of the lengths of the other two sides, then the triangle is an obtuse triangle.
If c2 > a2 + b2, then △ABC is obtuse.
Order the three lengths and identify the longest side; call it c.
Check the Triangle Inequality: the two shorter lengths must add to more than the longest, or no triangle exists at all.
Compute c2 and a2 + b2 separately.
Compare: = → right, < → acute, > → obtuse.
Study Tip — Determining the Longest Side: if the sides are radicals, you may wish to use a calculator to decide which is longest. But keep the radicals when you square: (√13)2 = 13 exactly, while 3.6062 only rounds to 13.

Common mistakes to avoid
  • Calling the largest given number the hypotenuse. The hypotenuse is opposite the right angle — it may be the unknown.
  • Adding when you should subtract: if the unknown is a leg, the missing square is subtracted, not added.
  • Writing a + b = c instead of a2 + b2 = c2.
  • Forgetting to take the square root at the end — x2 = 338 is not the answer.
  • Keeping the negative root. Lengths are positive, so discard −√.
  • Classifying before checking the Triangle Inequality: 3, 4, 8 satisfies nothing because it is not a triangle at all.
  • Squaring rounded decimals when classifying — square exact radicals instead.
  • Assuming the converse works on any triangle: it tests whether a triangle is right; it does not assume it.
