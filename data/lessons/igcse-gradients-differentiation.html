A curve does not have one gradient — it has a different gradient at every point. This lesson takes you from drawing a tangent by hand to getting the exact answer by differentiation, and then to finding and classifying stationary points. Drag the chord in Lab 1 until it becomes the tangent: that single picture is what differentiation means.

◎ What you will be able to do

  • Estimate the gradient of a curve by drawing a tangent.
  • Understand gradient as a rate of change, average and instantaneous.
  • Differentiate simple polynomial functions with the power rule.
  • Find stationary points and decide whether each is a maximum or a minimum.

1 The gradient of a curve Interactive

✎
Key rule. The tangent at a point is the straight line that just touches the curve there and runs in the same direction as the curve. The gradient of that tangent is the gradient of the curve at that point.
  • Mark the point on the curve where the gradient is wanted.
  • Lay a ruler so it touches the curve at that point only, matching the direction of the curve. Draw a long tangent.
  • Choose two points on the tangent that are far apart and easy to read.
  • Work out $\text{gradient}=\dfrac{\text{change in }y}{\text{change in }x}$ using those two points.
  • Include units if the axes represent real quantities.
$\text{gradient}=\dfrac{\text{rise}}{\text{run}}=\dfrac{y_{2}-y_{1}}{x_{2}-x_{1}}$

A chord joins two points on the curve. Its gradient gives the average rate of change between them, not the gradient at a single point. As the two ends of the chord slide together, the chord turns into the tangent — and that is the idea behind differentiation.

Lab 1 — chord becomes tangent

Move the point along the curve, then shrink $h$. Watch the chord gradient close in on the tangent gradient — which is exactly what $\frac{dy}{dx}$ gives you without any drawing at all.

!
Watch out. Use two points on your tangent, not two points on the curve, and spread them out — points close together make the reading very inaccurate. A tangent drawn by eye gives an estimate, so a small difference from the exact answer is expected and accepted.

2 Gradient as a rate of change

✎
Key idea. Gradient answers: how fast does $y$ change when $x$ changes? A gradient of 5 means every increase of 1 in $x$ produces an increase of 5 in $y$.
GraphGradient representsUnits

Average rate of change over an interval → gradient of the chord across that interval.
Instantaneous rate of change at one moment → gradient of the tangent there, or $\frac{dy}{dx}$ evaluated at that point.

3 Differentiating polynomials Interactive

Differentiation gives an exact formula for the gradient — no drawing needed. If $y$ is written in terms of $x$, the gradient function is $\frac{dy}{dx}$; if the function is $f(x)$, it is $f'(x)$. They mean the same thing: the derivative.

$y=ax^{n}\ \longrightarrow\ \dfrac{dy}{dx}=anx^{\,n-1}$ — multiply by the power, then reduce the power by one
$y$$\frac{dy}{dx}$Reasoning
$x^{5}$$5x^{4}$Power 5 comes down, power becomes 4
$4x^{3}$$12x^{2}$$4\times3=12$, power 3 → 2
$7x^{2}$$14x$$7\times2=14$, power 2 → 1
$6x$$6$$6\times1=6$, power 1 → 0, and $x^{0}=1$
$-9$$0$A constant graph is horizontal, gradient 0
$x^{2}+3x-4$$2x+3$Differentiate term by term
!
Watch out. Differentiating does not mean "solve". $\frac{dy}{dx}$ is a new expression, still in terms of $x$. To get an actual gradient you must substitute a value of $x$ into it.

4 Stationary points Interactive

✎
Definition. A stationary point (also called a turning point) is a point where the gradient is zero — the tangent there is horizontal.
Stationary points: solve $\dfrac{dy}{dx}=0$
  • Differentiate the function.
  • Set $\frac{dy}{dx}=0$ and solve for $x$ (usually by factorising).
  • Substitute each $x$ back into the original equation to find $y$.
  • Write the answers as coordinate pairs.
  • Decide the nature of each point — maximum or minimum.
!
Watch out. Put $x$ back into the original equation to get $y$, never into $\frac{dy}{dx}$ — that would just give 0 again.
Three ways to decide maximum or minimum
MethodHow it works
Sign of the gradientCheck $\frac{dy}{dx}$ just before and just after: $+,0,-$ → maximum; $-,0,+$ → minimum
Second derivative$\frac{d^{2}y}{dx^{2}}<0$ → maximum  ·  $\frac{d^{2}y}{dx^{2}}>0$ → minimum
Shape of the curveQuadratic: $a>0$ minimum, $a<0$ maximum. Positive cubic: left point is the max, right point the min

✍️ Worked examples

Example 1
Estimating a gradient from a graph
 ⌄

The curve $y=x^{2}$ is drawn. Estimate the gradient at $(2,4)$ by drawing a tangent.

1

Mark the point $(2,4)$ on the curve.

2

Draw a long straight line touching the curve only at that point.

3

Read two well-separated points on the tangent, e.g. $(1,0)$ and $(3,8)$.

4

$\text{gradient}=\dfrac{8-0}{3-1}=\dfrac{8}{2}=4$.

Check by differentiation. $y=x^{2}$ → $\frac{dy}{dx}=2x$ → at $x=2$, gradient $=4$ ✔ — set $y=x^{2}$ and the point to $x=2$ in Lab 1.
Example 2
Differentiating a polynomial
 ⌄

Differentiate $y=3x^{4}-5x^{2}+7x-2$.

$3x^{4}$ → multiply by 4: $3\times4=12$; reduce the power: $12x^{3}$.

$-5x^{2}$ → $-5\times2=-10$; power 2 → 1: $-10x$.

$7x$ → power 1 comes down and disappears: $7$.

$-2$ is a constant → $0$.

Answer. $\dfrac{dy}{dx}=12x^{3}-10x+7$.
Example 3
Gradient at a point by differentiation
 ⌄

Find the gradient of $y=x^{3}-4x$ at the point where $x=-1$.

Differentiate: $\frac{dy}{dx}=3x^{2}-4$. Substitute $x=-1$: $3(-1)^{2}-4=3-4=-1$.

!
$(-1)^{2}=+1$. Always bracket a negative before squaring.
Answer. Gradient $=-1$ — the curve is falling at that point. Choose $y=x^{3}-4x$ in Lab 1 and set the point to $x=-1$ to see the tangent slope down.
Example 4
Stationary point of a quadratic
 ⌄

Find the stationary point of $y=x^{2}-6x+5$ and state its nature.

$\frac{dy}{dx}=2x-6$ → set to zero: $2x-6=0$ → $x=3$.

Substitute into the original: $y=9-18+5=-4$.

Nature: $\frac{d^{2}y}{dx^{2}}=2>0$ → minimum. (Also $a=1>0$, so the parabola opens upwards.)

Answer. Minimum stationary point at $(3,-4)$ — this is Q1 in Lab 3.
Example 5
Stationary points of a cubic
 ⌄

Find the coordinates of the stationary points of $y=x^{3}-3x^{2}-9x+5$ and determine whether each is a maximum or a minimum.

1

Differentiate: $\frac{dy}{dx}=3x^{2}-6x-9$.

2

Set to zero and factorise: $3(x^{2}-2x-3)=3(x-3)(x+1)=0$, so $x=3$ or $x=-1$.

3

Find $y$ from the original: $x=-1$ → $y=-1-3+9+5=10$; $x=3$ → $y=27-27-27+5=-22$.

4

Nature with $\frac{d^{2}y}{dx^{2}}=6x-6$: at $x=-1$, $-12<0$ → maximum; at $x=3$, $12>0$ → minimum.

Answer. Maximum at $(-1,10)$, minimum at $(3,-22)$. Set $a=1$, $b=-3$, $c=-9$, $d=5$ in Lab 2 — the dashed derivative crosses zero at exactly those two x-values.
Example 6
Average versus instantaneous rate
 ⌄

For $y=x^{2}$, find the average rate of change from $x=1$ to $x=3$, and the instantaneous rate at $x=1$.

Average rate — gradient of the chord from $(1,1)$ to $(3,9)$: $\dfrac{9-1}{3-1}=\dfrac{8}{2}=4$.

Instantaneous rate at $x=1$ — use the derivative: $\frac{dy}{dx}=2x$, so at $x=1$ the gradient is $2$.

Answer. Average 4, instantaneous 2. They differ because the curve gets steeper as $x$ increases — the average is pulled up by the steeper right-hand end. Set the point to $x=1$ and $h=2$ in Lab 1 to see both numbers side by side.

! Common mistakes to avoid

  • Reading the gradient from two points on the curve instead of the tangent. That gives the average rate, not the gradient at a point.
  • Forgetting that a constant differentiates to zero. The $-2$ in $3x^{4}-2$ simply disappears.
  • Losing the coefficient. $4x^{3}$ differentiates to $12x^{2}$, not $3x^{2}$ or $4x^{2}$.
  • Substituting into $\frac{dy}{dx}$ to find the y-coordinate. Always go back to the original equation.
  • Giving only the x-value of a stationary point. Answers must be coordinates.
  • Not stating the nature. If the question says "determine whether it is a maximum or minimum", the classification carries marks.
