15.3  The Tangent, Sine and Cosine Ratios

Naming the sides relative to the angle you are using, choosing the right ratio with SOHCAHTOA, and finding missing sides and angles in right-angled triangles.

Labelling the sides — relative to the angle

Trigonometry only works in a right-angled triangle. Before you can choose a ratio you must name the three sides. This is the step that costs more marks than any other in the whole topic, because two of the three names change depending on which angle you are working with.

Definition
  • The hypotenuse (H) is the side opposite the right angle. It is always the longest side, and it never changes, whatever angle you use.
  • The opposite (O) is the side facing the angle you are using — it does not touch it.
  • The adjacent (A) is the side that touches the angle you are using and is not the hypotenuse.

The same triangle, two different angles

AC is the hypotenuse in both pictures. But BC is the opposite for angle A and the adjacent for angle C — they swap over.

The three-step labelling routine

  1. Find the right angle. Label the side facing it H.
  2. Find the angle you are using (the one you know or the one you want). Label the side facing it O.
  3. The remaining side is A.
Watch out Never label the sides before you have decided which angle you are using. In a two-part question — “find BC, then find angle ACB” — you may have to re-label the same triangle half way through.

The three ratios

In every right-angled triangle with the same angle θ, the three possible pairs of sides are always in the same proportion. Those three fixed proportions are called the sine, cosine and tangent of θ.

sin θ = OH     cos θ = AH     tan θ = OA SOH · CAH · TOA

Reading SOHCAHTOA

Sin = Opposite over Hypotenuse
Cos = Adjacent over Hypotenuse
Tan = Opposite over Adjacent

Write the letters down the side of your working every single time. It costs four seconds and saves whole questions.

Choosing the ratio

Mark the side you know and the side you want. Ignore the third side completely — you do not need it.

Finding a missing side

Once the ratio is chosen, the equation always has the unknown either on the top or on the bottom of the fraction. What you do next depends entirely on which.

The equation looks like sin θ = x12. Multiply both sides by the number underneath.

x = 12 × sin θknown side × ratio

The equation looks like tan θ = 7x. The unknown is stuck underneath, so swap it with the ratio and divide.

x = 7tan θknown side ÷ ratio
Example 1In a right-angled triangle the hypotenuse is 12 cm and one angle is 35°. Find the side opposite that angle.
1
Label relative to the 35° angle: the 12 cm side faces the right angle so it is H; the side we want faces 35° so it is O. know H = 12, want O
2
O and H together → SOH, so use sine. sin 35° = x / 12
3
The unknown is on the top, so multiply both sides by 12. x = 12 × sin 35°
x = 12 × 0.573576… = 6.8829…
x = 6.88 cm (3 s.f.)
Exam tip Keep the full display value in your calculator. Rounding sin 35° to 0.57 first gives 6.84 — a wrong answer for an accuracy mark.
Example 2A right-angled triangle has an angle of 40° and the side opposite it is 7 cm. Find the side adjacent to that angle.
1
Relative to 40°: 7 cm is O, the side we want is A. know O = 7, want A
2
O and A together → TOA, so use tangent. tan 40° = 7 / x
3
The unknown is on the bottom. Multiply both sides by x, then divide by tan 40°. x × tan 40° = 7
x = 7 ÷ tan 40°
4
Evaluate in one go. x = 7 ÷ 0.8390996… = 8.3422…
x = 8.34 cm (3 s.f.)
Watch out A quick sense check: 40° is less than 45°, so the opposite must be shorter than the adjacent. 8.34 > 7, so the answer is believable. Had you multiplied by mistake you would have got 5.87, which is too small.

Finding a missing angle

If you know two sides and want the angle, work out the ratio as a number first, then undo the sine, cosine or tangent with the inverse function — the second function on your calculator keys, written sin−1, cos−1, tan−1.

sin θ = 0.625  ⇔  θ = sin−1(0.625) The inverse function answers “which angle has this ratio?”
Watch out sin−1x does not mean 1sin x. It is the inverse operation, like √ undoing a square. Also check your calculator is in degree mode — a D or DEG on the display. In radian mode every answer in this chapter is wrong.
Example 3A right-angled triangle has hypotenuse 8 cm and one side of 5 cm. Find the angle between the hypotenuse and the third side.
1
The 5 cm side faces θ, so it is O; 8 cm faces the right angle, so it is H. O = 5, H = 8 → SOH → sine
2
Write the ratio as a decimal. sin θ = 5 / 8 = 0.625
3
Apply the inverse sine. θ = sin−1(0.625) = 38.6821…°
θ = 38.7° (1 d.p.)
Exam tip Angles are normally asked to 1 decimal place; lengths to 3 significant figures. If the question says nothing, 3 s.f. is always accepted.

The exact values you should know

Two special triangles generate all the exact values on the syllabus: half a square gives 45°, and half an equilateral triangle gives 30° and 60°.

The two special triangles

30°12√321√3 = √33
45°1√2 = √221√2 = √221
60°√3212√3

If a question says “give your answer in exact form” or “in surd form”, it is telling you the angle is 30°, 45° or 60° and that no calculator rounding is allowed. For example, with an adjacent of 6 cm and an angle of 60°: cos 60° = 6h, so h = 6 ÷ ½ = 12 cm exactly, and the opposite is 6 tan 60° = 6√3 cm.

Angles of elevation and depression

Definition The angle of elevation is measured upwards from the horizontal to your line of sight. The angle of depression is measured downwards from the horizontal to your line of sight. Both are measured from the horizontal, never from a vertical wall or mast.

The two angles, and why depression is easy to get wrong

Exam tip The angle of depression is outside the triangle you want to use. Because the dashed horizontal at the top is parallel to the ground, the angle at the boat equals the angle of depression (alternate angles). Drop it into the triangle and carry on as normal.
Example 4Amira stands 47 m from the foot of a vertical tower. The angle of elevation of the top of the tower is 32°. Find (a) the height of the tower, (b) the distance from Amira to the top.
1
(a) Relative to 32°: 47 m is A, the height is O. O and A → tangent. tan 32° = h / 47
2
Unknown on top, so multiply. h = 47 × tan 32° = 47 × 0.624869… = 29.3688…
3
(b) Now the 47 m is still A but we want H → cosine. cos 32° = 47 / d
4
Unknown on the bottom, so divide. d = 47 ÷ cos 32° = 47 ÷ 0.848048… = 55.4213…
(a) h = 29.4 m    (b) d = 55.4 m (3 s.f.)
Check Pythagoras on the unrounded values: √(47² + 29.3688²) = √3071.5 = 55.42 ✓. Notice the hypotenuse is the longest side, as it must be.

Trigonometry or Pythagoras?

What the question gives youWhat to use
Two sides, want the third sidePythagoras a² + b² = c²
Two sides, want an angleInverse trig: sin−1, cos−1, tan−1
One side and an angle, want another sidesin, cos or tan

In short: no angle mentioned anywhere → Pythagoras. As soon as an angle appears, trigonometry is faster and usually the only route.

More worked examples

Example 5From the top of a vertical cliff 85 m high, the angle of depression of a boat at sea is 24°. How far is the boat from the foot of the cliff?
1
The 24° is between the horizontal at the top and the line of sight, so it sits outside the triangle. The horizontal is parallel to the sea, so the angle at the boat is also 24° (alternate angles). angle at boat = 24°
2
Now label relative to that 24°: the 85 m cliff faces it, so it is O; the distance d touches it, so it is A. O = 85, want A → TOA
3
Write the equation. The unknown is on the bottom, so divide. tan 24° = 85 / d
d = 85 ÷ tan 24° = 85 ÷ 0.445228…
d = 191 m (3 s.f.)
Watch out The commonest error is using 24° as the angle at the top inside the triangle, giving 85 tan 24° = 37.8 m. If you prefer, use the angle at the top directly — but then it is 90 − 24 = 66°, and 85 tan 66° = 190.9 m ✓.
Example 6In triangle PQR, angle Q = 90°, PQ = 8.2 cm and QR = 11.5 cm. Find (a) PR, (b) angle PRQ.
1
(a) Two sides given, third side wanted, no angle involved — that is Pythagoras, not trigonometry. PR faces the right angle, so PR is the hypotenuse. PR² = 8.2² + 11.5²
PR² = 67.24 + 132.25 = 199.49
2
Square root. PR = √199.49 = 14.1240…
3
(b) Now an angle is wanted from two known sides. Relative to angle R: PQ = 8.2 is O, QR = 11.5 is A. tan R = 8.2 / 11.5 = 0.713043…
4
Inverse tangent. R = tan−1(0.713043…) = 35.4905…°
(a) PR = 14.1 cm    (b) angle PRQ = 35.5°
Exam tip Use the two given sides for part (b), never your rounded answer from part (a). Using PR = 14.1 with sine gives 35.5° here, but on other questions that error loses the mark.
Example 7Triangle ABC is isosceles with AB = AC = 9.5 cm and BC = 12 cm. Find (a) angle BAC, (b) the perpendicular height from A to BC.
1
There is no right angle yet. Drop the perpendicular from A to the midpoint M of BC — in an isosceles triangle it bisects both the base and the apex angle. BM = MC = 12 ÷ 2 = 6 cm
2
(a) Work in right-angled triangle ABM. Relative to angle BAM: BM = 6 is O, AB = 9.5 is H → SOH. sin(BAM) = 6 / 9.5 = 0.631578…
BAM = sin−1(0.631578…) = 39.1667…°
3
That is only half of angle BAC, so double it. BAC = 2 × 39.1667… = 78.3334…°
4
(b) For the height, two sides of triangle ABM are known and no angle is needed — use Pythagoras. h² = 9.5² − 6² = 90.25 − 36 = 54.25
h = √54.25 = 7.36545…
(a) angle BAC = 78.3°    (b) h = 7.37 cm (3 s.f.)
Method note You could also get the height from h = 9.5 × cos 39.1667° = 7.365 cm. Pythagoras is safer because it uses only the numbers printed in the question.

Your turn

Practice set

  1. In triangle ABC, angle B = 90°. Using angle C, name the opposite, the adjacent and the hypotenuse.
  2. A right-angled triangle has hypotenuse 15 cm and an angle of 52°. Find the side opposite the 52° angle.
  3. In a right-angled triangle, the side adjacent to a 63° angle is 6 cm. Find the hypotenuse.
  4. A right-angled triangle has hypotenuse 14 cm and an adjacent side of 9 cm. Find the angle between them.
  5. Write down the exact value of tan 60°, and use it to find the side opposite a 60° angle when the adjacent side is 7 cm. Leave your answer in surd form.
  6. A ladder 4.5 m long leans against a vertical wall with its foot 1.6 m from the base of the wall. Find (a) the angle the ladder makes with the ground, (b) how far up the wall it reaches.

  1. Hypotenuse = AC (it faces the right angle at B); opposite angle C = AB; adjacent to angle C = BC.
  2. O and H → sine. sin 52° = x/15 → x = 15 sin 52° → 11.8 cm
  3. A and H → cosine. cos 63° = 6/x, unknown on the bottom → x = 6 ÷ cos 63° → 13.2 cm
  4. A and H → cosine. cos θ = 9/14 = 0.642857… → θ = cos−1(0.642857…) → 50.0°
  5. tan 60° = √3, so O = 7 tan 60° → 7√3 cm (≈ 12.1 cm).
  6. (a) The ladder is H = 4.5, the ground distance is A = 1.6 → cosine. cos θ = 1.6/4.5 → 69.2°. (b) Two sides known, no angle needed → Pythagoras: √(4.5² − 1.6²) = √17.69 → 4.21 m

Key points to remember

  • The hypotenuse always faces the right angle. Opposite and adjacent depend on which angle you are using — label them again for every new part of a question.
  • SOH CAH TOA: sin θ = O/H, cos θ = A/H, tan θ = O/A.
  • Choose the ratio from the side you know and the side you want; the third side is irrelevant.
  • Unknown on the top → multiply. Unknown on the bottom → divide the known side by the ratio.
  • Two sides and you want the angle → work out the ratio as a decimal, then use sin−1, cos−1 or tan−1. Keep the calculator in degree mode.
  • Lengths to 3 significant figures, angles to 1 decimal place, and never round part-way through a calculation.
  • Angles of elevation and depression are measured from the horizontal; a depression angle equals the alternate angle inside the triangle.
  • No angle anywhere in the question → use Pythagoras, not trigonometry.
  • Exact values worth memorising: sin 30° = cos 60° = ½, tan 45° = 1, tan 60° = √3.
