3.6  Construction

Ruler-and-compasses constructions of triangles, bisectors and perpendiculars, and the four standard loci used to shade a region.

Equipment, accuracy and arcs

Construction questions are marks you can bank, because there is no algebra to go wrong — only care. Bring the right kit and use it properly.

What you need

  • A ruler marked in millimetres.
  • A pair of compasses stiff enough to hold its setting — tighten the screw.
  • A sharp pencil (HB). A blunt pencil costs you the tolerance.
  • A protractor, for angles that are given to you.

How accurate must you be?

  • Lengths within ±2 mm of the correct value.
  • Angles within ±2°.
  • Always draw a rough sketch first so you know where the shape will sit on the page.
  • Work in pencil, not pen — but never rub out your arcs.
Watch out “Using ruler and compasses only” means the protractor stays in the case. A bisector measured with a protractor scores zero, even if the line is in exactly the right place.

The golden rule: leave your arcs showing

Exam tip The arcs are the evidence. The mark scheme says “correct arcs”, so a perfect answer with no arcs can only score the accuracy mark, not the method mark. Draw them clearly and leave them alone.

Constructing triangles

There are three sets of information that fix a triangle exactly. Learn which tool opens each one.

You are givenNameToolsMethod in one line
Three sidesSSSruler + compassesDraw the longest side, then swing an arc from each end.
Two sides and the angle between themSASruler + protractorDraw one side, measure the angle, mark the second side along it.
Two angles and a sideASAruler + protractorDraw the side, draw both angles at its ends, extend until the rays meet.
Example 1Construct triangle ABC with AB = 7 cm, AC = 6 cm and BC = 5 cm. Measure angle BAC.
1
Draw the longest side accurately with the ruler and label its ends. AB = 7.0 cm
2
Open the compasses to 6 cm against the ruler. With the point on A, draw an arc above the line.
3
Open the compasses to 5 cm. With the point on B, draw a second arc that crosses the first. The crossing point is C.
4
Join AC and BC with the ruler. Leave both arcs on the page.
5
Measure angle BAC with a protractor. angle BAC ≈ 44°
C is where the arcs cross; angle BAC = 44° (accept 42° to 46°)
Note Starting with the longest side keeps the arcs crossing at a good wide angle, which makes the point C easy to see.
Example 2Construct triangle PQR with PQ = 6 cm, angle P = 50° and PR = 4.5 cm. Measure QR.
1
Draw PQ = 6 cm and label both ends.
2
Put the centre of the protractor exactly on P with the base line along PQ, and mark 50° with a fine dot.
3
Rule a line from P through that dot, and measure 4.5 cm along it to fix R.
4
Join QR and measure it. QR ≈ 4.6 cm
QR = 4.6 cm (accept 4.4 cm to 4.8 cm)
Watch out Protractors have two scales. Angle P = 50° is clearly acute, so if your line looks obtuse you have read the wrong row of numbers.

The three compass constructions

These three appear again and again, both on their own and inside locus questions. In every one you keep the compasses at the same setting for a pair of arcs — that is what makes the construction exact.

Perpendicular bisector of AB

  1. Open the compasses to more than half of AB.
  2. Centre A: arcs above and below the line.
  3. Same radius, centre B: two more arcs, crossing the first pair.
  4. Join the two crossing points. It cuts AB in half at 90°.

Bisector of an angle

  1. Centre the vertex V: one arc crossing both arms, at P and Q.
  2. Centre P: an arc inside the angle.
  3. Same radius, centre Q: an arc crossing it at R.
  4. Join VR. The angle is now split into two equal parts.
Key idea Both constructions work because the arcs build a rhombus. Every side of a rhombus is equal, and the diagonals of a rhombus bisect each other at right angles and bisect its angles. That is the whole proof.
Example 3Construct the perpendicular from the point P to the line L, and hence find the shortest distance from P to L.
1
Put the compass point on P and open it wide enough to reach past the line. Draw an arc that cuts L twice, at X and Y.
2
Now construct the perpendicular bisector of XY: equal arcs from X and from Y, crossing below the line.
3
Join P to that crossing point. Because PX = PY, the line passes through P and meets L at 90°.
4
Measure from P to the foot of the perpendicular. That length is the shortest distance, because any other route to the line is the hypotenuse of a right-angled triangle and so is longer.
The shortest distance from a point to a line is always measured at 90° to the line

Loci

Definition A locus (plural loci) is the set of all the points that satisfy a rule. Read the rule, decide which of the four standard pictures it is, then draw it.

1. A fixed distance from a point → a circle

“Exactly 3 cm from P” is the circle itself; “less than 3 cm from P” is the region inside it.

2. A fixed distance from a line → a racetrack

Two parallel lines a distance d away, joined by a semicircle at each end — the ends are the bit students forget.

3. Equidistant from two points → perpendicular bisector

“Nearer to A than to B” is the side of that line containing A.

4. Equidistant from two lines → angle bisector

“Nearer to one wall than the other” means the angle bisector of the corner where they meet.

Shading a region that satisfies two or more conditions

  1. Draw each locus separately, in pencil, with construction arcs showing.
  2. Decide which side of each boundary you want, one condition at a time.
  3. Shade only the overlap, and label it R.
  4. Use a solid boundary when the condition allows equality (“not more than 4 m”) and a dashed one when it does not (“less than 4 m”).

More worked examples

Example 4Construct triangle ABC with BC = 7 cm, angle B = 40° and angle C = 65°. Measure AB.
1
Draw the given side first, because both angles sit on it. BC = 7.0 cm
2
At B measure 40° and rule a long ray. Make it longer than you think you need.
3
At C measure 65° on the same side of BC and rule another long ray.
4
Where the rays cross is A. Measure AB. AB ≈ 6.6 cm
5
Check with the angle sum before you leave the question. angle A = 180 − 40 − 65 = 75°
AB = 6.6 cm (accept 6.4 cm to 6.8 cm)
Example 5Two straight paths meet at a corner V. A bench must be the same distance from both paths and exactly 5 m from V. Describe how to find its position.
1
“Same distance from both paths” is the locus equidistant from two lines, so construct the bisector of angle V with compasses.
2
“Exactly 5 m from V” is the locus a fixed distance from a point, so draw an arc of radius 5 m centred on V, using the scale of the drawing.
3
The bench is where the two loci cross. Mark it clearly with a cross and label it.
The bench is at the point where the angle bisector meets the arc of radius 5 m from V
Exam tip When a question says “equidistant”, it is telling you which construction to use. Two points → perpendicular bisector. Two lines → angle bisector. Learn that pairing and these questions become quick.
Example 6A goat is tied by a 4 m rope to a post at the corner A of a large field. A second post at B, 6 m from A, has a second goat on a 4 m rope. Shade the region that both goats can reach.
1
Each rope gives a locus “not more than 4 m from a point”, which is the region inside a circle of radius 4 m. Draw both circles with compasses, using the scale given.
2
“Both goats” means the region must satisfy both conditions at once, so you want the overlap of the two circles.
3
Shade only that lens-shaped overlap and label it R. Draw the boundaries solid, because the goat can reach exactly 4 m.
R is the lens-shaped overlap of the two circles
Watch out If the posts had been more than 8 m apart the circles would not meet at all and the answer would be “no such region”. Always check the two loci really do cross.
Example 7A rectangular field ABCD has AB = 80 m and AD = 60 m, drawn to a scale of 1 cm to 10 m. Shade the region R that is less than 30 m from A and nearer to AB than to AD. Find the real area of R.
1
Convert to the scale of the drawing first — everything you draw is in centimetres. 80 m → 8 cm,   60 m → 6 cm,   30 m → 3 cm
2
“Less than 30 m from A”: draw an arc of radius 3 cm centred on A. You want the inside, so the boundary is dashed.
3
“Nearer to AB than to AD” is equidistant-from-two-lines, so construct the bisector of angle A. As A is a right angle, the bisector runs at 45°. You want the side towards AB.
4
Shade the overlap: a sector of angle 45° and radius 30 m, and label it R.
5
Its real area is that fraction of a full circle of radius 30 m. A = 45360 × π × 30² = 18 × 2827.43… = 353.429…
R is a 45° sector of radius 30 m, area = 353 m² (3 s.f.)
Exam tip Do the drawing in centimetres and the area calculation in metres. Converting at the start, and writing the scale conversion down, stops you finding the area of the drawing instead of the field.

Your turn

Practice set

  1. Which construction gives the set of points the same distance from two fixed points A and B?
  2. Describe the locus of points exactly 5 cm from a fixed point O, and the locus of points less than 5 cm from O.
  3. Construct triangle XYZ with XY = 8 cm, XZ = 5 cm and YZ = 6 cm. Which side would you draw first, and why?
  4. A rectangular lawn is 10 m by 6 m. Describe the locus of points inside the lawn that are exactly 2 m from its longest side.
  5. Explain why the perpendicular bisector construction needs the compasses opened to more than half of AB.
  6. A park is a rectangle PQRS with PQ = 60 m and PS = 40 m. A fountain must be nearer to P than to Q, and not more than 25 m from PS. Describe the two boundaries you would draw and the region you would shade.

  1. The perpendicular bisector of AB — two points, so bisect the line joining them.
  2. Exactly 5 cm is a circle of radius 5 cm, centre O; less than 5 cm is the region inside that circle (boundary dashed, as 5 cm itself is not included).
  3. XY = 8 cm first, because it is the longest side; the arcs of radius 5 cm from X and 6 cm from Y then cross cleanly at Z.
  4. A straight line 2 m from that side and parallel to it, 10 m long. As the lawn is only 6 m wide there are two such lines, one on each side of the centre.
  5. If the radius were less than half of AB the two arcs would never meet, so there would be no crossing points to join. Equal radii also guarantee the four points form a rhombus.
  6. Draw the perpendicular bisector of PQ (keep the half containing P) and a line parallel to PS and 25 m from it (keep the side towards PS); shade the overlap and label it R. The second boundary is solid because 25 m is allowed.

Key points to remember

  • Never rub out your construction arcs — they carry the method marks.
  • SSS uses compasses; SAS and ASA use a protractor. “Ruler and compasses only” bans the protractor completely.
  • Draw the longest side first for SSS, and keep lengths within ±2 mm and angles within ±2°.
  • Perpendicular bisector: equal arcs from each end, radius more than half the line; join the two crossings.
  • Angle bisector: one arc across both arms, then equal arcs from those two points; join to the vertex.
  • Loci: fixed distance from a point is a circle; from a line it is two parallels with semicircular ends; equidistant from two points is the perpendicular bisector; from two lines it is the angle bisector.
  • For a region, draw every boundary, decide which side of each you want, shade only the overlap and label it R.
  • The shortest distance from a point to a line is always the perpendicular distance.
