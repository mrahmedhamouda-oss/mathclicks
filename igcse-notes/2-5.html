2.5  Indices

The laws of indices applied to algebraic terms, including zero, negative and fractional powers, and solving equations by matching bases.

Index notation in algebra

An index (plural indices) is a shorthand for repeated multiplication. Instead of writing x × x × x × x you write x4. The number being multiplied is the base; the small raised number is the index or power.

The parts of a term

Watch out The index attaches only to the letter immediately in front of it. 3x² means 3 × x × x, whereas (3x)² means 3x × 3x = 9x². Test with x = 2: 3x² = 12 but (3x)² = 36. The bracket changes everything.

The same distinction decides whether terms can be added or must be multiplied:

ExpressionSimplifies toWhy
5x² + 3x²8x²Like terms — add the coefficients, index unchanged
5x² × 3x²15x4Multiply coefficients, add the indices
x² + x³will not simplifyNot like terms — leave it alone
4a³b²4 × a × a × a × b × bEach letter carries its own index

The laws of indices

Every law below is just a shortcut for writing the multiplication out in full. If you ever forget one, expand a small case and read the answer off.

LawRuleAlgebraic example
Multiplyingam × an = am+nx³ × x² = x5
Dividingam ÷ an = am−nx5 ÷ x² = x³
Power of a power(am)n = amn(x³)² = x6
Power of a product(ab)n = anbn(2x)³ = 8x³
Power of a quotient(ab)n = anbn(x3)² = x²9
Zero indexa0 = 1  (a ≠ 0)7x0 = 7
Where the first three come from x³ × x² = (xxx)(xx) = x5 — you are just counting xs. Dividing cancels them: x5 ÷ x² = xxxxxxx = x³. And (x³)² = x³ × x³ = x6.
Watch out The laws work only when the bases match. You cannot combine 2³ × 3² into a single power, and x² + x³ is not x5 — the adding law is for multiplying terms, never for adding them.

Index-law simplifier

Build two terms, choose × or ÷, and see which law is used at each stage. Try 12a5b² ÷ 4a²b by entering 12, 5, 2 and 4, 2, 1 with ÷.

Example 1Simplify (a) 5x³ × 4x4  (b) 20a6 ÷ 5a²  (c) (2x5)³.
1
(a) Deal with the numbers and the letters separately. Multiply the coefficients, add the indices. 5 × 4 = 20    x³ × x4 = x3+4 = x7
2
(b) Divide the coefficients, subtract the indices. 20 ÷ 5 = 4    a6 ÷ a² = a6−2 = a4
3
(c) The index outside applies to everything inside the bracket — the 2 as well as the x5. 2³ = 8    (x5)³ = x15
(a) 20x7    (b) 4a4    (c) 8x15
Common error In (c), writing 2x15 forgets to cube the coefficient. Check with x = 2: (2 × 32)³ = 64³ = 262144, and 8 × 215 = 8 × 32768 = 262144.
Example 2Simplify (3x²y)³.
1
Use (abc)n = anbncn: cube each factor inside the bracket. (3x²y)³ = 3³ × (x²)³ × y³
2
Evaluate the number and use the power-of-a-power law. Remember y = y1. 3³ = 27    (x²)³ = x6    (y1)³ = y³
27x6y³
Check With x = 2, y = 3: (3 × 4 × 3)³ = 36³ = 46656, and 27 × 64 × 27 = 46656.

Zero, negative and fractional indices

The three laws above force the meaning of these indices — they are not arbitrary definitions.

Zero index

x5 ÷ x5 = 1, but the law gives x5−5 = x0.

a0 = 1

So 5x0 = 5 × 1 = 5, but (5x)0 = 1.

Negative index

x² ÷ x5 = 1x³, and the law gives x−3.

a−n = 1an

A negative index means reciprocal, not a negative answer.

Fractional index

a1/2 × a1/2 = a1, so a1/2 must be the square root.

a1/2 = a    a1/3 = 3a    am/n = (na)m

Bottom of the fraction is the root, top is the power.

Exam tip For am/n always take the root first, then the power — the numbers stay small. 82/3 = (38)² = 2² = 4 is far easier than working out 8² = 64 and then cube-rooting it.

Solving index equations

When the unknown sits in the index, you cannot solve by the usual balancing moves. Instead write both sides as powers of the same base. Once the bases match, the indices must be equal.

If ap = aq then p = q Valid for any base a > 0, a ≠ 1

Straightforward: 2x = 32

Write 32 as a power of 2: 32 = 25.

2x = 25, so x = 5.

Reciprocal: 5x = 1125

1125 = 15³ = 5−3.

So x = −3.

Exam tip Learn the small powers by heart — 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64, 128; 3, 9, 27, 81; 5, 25, 125. Spotting that both 9 and 27 are powers of 3 is the whole question.

Common-base solver

Enter a and b to solve ax = b. Fractions are allowed on the right, e.g. 1/125. Try a = 9, b = 27.

Example 4Solve 9x = 27.
1
9 and 27 are not powers of each other, but both are powers of 3. That is the common base. 9 = 3²    27 = 3³
2
Rewrite the left-hand side and use (am)n = amn. 9x = (3²)x = 32x
3
Bases now match, so the indices are equal. 32x = 3³  →  2x = 3
x = 32 = 1.5
Check 91.5 = (√9)³ = 3³ = 27.

1

Key points to remember

  • am × an = am+n, am ÷ an = am−n, (am)n = amn — only when the bases are the same.
  • With algebraic terms, handle the coefficients separately from the letters: 12a5b² ÷ 4a²b = 3a³b.
  • (ab)n = anbn — an index outside a bracket applies to the coefficient too: (3x²y)³ = 27x6y³.
  • a0 = 1 for any non-zero a, so 5x0 = 5 but (5x)0 = 1.
  • a−n = 1an — a negative index means reciprocal, never a negative answer.
  • am/n: the bottom is the root, the top is the power. Take the root first to keep the numbers small.
  • To solve an equation with the unknown in the index, write both sides to a common base, then equate the indices.
  • Adding is not multiplying: x² + x³ does not simplify, but x² × x³ = x5.
