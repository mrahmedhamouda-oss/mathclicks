6.3  Rearranging Formulae

Changing the subject of a formula — from one-step rearrangements to powers, roots, fractions and the case where the new subject appears twice.

The subject Powers & roots Fractions Subject twice Examples Your turn Key points

What “make x the subject” means

In the formula v = u + at the letter v is the subject: it stands alone on one side and appears nowhere else. Rearranging, or transforming, a formula means rewriting it so that a different letter becomes the subject.

Definition A letter is the subject of a formula when it appears once only, on its own, on one side of the equals sign. Making x the subject means finishing with x = … where the right-hand side contains no x.

The method is exactly the balance method of lesson 6.1 — do the same thing to both sides — but now you are working with letters instead of numbers, so you cannot simplify as you go. That is the only real difference.

The reverse flowchart

Read the formula as a machine that builds up the subject, then run the machine backwards using inverse operations.

Building y = 5x + 3, and undoing it

y = 5x + 3   →   x = y − 35 Undo the + 3 first, then the × 5
Example 1Make a the subject of v = u + at.
1
Find the term containing a and get it on its own: subtract u from both sides. v − u = at
2
a is multiplied by t, so divide both sides by t. The whole of v − u goes over the fraction line. a = v − ut
3
Check with numbers. Take u = 5, a = 3, t = 4; then v = 5 + 12 = 17. 17 − 54 = 124 = 3  ✓
a = v − ut
Watch out v − ut is not the same as v − ut. Dividing by t divides every term on that side.

Powers and roots

Squaring and square-rooting are inverses of each other, so they undo each other — on both sides, and only when the side is a single term.

To undo a square

Take the square root of both sides. In a formula from a real context (a length, a time, a radius) you take the positive root only; in a pure algebra question write ±.

To undo a square root

Square both sides. Square everything on each side, so (2√k)² = 4k — the 2 gets squared too.

Example 2The area of a circle is A = πr². Make r the subject, and find the radius of a circle of area 50 cm².
1
Divide both sides by π to isolate r². r² = Aπ
2
Square root both sides. A radius is a length, so only the positive root makes sense. r = A/π
3
Substitute A = 50. Keep the full calculator value until the end. r = √(50 ÷ π) = √15.9154… = 3.9894…
4
Check: square it and multiply by π. π × 3.9894…² = 50  ✓
r = A/π     r = 3.99 cm (3 s.f.)
Exam tip Unless the question says otherwise, give a numerical answer to 3 significant figures and do not round any intermediate value.

When the subject sits in a fraction

Two situations, one idea: multiply both sides by the denominator to lift everything back onto the line.

Subject on top

y = x + 14 → multiply by 4 → 4y = x + 1. The bracket round x + 1 is already implied by the fraction line.

Subject underneath

y = 6x → multiply by x → xy = 6 → divide by y → x = 6y.

Example 3Make x the subject of (a) y = 4x − 53  (b) y = 12x − 4.
1
(a) Multiply both sides by 3 — the fraction line acts as a bracket, so the whole numerator comes down. 3y = 4x − 5
2
Add 5, then divide by 4. 3y + 5 = 4x
x = 3y + 54
3
Check with x = 5: y = (20 − 5) ÷ 3 = 5. (3 × 5 + 5) ÷ 4 = 20 ÷ 4 = 5  ✓
4
(b) Here x is in the denominator, so multiply both sides by (x − 4) first. y(x − 4) = 12
5
Divide by y, then add 4. (Do not expand the bracket — there is only one x, so dividing is quicker.) x − 4 = 12y
x = 12y + 4
6
Check with x = 10: y = 12 ÷ 6 = 2. 12 ÷ 2 + 4 = 6 + 4 = 10  ✓
(a) x = 3y + 54     (b) x = 12y + 4 = 12 + 4yy

When the new subject appears twice

This is the Extended-tier case, and it is worth learning as a fixed routine because the marks are almost always awarded for the same four moves.

The routine

  1. Clear all fractions — multiply by every denominator.
  2. Expand any brackets.
  3. Collect every term containing the new subject on one side, everything else on the other.
  4. Factorise the new subject out, then divide by the bracket.
Example 4Make x the subject of y = 2x + 3x − 1.
1
Multiply both sides by (x − 1) to clear the fraction. y(x − 1) = 2x + 3
2
Expand the left-hand side. x now appears twice, once on each side. xy − y = 2x + 3
3
Collect the x terms on the left and everything else on the right. xy − 2x = 3 + y
4
Factorise — take x out as the common factor. This is the step examiners look for. x(y − 2) = y + 3
5
Divide both sides by the whole bracket. x = y + 3y − 2
6
Check with x = 4: y = 11 ÷ 3. 11/3 + 311/3 − 2 = 20/35/3 = 4  ✓
x = y + 3y − 2
Watch out You cannot cancel the ys in that answer. Only factors cancel, and y is a term here, not a factor.

More worked examples

Example 5Make a the subject of s = ut + ½at².
1
Only the second term contains a, so subtract ut from both sides. s − ut = ½at²
2
Multiply both sides by 2 to clear the half. 2(s − ut) = at²
3
Divide both sides by t². a = 2(s − ut)t²
4
Check with u = 3, t = 4, a = 2, which give s = 12 + ½(2)(16) = 28. 2(28 − 12)16 = 3216 = 2  ✓
a = 2(s − ut)t²
Note 2s − 2utt² is the same answer written differently, and is equally acceptable. What is not acceptable is cancelling one t from 2ut and t² while the 2s is still there.
Example 6The period of a pendulum is T = 2πl/g. Make l the subject.
1
Get the root on its own: divide both sides by 2π. T2π = l/g
2
Square both sides. Remember the whole left-hand side is squared, so the 2π becomes 4π². T²4π² = lg
3
Multiply both sides by g. l = gT²4π²
4
Check with l = 1 and g = 9.8, which give T = 2π√(1/9.8) = 2.0071… 9.8 × 2.0071…²4π² = 39.478…39.478… = 1  ✓
l = gT²4π²
Watch out Squaring 2πk gives 4π²k, not 2π²k. Every factor outside the root is squared.
Example 7Make x the subject of y = (x + 1)/(x − 1).
1
Square both sides to remove the root. y² = x + 1x − 1
2
Multiply both sides by (x − 1). y²(x − 1) = x + 1
3
Expand. xy² − y² = x + 1
4
Collect the x terms on one side, the rest on the other. xy² − x = 1 + y²
5
Factorise out x and divide. x(y² − 1) = y² + 1
x = y² + 1y² − 1
6
Check with x = 3: y = √(4/2) = √2, so y² = 2. 2 + 12 − 1 = 3  ✓
x = y² + 1y² − 1
Exam tip Treat y² as a single block. If it helps, write k = y², rearrange to x = (k + 1)/(k − 1), then put y² back at the very end.

Your turn

Practice set

  1. Make c the subject of P = a + b + c.
  2. Make h the subject of A = ½bh.
  3. Make r the subject of V = πr²h.
  4. Make x the subject of y = x − 47 + 2.
  5. Make t the subject of s = 6t + 1.
  6. Make x the subject of y = 3x − 1x + 2.

  1. Subtract a and b from both sides → c = P − a − b
  2. 2A = bh → divide by b → h = 2Ab
  3. r² = Vπh → r = V/(πh) (positive root, since r is a length)
  4. y − 2 = x − 47 → 7(y − 2) = x − 4 → x = 7y − 10 (check x = 11: y = 1 + 2 = 3 and 21 − 10 = 11)
  5. s(t + 1) = 6 → t + 1 = 6s → t = 6s − 1
  6. y(x + 2) = 3x − 1 → xy + 2y = 3x − 1 → 2y + 1 = 3x − xy → 2y + 1 = x(3 − y) → x = 2y + 13 − y (check x = 1: y = 23, and 4/3 + 13 − 2/3 = 7/37/3 = 1)

Key points to remember

  • The new subject must end up alone, on one side, appearing only once.
  • Use the same balance method as for equations: the same operation on both sides, undoing operations with their inverses in reverse order.
  • Dividing by a letter divides every term on that side — keep the numerator together, using a bracket or the fraction line.
  • Square to undo a root; square-root to undo a square. Anything multiplying a root gets squared too: (2πk)² = 4π²k.
  • If the subject is in a denominator, multiply both sides by that denominator first.
  • If the subject appears twice: clear fractions, expand, collect those terms on one side, factorise and divide by the bracket.
  • Only factors cancel in a fraction, never individual terms.
  • Check any rearrangement by putting simple numbers into the original and into your new version — they must give the same value.
