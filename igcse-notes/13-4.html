13.4  Conversion Graphs

Drawing and reading straight-line graphs that convert one quantity into another, in both directions, accurately and quickly.

The idea Reading off Gradient Not proportional Examples Your turn Key points

What a conversion graph is

A conversion graph is a straight line that lets you swap between two units without doing any arithmetic at all. Put one quantity on each axis, draw the line once, and every reading afterwards is free. Paper 2 and Paper 4 both use them — sometimes you draw the graph, more often you are given one and asked to read values off it.

Definition Two quantities are in direct proportion when doubling one doubles the other, and when zero of one means zero of the other. Their conversion graph is a straight line through the origin.

That last sentence is the whole reason the graph is straight. If 5 miles is 8 km, then 10 miles must be 16 km and 0 miles is certainly 0 km. Every point sits on the line y = mx, where m is the number of km in one mile.

A conversion graph for miles and kilometres

Example 1Given that 5 miles = 8 km, draw a conversion graph for distances from 0 to 50 miles.
1
Miles and kilometres are in direct proportion, so the graph is a straight line through the origin. That gives your first point for free. Point A: (0, 0)
2
A straight line needs only one more point, and the best one to choose is at the far end of the axis — a long line is much more accurate to draw than a short one. Scale the given conversion up by ×10. 5 miles = 8 km
50 miles = 80 km
3
Choose sensible scales so the graph fills the grid: miles across from 0 to 50, kilometres up from 0 to 80. Plot (0, 0) and (50, 80) and join them with a ruler, extending the line right across the grid. Point B: (50, 80)
A straight line from (0, 0) to (50, 80)
Exam tip Plot a third point as a check — here (25, 40). If it does not lie on your line, one of your points is wrong.
Watch out Do not join the points with a freehand wobble or a series of short strokes. Marks are given for a single ruled line that reaches both edges of the grid, because you will need to read values right up to the ends.

Reading values off the graph

The examiner wants to see how you got your reading. The method has a name in every mark scheme: draw the lines on the graph.

Miles → km (left to right)

  1. Find the number of miles on the horizontal axis.
  2. Draw a dotted line straight up until it meets the graph.
  3. Turn and draw a dotted line straight across to the vertical axis.
  4. Read the value where it lands.

km → miles (right to left)

  1. Find the number of kilometres on the vertical axis.
  2. Draw a dotted line straight across until it meets the graph.
  3. Turn and draw a dotted line straight down to the horizontal axis.
  4. Read the value where it lands.
Exam tip Leave the dotted lines on the paper. If your final number is slightly outside the accepted range but your construction lines are correct, you can still pick up the method mark.
Example 2Use the miles–kilometres graph above to convert (a) 30 miles into kilometres, (b) 60 km into miles.
1
(a) Start on the miles axis at 30. Go vertically up to the line, then horizontally across to the kilometre axis. 30 miles → 48 km
2
Check it against the rate: one mile is 8 ÷ 5 = 1.6 km. 30 × 1.6 = 48  ✓
3
(b) Now work the other way. Start on the kilometre axis at 60, go horizontally across to the line, then vertically down to the miles axis. The reading falls halfway between 37 and 38. 60 km → 37.5 miles
4
Check by converting back. 37.5 × 1.6 = 60  ✓
(a) 48 km     (b) 37.5 miles

How accurate is a reading?

A graph reading is only as good as your eye and your ruler. Mark schemes normally allow a tolerance of about half a small square either side of the true value, so on a grid where one square is 2 km you would be accepted anywhere within about ±1 km.

Watch out Never write a graph reading to lots of decimal places. Quoting “47.83 km” from a graph claims an accuracy you cannot possibly have. Read to the nearest sensible division — 48 km — unless the gridlines genuinely support more. If a question wants an exact answer, it will give you the rate and expect calculation, not a graph.

The gradient is the conversion rate

For a graph through the origin the equation is y = mx, and m — the gradient — is exactly the number of y-units in one x-unit.

gradient = riserun = 80 − 050 − 0 = 1.6 1.6 kilometres in every mile
Note The gradient of the graph the other way round (km across, miles up) is 11.6 = 0.625. The two rates are reciprocals — multiplying by one undoes multiplying by the other.

Reading beyond the end of the graph

A graph drawn to 50 miles cannot show 250 miles. You do not redraw it — you use proportion. Read a value that is on the graph, then scale the answer by the same factor.

Example 3The conversion graph only goes up to 50 miles. Use it to convert 250 miles into kilometres.
1
Divide the distance by a convenient factor so it fits on the graph. Here 250 ÷ 10 = 25. 250 miles = 10 × 25 miles
2
Read 25 miles off the graph in the usual way. 25 miles → 40 km
3
Because the quantities are in direct proportion, multiply the reading by the same factor of 10. 250 miles = 10 × 40 = 400 km
4
Check with the rate. 250 × 1.6 = 400  ✓
250 miles ≈ 400 km
Watch out This scaling trick works only when the line passes through the origin. On a graph with a fixed charge, doubling the distance does not double the cost.

When the line misses the origin

Plenty of real conversion graphs are straight lines that do not go through the origin: a taxi fare with a fixed charge before you travel a single metre, or the Celsius-to-Fahrenheit scale, where 0°C is already 32°F. The line is still straight, so reading it works exactly the same way — but the two quantities are no longer in direct proportion.

Two straight lines, only one of them proportional

Taxi fare

A fixed charge of $2.50 plus $1.20 per kilometre.

F = 1.20d + 2.50

The intercept is the fare before you move; the gradient is the cost of each extra kilometre.

Celsius to Fahrenheit

0°C = 32°F and 100°C = 212°F.

F = 1.8C + 32

Gradient 212 − 32100 − 0 = 1.8, intercept 32.

Watch out Temperature is a favourite exam trap. 20°C is not twice as warm as 10°C on the Fahrenheit scale: 10°C = 50°F but 20°C = 68°F, not 100°F. Whenever the line misses the origin, you cannot scale readings up and down.
Example 4A taxi charges a fixed $2.50 plus $1.20 for each kilometre. Draw a conversion graph for 0 to 20 km and use it to find (a) the fare for 14 km, (b) the distance covered for a fare of $17.50.
1
Work out two points. At d = 0 the fare is just the fixed charge; at d = 20 add twenty lots of $1.20. (0, 2.50)
20 × 1.20 = 24.00, so (20, 26.50)
2
Plot the two points and rule the line. It cuts the vertical axis at $2.50, not at the origin.
3
(a) Go up from 14 on the distance axis to the line, then across to the fare axis. 2.50 + 14 × 1.20 = 2.50 + 16.80 = 19.30
4
(b) Go across from 17.50 on the fare axis to the line, then down. To confirm the reading, subtract the fixed charge first, then divide by the rate. 17.50 − 2.50 = 15.00
15.00 ÷ 1.20 = 12.5 km
5
Check by converting back. 2.50 + 12.5 × 1.20 = 2.50 + 15.00 = 17.50  ✓
(a) $19.30     (b) 12.5 km
Exam tip Money answers are written to 2 decimal places — write $19.30, never $19.3.

More worked examples

Example 5A conversion graph is drawn using 1 gallon = 4.5 litres, for 0 to 10 gallons. Use it to convert (a) 6.5 gallons into litres, (b) 27 litres into gallons.
1
The two points needed for the line are (0, 0) and, at the far end, 10 gallons. 10 × 4.5 = 45, so (10, 45)
2
(a) Up from 6.5 gallons, across to the litre axis. The gradient is 4.5 litres per gallon. 6.5 × 4.5 = 29.25 litres
3
A graph reading here would give about 29 litres, which is inside the usual half-square tolerance. Quote 29.25 only if you calculated it.
4
(b) Across from 27 litres, down to the gallon axis. Going this way you divide by the rate. 27 ÷ 4.5 = 6 gallons
5
Check by converting back. 6 × 4.5 = 27  ✓
(a) 29.25 litres     (b) 6 gallons
Example 6A conversion graph for temperature passes through (0, 32) and (100, 212), with °C across and °F up. (a) Find its equation. (b) Convert 25°C to °F. (c) Convert 5°F to °C.
1
(a) Gradient first, using the two given points. m = 212 − 32100 − 0 = 180100 = 1.8
2
The graph crosses the vertical axis at 32, so the intercept is 32. F = 1.8C + 32
3
(b) Substitute C = 25. F = 1.8 × 25 + 32 = 45 + 32 = 77
4
(c) Going the other way, undo the operations in reverse order: subtract 32 first, then divide by 1.8. C = 5 − 321.8 = −271.8 = −15
5
Check by converting back. 1.8 × (−15) + 32 = −27 + 32 = 5  ✓
(a) F = 1.8C + 32    (b) 77°F    (c) −15°C
Watch out Dividing by 1.8 before subtracting 32 is the most common error in this topic. The intercept must come off first.
Example 7An electricity bill is a straight-line graph of cost against units used. It passes through (100, 32) and (500, 96), where the cost is in dollars. (a) Find the standing charge and the cost per unit. (b) Find the bill for 350 units. (c) A customer is charged $60. How many units did they use?
1
(a) The cost per unit is the gradient. m = 96 − 32500 − 100 = 64400 = 0.16
2
The standing charge is the intercept — the cost of zero units. Substitute one known point into C = 0.16u + c. 32 = 0.16 × 100 + c
32 = 16 + c
c = 16
3
Check with the other point. 0.16 × 500 + 16 = 80 + 16 = 96  ✓
4
(b) Substitute u = 350. C = 0.16 × 350 + 16 = 56 + 16 = 72
5
(c) Take the standing charge off first, then divide by the cost per unit. 60 − 16 = 44
44 ÷ 0.16 = 275 units
6
Check by converting back. 0.16 × 275 + 16 = 44 + 16 = 60  ✓
(a) standing charge $16.00, cost per unit $0.16    (b) $72.00    (c) 275 units
Exam tip When a question gives you two exact points, calculate from the equation rather than reading off the graph. You get an exact answer, and the working earns method marks even if the arithmetic slips.

Your turn

Practice set

  1. A conversion graph for kilograms to pounds is to be drawn for 0 to 20 kg, using 1 kg = 2.2 lb. (a) Write down the coordinates of two points you would plot. (b) Use the graph to convert 15 kg into pounds.
  2. Use the same graph to convert 27.5 lb into kilograms.
  3. The graph in question 1 only reaches 20 kg. Explain how you would use it to convert 170 kg into pounds, and give the answer.
  4. A conversion graph with kilometres across and miles up passes through the origin and (40, 25). Find its gradient and say what the gradient means.
  5. A taxi fare graph is a straight line through (0, 3) and (12, 21), where the fare is in dollars and the distance in kilometres. Find (a) the fixed charge, (b) the cost per kilometre, (c) the fare for 8 km, (d) the distance travelled for a fare of $16.50.
  6. The temperature conversion line passes through (0, 32) and (100, 212). (a) Write its equation. (b) Convert 35°C to °F. (c) Find the temperature at which both scales show the same number.

  1. (a) (0, 0) and 20 × 2.2 = 44, so (0, 0) and (20, 44). (b) 15 × 2.2 = 33 lb (check: 33 ÷ 2.2 = 15).
  2. Across from 27.5 lb, then down: 27.5 ÷ 2.2 = 12.5 kg (check: 12.5 × 2.2 = 27.5).
  3. The line goes through the origin, so scale. Read 170 ÷ 10 = 17 kg, which gives 17 × 2.2 = 37.4 lb, then multiply by 10 → 374 lb.
  4. 25 ÷ 40 = 0.625; there are 0.625 miles in one kilometre (equivalently 1 mile = 1.6 km).
  5. (a) intercept $3.00; (b) 21 − 312 = $1.50 per km; (c) 3 + 8 × 1.5 = $15.00; (d) (16.50 − 3) ÷ 1.5 = 13.5 ÷ 1.5 = 9 km (check: 3 + 9 × 1.5 = 16.50).
  6. (a) gradient 180 ÷ 100 = 1.8, intercept 32 → F = 1.8C + 32; (b) 1.8 × 35 + 32 = 63 + 32 = 95°F; (c) set x = 1.8x + 32 → −0.8x = 32 → −40° (check: 1.8 × (−40) + 32 = −40).

Key points to remember

  • Two quantities in direct proportion give a straight line through the origin; one point is therefore free and you only need to calculate one more.
  • Choose your second point at the far end of the axis — a long ruled line is far more accurate than a short one.
  • Read values by drawing dotted lines: up then across, or across then down. Leave them on the paper as evidence of method.
  • The gradient is the conversion rate. Multiply by it going one way, divide by it going the other; the two rates are reciprocals.
  • To convert a value beyond the graph, divide by 10 (or 100), read off, then multiply the answer back — but only if the line passes through the origin.
  • If the line has an intercept, the rule is y = mx + c: going backwards you subtract c first, then divide by m.
  • Graph readings are accurate to about half a small square — do not quote them to several decimal places, and calculate instead whenever the exact rate is given.
  • Money answers are always given to 2 decimal places.
