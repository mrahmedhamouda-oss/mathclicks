15.9  Trigonometry in Three Dimensions

Finding a right-angled triangle inside a solid, redrawing it flat, and using Pythagoras or trigonometry on cuboids, pyramids and cones.

The method Cuboids Line and plane Two planes Examples Your turn Key points

The method: hunt the triangle, then draw it flat

There is no new mathematics in this topic. Every 3D question reduces to a right-angled triangle that you already know how to solve — the only difficulty is seeing it inside the solid and getting its side lengths right.

The routine
  1. Find a right-angled triangle inside the solid that contains what you know and what you want.
  2. Redraw it flat, on its own, as a plain 2D triangle. Mark the right angle.
  3. Fill in every length you can. You will often need a first triangle just to supply a length for the second.
  4. Solve it with Pythagoras (no angles) or SOHCAHTOA (an angle involved).

The same triangle, seen twice

Watch out Do not try to do the trigonometry on the 3D picture. Lengths in a 3D sketch are distorted, and angles that look like right angles often are not. Once the triangle is on its own, the picture is honest again.
Exam tip In Paper 4 the flat sketch earns you method marks even if the arithmetic slips. Draw it, label all three sides, and mark the right angle with a small square.

The space diagonal of a cuboid

Label a cuboid ABCDEFGH with ABCD the base and E, F, G, H directly above A, B, C, D. The longest straight line inside it runs from one corner to the opposite corner — here AG. Finding it takes two applications of Pythagoras.

Stage 1 then stage 2

Stage 1 — the base triangle ABC, right angled at B, gives the base diagonal:

AC² = AB² + BC² = l² + w²

Stage 2 — triangle ACG, right angled at C because the vertical edge CG meets the base at right angles, gives the space diagonal:

AG² = AC² + CG² = l² + w² + h² so AG = √(l² + w² + h²)

You may quote the one-line formula, but write the two stages out if the question says show that, and always keep AC in squared form (AC² = 100) so you never round it.

Example 1A cuboid measures 8 cm by 6 cm by 5 cm. Find the length of the space diagonal AG.
1
Draw the base triangle ABC flat. It is right angled at B because the base is a rectangle. AC² = 8² + 6² = 64 + 36 = 100
2
Here AC = 10 exactly, but leave it as AC² = 100 to carry forward.
3
Draw triangle ACG flat. The vertical edge CG is perpendicular to the base, so the right angle is at C. AG² = AC² + CG² = 100 + 5² = 125
4
Square root. AG = √125 = 11.1803…
AG = 11.2 cm (3 s.f.)
One-line check √(8² + 6² + 5²) = √125 = 11.18 ✓. The space diagonal must be longer than every edge and longer than every face diagonal.
Example 2For the same 8 cm by 6 cm by 5 cm cuboid, find the angle that AG makes with the base ABCD.
1
The angle between AG and the base is the angle inside triangle ACG at A, because AC lies flat in the base and is the shadow of AG. required angle = angle GAC
2
Label relative to θ: CG = 5 is O, AC = 10 is A. O and A → tangent. tan θ = 5 / 10 = 0.5
3
Inverse tangent. θ = tan−1(0.5) = 26.5650…°
θ = 26.6° (1 d.p.)
Exam tip Use tangent with the base diagonal, not sine with the space diagonal. Both give 26.6° here, but tangent uses two exact numbers, while sine needs the rounded √125.

The angle between a line and a plane

Definition To find the angle between a line and a plane, drop a perpendicular from the far end of the line onto the plane. Join the foot of that perpendicular back to where the line meets the plane: that new line is the projection (the shadow) of the original line. The angle you want is between the line and its projection.

Reading the projection off a cuboid

Because GC is perpendicular to the base, C is the foot of the perpendicular and AC is the projection. So the angle between AG and the base is θ = angle GAC, exactly as in Example 2.

Watch out The angle is never measured to an edge of the solid unless that edge happens to be the projection. For AG and the base, the projection is the base diagonal AC — not the edge AB.
Example 3In the same cuboid (AB = 8 cm, BC = 6 cm, height 5 cm), find the angle between the face diagonal AF and the base ABCD.
1
F is directly above B, so the perpendicular from F meets the base at B. The projection of AF is therefore the edge AB, not a diagonal. required angle = angle FAB
2
Draw triangle ABF flat, right angled at B. AB = 8, BF = 5
3
Relative to θ: BF is O, AB is A → tangent. tan θ = 5 / 8 = 0.625
θ = tan−1(0.625) = 32.0053…°
θ = 32.0° (1 d.p.)
Compare AG made 26.6° with the base and AF makes 32.0°. AF is steeper because its shadow (8 cm) is shorter than AG's shadow (10 cm), while both rise the same 5 cm.

The angle between two planes

Definition Two planes meet in a straight line. Pick a point on that line and draw, from that same point, a line in each plane that is perpendicular to the line of intersection. The angle between those two lines is the angle between the planes.

For a square-based pyramid VABCD with apex V vertically above the centre M of the base, the angle between a sloping face and the base is found at the midpoint of a base edge.

Slant edge

VA, from the apex to a corner. Its flat triangle uses half the base diagonal, AM.

Slant height

VN, from the apex to the midpoint of an edge. Its flat triangle uses half the base side, MN.

Where the two triangles live

Example 4A pyramid has a square base of side 10 cm and a vertical height of 12 cm. Find the angle between a sloping face and the base.
1
The face VBC meets the base along BC. Take N, the midpoint of BC. Then NV lies in the face and NM lies in the base, and both are perpendicular to BC. required angle = angle VNM
2
M is the centre of the square, so MN is half the side. MN = 10 ÷ 2 = 5 cm, VM = 12 cm
3
Draw triangle VMN flat, right angled at M. Relative to θ: VM = 12 is O, MN = 5 is A → tangent. tan θ = 12 / 5 = 2.4
θ = tan−1(2.4) = 67.3801…°
θ = 67.4° (1 d.p.)
Exam tip The slant height comes free from the same triangle: VN = √(12² + 5²) = √169 = 13 cm — the number you need for the surface area of the pyramid.

More worked examples

Example 5The same pyramid has a square base of side 10 cm and height 12 cm. Find (a) the length of a slant edge VA, (b) the angle VA makes with the base.
1
The projection of VA on the base is AM, half the base diagonal. Find the diagonal first. AC² = 10² + 10² = 200
AC = √200 = 14.1421…
2
Halve it. AM = √200 ÷ 2 = 7.07106…  (AM² = 50)
3
(a) Triangle AMV is right angled at M. Two sides known, angle not needed → Pythagoras. VA² = AM² + VM² = 50 + 144 = 194
VA = √194 = 13.9283…
4
(b) For the angle, use the two exact sides: VM = 12 is O, AM is A → tangent. tan θ = 12 ÷ 7.07106… = 1.69705…
θ = tan−1(1.69705…) = 59.4910…°
(a) VA = 13.9 cm    (b) θ = 59.5°
Watch out The slant edge (13.9 cm) and the slant height (13 cm) are different lengths, and the angles they make with the base (59.5° and 67.4°) are different too. Read the question carefully: edge means corner, face means edge midpoint.
Example 6A cone has base radius 6 cm and slant height 14 cm. Find (a) its vertical height, (b) the angle the slant height makes with the base.
1
Cut the cone vertically through the apex. The half cross-section is a right-angled triangle: radius along the base, height up the axis, slant height as the hypotenuse. r = 6, l = 14, right angle at the centre
2
(a) Two sides known, no angle needed → Pythagoras. The slant height is the longest side, so subtract. h² = 14² − 6² = 196 − 36 = 160
h = √160 = 12.6491…
3
(b) For the angle at the base rim, the 6 cm radius is A and the 14 cm slant is H → cosine. cos θ = 6 / 14 = 0.428571…
θ = cos−1(0.428571…) = 64.6230…°
(a) h = 12.6 cm    (b) θ = 64.6°
Bonus The angle at the apex between the axis and the slant is 90 − 64.6 = 25.4°, so the full angle at the point of the cone is 50.8°.
Example 7A closed box is a cuboid measuring 24 cm by 10 cm by h cm. The longest straight rod that fits inside is exactly 30 cm. Find h, and the angle the rod makes with the base.
1
The longest rod lies along the space diagonal AG, so use the 3D formula and work backwards. AG² = 24² + 10² + h²
30² = 576 + 100 + h²
2
Rearrange for h². 900 − 676 = h²
h² = 224
h = √224 = 14.9666…
3
For the angle, first get the base diagonal — the projection of the rod. AC² = 24² + 10² = 676
AC = 26 cm exactly
4
In triangle ACG the height is O and AC is A → tangent. Use the unrounded height. tan θ = 14.9666… ÷ 26 = 0.575641…
θ = tan−1(0.575641…) = 29.9264…°
h = 15.0 cm (3 s.f.)    θ = 29.9°
Exam tip Since AG = 30 and AC = 26 are both exact, you could instead use cos θ = 26/30, giving 29.9° as well — a quick way to check the answer without reusing your value of h.

Your turn

Practice set

  1. A cuboid measures 9 cm by 12 cm by 8 cm. Find the length of its space diagonal.
  2. For that cuboid, find the angle the space diagonal makes with the base.
  3. A pyramid has a square base of side 8 cm and a vertical height of 15 cm. Find the slant height (apex to the midpoint of a base edge) and the angle between a sloping face and the base.
  4. A cone has base radius 9 cm and vertical height 12 cm. Find the angle between a slant height and the base.
  5. For the pyramid in question 3, find the length of a slant edge and the angle it makes with the base.
  6. A room is a cuboid 6 m long, 4 m wide and 3 m high. A wire runs from a bottom corner to the opposite top corner. Find (a) its length, (b) the angle it makes with the floor.

  1. d² = 9² + 12² + 8² = 81 + 144 + 64 = 289 → 17 cm exactly.
  2. Base diagonal √(81 + 144) = 15; tan θ = 8/15 → 28.1°
  3. Half the base side is 4. VN = √(15² + 4²) = √241 = 15.5 cm; tan θ = 15/4 → 15.5 cm and 75.1°
  4. Radius is A, height is O → tan θ = 12/9 → 53.1°
  5. Half the base diagonal = √(8² + 8²) ÷ 2 = 5.65685… (square 32). Slant edge = √(225 + 32) = √257; tan θ = 15 ÷ 5.65685… → 16.0 cm and 69.3°
  6. (a) √(36 + 16 + 9) = √61 → 7.81 m. (b) Floor diagonal √52 = 7.2111…; tan θ = 3 ÷ 7.2111… → 22.6°

Key points to remember

  • Every 3D question becomes a 2D one: find the right-angled triangle, redraw it flat, label all three sides, then use Pythagoras or SOHCAHTOA.
  • Space diagonal of a cuboid: AG = √(l² + w² + h²), or two stages — base diagonal first, then upright.
  • Carry lengths forward in squared form (AC² = 100) so nothing is rounded part way through.
  • The angle between a line and a plane is the angle between the line and its projection — drop a perpendicular to the plane and join the foot back to the start.
  • The angle between two planes is measured at their line of intersection, using a line in each plane that is perpendicular to that intersection.
  • In a square-based pyramid the slant edge uses half the base diagonal; the slant height uses half the base side. They are different, and so are their angles.
  • A cone splits into a right-angled triangle with sides r, h and slant height l, where l² = r² + h².
  • Lengths to 3 significant figures, angles to 1 decimal place, and never do trigonometry on the distorted 3D sketch.
