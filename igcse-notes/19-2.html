19.2  Symmetry in Three Dimensions

Planes of symmetry in solids and axes of rotational symmetry — how to count them systematically instead of guessing, for every solid on the syllabus.

Planes of symmetry

In two dimensions the mirror is a line. In three dimensions the mirror is a flat surface: a plane of symmetry. Everything you learned about line symmetry carries over — the test is still “does one half reflect onto the other?”

Definition A plane of symmetry is a flat surface that cuts a solid into two pieces such that each piece is the mirror image of the other in that plane. Every point of the solid has a matching point the same perpendicular distance on the other side of the plane.

The three planes of symmetry of a cuboid

Exam tip A cuboid whose three edge lengths are all different has exactly 3 planes of symmetry: one parallel to each pair of opposite faces, each one halfway between them. Say it as “one for each pair of faces” and you will never miscount.

Why the diagonal cut fails

Watch out Cutting a cuboid diagonally does produce two congruent pieces — but one is a rotation of the other, not a reflection, so it is not a plane of symmetry. The diagonal cut only works when the two faces it meets are equal squares — which is why a cube has extra planes and an ordinary cuboid does not.
Example 1A cuboid measures 8 cm by 5 cm by 3 cm. Write down the number of planes of symmetry and describe each one.
1
The three edge lengths are all different, so no diagonal cut can work. Take the pairs of opposite faces one at a time.
2
Parallel to the two 5 × 3 faces, halfway along the 8 cm edges. a 5 cm × 3 cm rectangular cut, 4 cm from each end
3
Parallel to the two 8 × 3 faces, halfway along the 5 cm edges. an 8 cm × 3 cm cut, 2.5 cm from each side
4
Parallel to the two 8 × 5 faces, halfway along the 3 cm edges. an 8 cm × 5 cm cut, 1.5 cm from each face
3 planes of symmetry

The cube — nine planes

A cube is a cuboid whose edges are all equal, so it keeps the three “face” planes and gains six more. Count them in two groups and the answer is never in doubt.

Group 1 — parallel to the faces

One plane halfway between each pair of opposite faces. A cube has 3 pairs of opposite faces.

3 planes

Group 2 — the diagonal planes

Each one contains a pair of opposite edges. The 12 edges pair up into 6 such pairs, and every one of these cuts works because the faces of a cube are squares.

6 planes

3 + 6 = 9 planes of symmetry A cube has 9; a cuboid with different edges has only 3

Two of the six diagonal planes

In between the cube and the cuboid A square prism — a cuboid with a square cross-section, such as 4 × 4 × 10 — has 5 planes: the one cutting the length in half, plus the four planes containing the long axis that match the four lines of symmetry of the square cross-section (two through the faces, two through the diagonals).
Example 2A solid cuboid measures 4 cm by 4 cm by 10 cm. Find the number of planes of symmetry, and explain why the answer is not 3.
1
The cross-section is a square 4 × 4. Any plane that contains the long axis and is a line of symmetry of that square is a plane of symmetry of the solid. A square has 4 lines of symmetry. 4 planes containing the 10 cm axis
2
There is also one plane perpendicular to the long axis, halfway along it. 1 more plane, 5 cm from each square face
3
Add them. 4 + 1 = 5
5 planes of symmetry
Exam tip For any prism: number of planes = (lines of symmetry of the cross-section) + 1 for the cut halfway along its length. Test it on a cube: 4 + 1 = 5? No — a cube is a special case where the “length” equals the side, so all three directions act as the cross-section, giving 9.

Pyramids, prisms and curved solids

Square-based pyramid

2 planes through the midpoints of opposite base edges, and 2 through the base diagonals. All four contain the apex.

Triangular prism

Cross-section equilateral: 3 + 1 = 4. Cross-section isosceles only: 1 + 1 = 2. Cross-section scalene: 1.

Cylinder

Every vertical plane through the axis is a plane of symmetry — there are infinitely many — and so is the one horizontal plane halfway up.

Cone

Every plane containing the axis works. A horizontal cut does not: the top piece is a small cone and the bottom piece is a frustum.

Sphere

A sphere is the most symmetrical solid of all: every plane through the centre is a plane of symmetry, and the centre is its centre of symmetry.

The rule for prisms

For a prism whose cross-section is not a square or a circle:

planes = (lines of symmetry of the cross-section) + 1

Regular hexagonal prism: 6 + 1 = 7. Regular pentagonal prism: 5 + 1 = 6.

Example 3A prism is 12 cm long. Its cross-section is a triangle with sides 5 cm, 5 cm and 8 cm. How many planes of symmetry does the prism have?
1
Deal with the cross-section first. A triangle with sides 5, 5, 8 is isosceles, not equilateral, so it has just 1 line of symmetry. 1 plane containing that line and the 12 cm length
2
Then add the cut perpendicular to the length, halfway along. 1 more plane, 6 cm from each end
3
Total. 1 + 1 = 2
2 planes of symmetry
Watch out If the triangle were equilateral the answer would be 3 + 1 = 4, and if it were scalene it would be just 1 (only the halfway cut). Always check the cross-section before you write anything down.

Axes of rotational symmetry

In three dimensions a shape rotates about a line, not a point. That line is an axis of rotational symmetry, and it has an order in exactly the same sense as in two dimensions.

Definition An axis of rotational symmetry is a line about which the solid can be turned so that it occupies the same space as before. The order is the number of positions in one full turn where the solid looks unchanged.

The three axes of a cuboid

How to describe an axis

Say where it goes in, and where it comes out: “the axis through the centres of the two 5 × 3 faces”, or “the vertical axis through the apex and the centre of the base”. A vague answer such as “down the middle” does not score.

Orders you should know

  • Cuboid: 3 axes, each of order 2
  • Square-based pyramid: 1 axis of order 4
  • Equilateral triangular prism: the long axis has order 3
  • Regular hexagonal prism: the long axis has order 6
  • Cylinder and cone: the axis has infinite order — any rotation at all works
Going further A cube has 13 axes altogether: 3 of order 4 through the centres of opposite faces, 4 of order 3 through opposite corners, and 6 of order 2 through the midpoints of opposite edges. You will not be asked to list all 13, but being able to see the order-3 corner-to-corner axis is a genuine test of 3-D thinking.

More worked examples

Example 4Compare a solid cylinder and a solid cone. For each, state the number of planes of symmetry and describe the axis of rotational symmetry.
1
Cylinder. Any plane containing the axis cuts it into two identical half-cylinders that are mirror images, and there are infinitely many such planes. infinitely many vertical planes
2
The cylinder also has one horizontal plane, halfway up, because the top half is a mirror image of the bottom half. still infinitely many in total
3
Cone. Every plane through the apex and the centre of the base works, so again there are infinitely many. But a horizontal cut does not, because the two pieces are different shapes. infinitely many, all containing the axis
4
Both have one axis of rotational symmetry: the line joining the centres of the circular ends (cylinder), or the apex to the centre of the base (cone). Any rotation at all leaves the solid looking the same. order is infinite for both
Both: infinitely many planes; one axis of infinite order
Example 5A cuboid measures 6 cm by 6 cm by 6 cm. State (a) how many planes of symmetry it has, (b) the order of rotational symmetry about an axis through the centres of two opposite faces, (c) the order about a body diagonal.
1
All edges are 6 cm, so this cuboid is a cube. 3 face planes + 6 diagonal planes = 9
2
(b) Look down the axis through two opposite face centres: you see a square, and a square has rotational symmetry of order 4. Turning the cube through 90° about that axis leaves it looking identical. order 4 — it fits every 90°
3
(c) Look down a corner-to-corner body diagonal: the three faces meeting at that corner take each other’s places every 120°. 360° ÷ 120° = 3
(a) 9    (b) order 4    (c) order 3
Example 6The diagram shows a prism whose cross-section is a regular hexagon of side 3 cm; the prism is 20 cm long. Find the number of planes of symmetry, and state the order of rotational symmetry about the long axis.
1
Start with the cross-section. A regular hexagon has 6 lines of symmetry: 3 joining opposite vertices and 3 joining the midpoints of opposite sides. 6 planes, each containing the 20 cm axis
2
Add the plane perpendicular to the axis, halfway along. 6 + 1 = 7 planes
3
Rotational symmetry about the long axis matches the rotational symmetry of the cross-section, which is order 6 for a regular hexagon. order 6 — the prism fits every 60°
7 planes of symmetry; rotational symmetry of order 6 about the long axis
Exam tip Two-dimensional symmetry does the heavy lifting for every prism. Draw the cross-section, count its lines and its order, then add the single halfway plane.

Your turn

Practice set

  1. Write down the number of planes of symmetry of (a) a cuboid measuring 7 × 4 × 2, (b) a cube, (c) a sphere, (d) a square-based pyramid.
  2. A prism 10 cm long has a cross-section that is an isosceles triangle with sides 6 cm, 6 cm and 4 cm. How many planes of symmetry does it have?
  3. A cuboid measures 5 × 5 × 12 centimetres. Explain why it has more than 3 planes of symmetry and state how many it has.
  4. Describe the axis of rotational symmetry of a cone and state its order.
  5. A solid cylinder has radius 4 cm and height 10 cm. Describe all of its planes of symmetry.
  6. A prism 15 cm long has a regular pentagonal cross-section. Find the number of planes of symmetry and the order of rotational symmetry about the long axis. Would your answers change if the pentagon were irregular but had one line of symmetry?

  1. (a) all edges different → 3  (b) 3 face planes + 6 diagonal planes → 9  (c) infinitely many  (d) 2 through opposite edge midpoints + 2 through the diagonals → 4
  2. Isosceles cross-section has 1 line of symmetry → 1 plane along the length, plus the halfway cut → 2 planes
  3. The cross-section is a square, which has 4 lines of symmetry, so 4 planes contain the 12 cm axis; add the plane 6 cm from each square face → 5 planes
  4. The line from the apex to the centre of the circular base; any rotation about it maps the cone onto itself, so the order is infinite
  5. Every plane containing the axis (infinitely many, each a 8 cm × 10 cm rectangle) and the horizontal plane 5 cm up (a circle of radius 4 cm) → infinitely many in total
  6. Regular pentagon: 5 lines, order 5 → 5 + 1 = 6 planes, order 5. With only one line of symmetry the prism would have 1 + 1 = 2 planes and order 1 (no rotational symmetry about the long axis).

Key points to remember

  • A plane of symmetry cuts a solid into two halves that are mirror images — congruent is not enough.
  • Cuboid with all edges different: 3 planes. Cube: 9 (3 face planes + 6 diagonal planes). Square prism: 5.
  • Square-based pyramid: 4 planes, all through the apex.
  • For any prism, planes = lines of symmetry of the cross-section + 1 (the cut halfway along the length).
  • Cylinder, cone and sphere all have infinitely many planes of symmetry; the cone has no horizontal one.
  • An axis of rotational symmetry is a line; its order is how many times the solid fits in one full turn about it.
  • A cuboid has 3 axes each of order 2; a square-based pyramid has one axis of order 4; a cylinder and a cone have one axis of infinite order.
  • Describe an axis by naming both points it passes through — two face centres, or the apex and the centre of the base.
