17.3  Buying and Selling

Profit and loss, percentage profit, discounts, sales tax and unit pricing — the percentage skills every shopkeeper and every examiner uses.

Profit and loss Discounts Sales tax Best value Examples Your turn Key points

Cost price, selling price, profit and loss

Definition The cost price (CP) is what the seller paid. The selling price (SP) is what the customer pays. If SP > CP there is a profit; if SP < CP there is a loss.
profit = SP − CP      loss = CP − SP Always subtract the smaller from the larger, then say which it is

A profit of $48 means very different things on a $60 item and on a $6000 item, so profit is almost always converted into a percentage. In IGCSE this percentage is measured against the cost price unless the question says otherwise.

percentage profit = profitcost price × 100 The cost price is the denominator — it is the 100% the change is measured from
percentage loss = losscost price × 100 Same denominator: the price the seller originally paid
Watch out Dividing by the selling price is the most frequent error in this whole chapter. The only time you divide by something else is when the question explicitly says so, for example “express the profit as a percentage of the selling price”. Underline that word as you read.
Example 1A shop buys a jacket for $240 and sells it for $288. Find the profit and the percentage profit.
1
Identify the two prices. CP = $240.00,   SP = $288.00
2
Subtract to find the profit. 288.00 − 240.00 = $48.00
3
Divide by the cost price and multiply by 100. 48240 × 100 = 0.2 × 100 = 20
Profit = $48.00, percentage profit = 20%
Exam tip Check with a multiplier: a 20% profit means SP = 1.2 × CP = 1.2 × 240 = 288 ✓. One line, and it confirms both answers.
Example 2A trader buys a scooter for $1450 and later sells it for $1218. Find the percentage loss.
1
The selling price is lower, so this is a loss. 1450 − 1218 = $232.00
2
Divide the loss by the cost price. 2321450 = 0.16
3
Multiply by 100 and state that it is a loss. 0.16 × 100 = 16
Percentage loss = 16%
Note Never write a percentage loss as a negative number in this topic. Write the size of the change and label it “loss”.

Discounts and sale prices

A discount is a reduction from the marked (or list) price. There are two ways to find the sale price, and you should be able to use both.

Work out the discount, then take it off.

30% of 85 = 0.30 × 85 = 25.50

85.00 − 25.50 = 59.50

Safer if the question also asks “how much was saved?”.

Subtract the percentage from 100 first, then multiply once.

100% − 30% = 70%

0.70 × 85 = 59.50

One calculator step, so fewer chances to slip — and it is the only sensible method when two reductions follow one another.

Example 3A coat is marked at $85. In a sale it is reduced by 30%. (a) Find the sale price. (b) A second coat has a sale price of $59.50 after the same 30% reduction — find its marked price.
1
(a) A 30% reduction leaves 70%, so the multiplier is 0.7. 0.70 × 85.00 = 59.50
2
(b) Going backwards, divide by the same multiplier. marked price = 59.50 ÷ 0.70 = 85.00
3
Check forwards. 0.70 × 85.00 = 59.50 ✓
(a) $59.50    (b) $85.00
Watch out To reverse a 30% discount you divide by 0.7. Adding 30% back on gives 1.3 × 59.50 = 77.35, which is wrong — the 30% was a percentage of the larger, original price.

Sales tax (VAT)

Definition Sales tax (called VAT in many countries) is a percentage added to the price of goods and services. A price is either excluding tax (pre-tax) or including tax. The tax is always a percentage of the pre-tax price.
price including tax = pre-tax price × (1 + rate100) Adding the tax — multiply going forwards
pre-tax price = price including tax1 + rate/100 Stripping the tax out — divide coming back

Forwards and backwards with 15% tax

Example 4Sales tax is charged at 15%. (a) A table is priced at $240 before tax. Find the price a customer pays. (b) A chair costs $471.50 including tax. Find its pre-tax price and the tax charged.
1
(a) Adding 15% means multiplying by 1.15. 240.00 × 1.15 = 276.00
2
(b) The $471.50 already contains the tax, so it is 115% of the pre-tax price. Divide. 471.50 ÷ 1.15 = 410.00
3
The tax is the difference between the two prices. 471.50 − 410.00 = 61.50
(a) $276.00    (b) pre-tax $410.00, tax $61.50
Watch out Do not take 15% of $471.50. That gives $70.73, and it is wrong, because the tax is 15% of the pre-tax price, not of the total.

Choosing the better value

To compare two packs of different sizes you must compare like with like. Reduce both to the same unit — either the price for one unit, or the amount you get for $1.

Price per unit

price ÷ quantity

The smaller answer is the better value.

Quantity per dollar

quantity ÷ price

The larger answer is the better value.

OfferWorkingPrice per litre
1.5 litres for $2.852.85 ÷ 1.5$1.90
2.5 litres for $4.604.60 ÷ 2.5$1.84

The 2.5 litre bottle is better value, by 6 cents per litre. Notice that the bigger pack is usually better value but not always — you must do the arithmetic, never assume.

Exam tip State your comparison in a full sentence: “the 2.5 litre bottle is better value because it costs $1.84 per litre compared with $1.90 per litre”. A bare answer with no comparison usually loses the final mark.

More worked examples

Example 5A shop sells a bicycle for $448, making a profit of 40%. Find the cost price and the profit.
1
A 40% profit means the selling price is 140% of the cost price. SP = 1.4 × CP
2
Substitute the selling price and divide by the multiplier. 448 = 1.4 × CP
CP = 448 ÷ 1.4 = 320
3
The profit is the difference. 448.00 − 320.00 = 128.00
4
Check the percentage. 128 ÷ 320 × 100 = 40% ✓
Cost price = $320.00, profit = $128.00
Watch out 40% of $448 is $179.20, and $448 − $179.20 = $268.80 — a tempting but wrong answer. The 40% belongs to the cost price, which is the unknown.
Example 6A trader buys 120 shirts for a total of $1800. He sells 90 of them at $22 each and the remaining 30 at a discount of 25% off $22. Find his total profit and his percentage profit.
1
Income from the shirts sold at full price. 90 × 22.00 = $1980.00
2
Find the discounted price using the multiplier 0.75. 0.75 × 22.00 = $16.50
3
Income from the remaining shirts. 30 × 16.50 = $495.00
4
Total income, then subtract the total cost. 1980.00 + 495.00 = $2475.00
2475.00 − 1800.00 = $675.00
5
Percentage profit, using the total cost price. 6751800 × 100 = 37.5
Profit = $675.00, percentage profit = 37.5%
Exam tip With mixed sales, work out the total income and the total cost before you touch percentages. Never average the two percentages.
Example 7A television is advertised at $644, a price that includes sales tax at 15%. The shop bought the television for $420. Find (a) the selling price before tax, (b) the shop’s percentage profit based on that pre-tax selling price.
1
(a) The advertised price is 115% of the pre-tax price, so divide. 644 ÷ 1.15 = 560
2
Check by multiplying forwards. 560.00 × 1.15 = 644.00 ✓
3
(b) Profit = pre-tax selling price − cost price. 560.00 − 420.00 = $140.00
4
Divide by the cost price and multiply by 100. 140420 × 100 = 33.333…
(a) $560.00    (b) 33.3% profit (3 s.f.)
Why pre-tax? The tax is collected for the government, not kept by the shop, so the shop’s profit is measured on the price before tax.

Your turn

Practice set

  1. An item is bought for $60 and sold for $75. Find the profit and the percentage profit.
  2. A van bought for $2400 is sold for $1872. Find the percentage loss.
  3. A coat marked at $180 is reduced by 35% in a sale. Find the sale price.
  4. A restaurant bill is $46 before a 12% service tax is added. Find the total to pay.
  5. Which is better value: 400 g of coffee for $3.60, or 750 g for $6.60? Show your comparison.
  6. A shop buys a laptop for $520 and marks it up by 45%. In a sale the marked price is reduced by 20%, and sales tax of 10% is then added at the till. Find (a) the price the customer pays, (b) the shop’s percentage profit on the pre-tax sale price.

  1. Profit 75 − 60 = 15 → $15.00; 15 ÷ 60 × 100 = 25%
  2. Loss 2400 − 1872 = 528; 528 ÷ 2400 × 100 = 22% loss
  3. 100% − 35% = 65%, so 0.65 × 180 = $117.00
  4. 1.12 × 46 = $51.52
  5. 3.60 ÷ 400 × 100 = $0.90 per 100 g; 6.60 ÷ 750 × 100 = $0.88 per 100 g → the 750 g pack is better value
  6. Marked 1.45 × 520 = 754.00; sale price 0.8 × 754 = 603.20; with tax 1.1 × 603.20 = 663.52. (a) $663.52  (b) profit 603.20 − 520 = 83.20, so 83.20 ÷ 520 × 100 = 16%

Key points to remember

  • Profit = SP − CP and loss = CP − SP. Say which one it is.
  • Percentage profit and percentage loss are calculated as a percentage of the cost price unless the question says otherwise.
  • Use multipliers: 25% off is × 0.75, 45% on is × 1.45. One step, fewer errors.
  • To undo a percentage change, divide by the multiplier. Never add the percentage back on.
  • Sales tax is a percentage of the pre-tax price: multiply by 1 + rate/100 to add it, divide by the same number to strip it out.
  • Successive changes multiply: a 20% reduction then 10% tax is × 0.8 × 1.1 = × 0.88, not a 10% reduction overall.
  • For best value, divide to get the price per unit (smaller wins) or the quantity per dollar (larger wins) — and write a sentence comparing them.
  • Money answers to 2 decimal places; percentages to 3 significant figures unless they come out exactly.
