14.1  Simultaneous Linear Equations

Finding the one pair of values that satisfies two linear equations at once, by elimination, by substitution and from a graph.

What it means Elimination Substitution Graphically Word problems Examples Your turn Key points

What a simultaneous pair means

One linear equation in two unknowns has endlessly many solutions. Look at 2x + y = 11: you can pick almost any x you like and find a y to match. The same is true of 3x − 2y = 6. But there is usually exactly one pair that works for both at the same time, and finding it is what this lesson is about.

x123456
y from 2x + y = 1197531−1
y from 3x − 2y = 6−1.501.534.56

Only x = 4 gives the same y in both rows. The solution of the pair is therefore x = 4, y = 3. Trial and improvement like this is far too slow for an exam, so we use algebra — but the meaning never changes.

Definition Simultaneous equations are two (or more) equations that must be true at the same time. Solving them means finding the value of every unknown that satisfies every equation. Two unknowns need two equations; three unknowns would need three.
Exam tip Always finish by substituting your pair into both original equations. It takes fifteen seconds and it is the only way to be certain. A pair that fits one equation but not the other scores nothing.
x = 4,   y = 3  →  2(4) + 3 = 11 ✓     3(4) − 2(3) = 6 ✓ Check in BOTH equations, every time

Method 1: elimination

The idea is to add or subtract the two equations so that one unknown disappears, leaving a simple linear equation you can solve in one line. This only works when the coefficients of that unknown match in size, so sometimes you must multiply an equation first.

Add or subtract? Look at the signs

Compare the coefficients of the letter you want to remove — once they are the same size:

  • Same signs → Subtract  (+3x and +3x, or −2y and −2y)
  • Different signs → Add  (+4y and −4y)

Some students remember it as SSS — Same Sign, Subtract.

Example 1Solve 3x + 2y = 19 and 3x − y = 4.
1
Label the equations. The coefficients of x are already both +3. (1)  3x + 2y = 19
(2)  3x − y = 4
2
Same signs, so subtract: (1) − (2). Take great care with 2y − (−y) = 3y. (3x − 3x) + (2y − (−y)) = 19 − 4
3y = 15
3
Solve for y. y = 5
4
Substitute y = 5 back into the simpler equation, (2). 3x − 5 = 4
3x = 9  →  x = 3
5
Check in both original equations. (1) 3(3) + 2(5) = 9 + 10 = 19 ✓
(2) 3(3) − 5 = 9 − 5 = 4 ✓
x = 3,   y = 5
Watch out Subtracting a negative term is where most marks are lost. Write the subtraction line out in full — in Paper 4 that line itself can earn a method mark.

When nothing matches: multiply first

If neither pair of coefficients matches, multiply one or both equations by whatever makes them match. You are allowed to multiply every term on both sides of an equation by any number — the equation stays true.

Example 2Solve 3x + 4y = 25 and x − 2y = −5.
1
Nothing matches yet. The y terms are the easier target: 4 is a multiple of 2, so multiply (2) by 2. (1)  3x + 4y = 25
(2)  x − 2y = −5
(2)×2:  2x − 4y = −10
2
Now +4y and −4y: different signs, so add. (3x + 2x) + (4y − 4y) = 25 + (−10)
5x = 15  →  x = 3
3
Substitute into (1). 3(3) + 4y = 25
4y = 16  →  y = 4
4
Check in both. (1) 9 + 16 = 25 ✓
(2) 3 − 8 = −5 ✓
x = 3,   y = 4
Note Multiply every term, including the number on the right-hand side. Forgetting the right-hand side is the second most common error in this topic.

Sometimes neither coefficient divides into the other — for example 3y and 2y. Then multiply both equations, using the lowest common multiple of the two coefficients: multiply (1) by 2 and (2) by 3 so that both become 6y. Example 5 does exactly that.

Elimination stepper

Type the six coefficients of ax + by = c for each equation (whole numbers) and read the elimination laid out line by line. Try making the coefficients match, then clash.

Method 2: substitution

Here you make one letter the subject of one equation, then replace that letter in the other equation. You are left with a single equation in a single unknown.

The four steps

  1. Choose the easiest equation and make x or y the subject. Pick the letter whose coefficient is 1 or −1 if there is one.
  2. Substitute that expression into the other equation — put it in brackets.
  3. Solve the resulting linear equation.
  4. Put that value back into your subject formula to get the second unknown, then check in both.

Substitution is the natural choice when one equation is already written as y = …, which is exactly how straight lines usually arrive. For y = 2x − 1 together with 3x + 2y = 12: 3x + 2(2x − 1) = 12 gives 7x − 2 = 12, so x = 2 and y = 3. Check: 3(2) + 2(3) = 12 ✓.

Example 3Solve 2x + y = 11 and 3x − 2y = 6 — once by elimination and once by substitution.
1
To match the y terms, multiply (1) by 2. (1)  2x + y = 11  →  4x + 2y = 22
(2)  3x − 2y = 6
2
+2y and −2y have different signs, so add. (4x + 3x) + (2y − 2y) = 22 + 6
7x = 28  →  x = 4
3
Substitute into (1). 2(4) + y = 11  →  y = 3
1
Equation (1) has a y with coefficient 1, so make y the subject of it. y = 11 − 2x
2
Substitute into (2), in brackets. 3x − 2(11 − 2x) = 6
3
Expand carefully: −2 × −2x = +4x. 3x − 22 + 4x = 6
7x = 28  →  x = 4
4
Back into the subject formula. y = 11 − 2(4) = 3
✓
Both routes give the same pair. Check in both equations. (1) 2(4) + 3 = 11 ✓
(2) 3(4) − 2(3) = 12 − 6 = 6 ✓
x = 4,   y = 3
Exam tip Unless a question says “use substitution”, either method earns full marks. Elimination is usually faster when every coefficient is a whole number; substitution wins when one equation already gives y in terms of x.
Watch out When you substitute, put the expression in brackets before multiplying. Writing 3x − 2 × 11 − 2x instead of 3x − 2(11 − 2x) changes the sign of the second term and wrecks the answer.

The graphical meaning

Each linear equation draws a straight line. Every point on a line is a pair (x, y) that satisfies its equation, so the pair that satisfies both equations must lie on both lines — it is the point of intersection.

One solution

Different gradients, so the lines cross exactly once. This is the ordinary case.

No solution

Same gradient, different intercepts — the lines are parallel and never meet, for example y = 2x + 1 and y = 2x − 3.

Infinitely many

One equation is a multiple of the other, so the lines are identical and every point on the line is a solution, for example x + y = 5 and 2x + 2y = 10.

Two-line grapher

Enter the coefficients of ax + by = c for both lines. The intersection is marked. Try 1, 1, 5 with 2, 2, 10, then with 2, 2, 6, and see what changes.

Exam tip If a question says “solve graphically” you must draw both lines accurately and read off the intersection — algebra alone will not earn the marks. If it just says “solve”, use algebra: reading a graph to 2 decimal places is impossible.

Forming the equations yourself

Most Paper 4 marks in this topic come from turning sentences into equations. The routine never changes.

From words to a pair of equations

  1. Define your letters in writing, with units: “let p be the cost of one pen in dollars”. Unlabelled letters lose marks.
  2. Each separate fact about the situation gives you one equation. Two unknowns means you must hunt for two facts.
  3. Keep the units consistent — all in dollars or all in cents, never a mixture.
  4. Solve, then answer the actual question in a sentence, with units.
Example 43 pens and 2 notebooks cost $11.60. 5 pens and 4 notebooks cost $20.80. Find the cost of one pen and the cost of one notebook.
1
Define the letters clearly. Let p = cost of one pen ($)
Let n = cost of one notebook ($)
2
Each purchase gives one equation. (1)  3p + 2n = 11.60
(2)  5p + 4n = 20.80
3
Multiply (1) by 2 so both have 4n. (1)×2:  6p + 4n = 23.20
4
Same signs, so subtract (2) from that line. (6p − 5p) + (4n − 4n) = 23.20 − 20.80
p = 2.40
5
Substitute into (1). 3(2.40) + 2n = 11.60
7.20 + 2n = 11.60
2n = 4.40  →  n = 2.20
6
Check in both. (1) 3(2.40) + 2(2.20) = 7.20 + 4.40 = 11.60 ✓
(2) 5(2.40) + 4(2.20) = 12.00 + 8.80 = 20.80 ✓
a pen costs $2.40 and a notebook costs $2.20
Exam tip Money answers must be given to 2 decimal places: write $2.40, not $2.4.

More worked examples

Example 5Solve 2x + 3y = 12 and 5x − 2y = 11.
1
Neither 3 nor 2 divides into the other, so multiply both equations. The lowest common multiple of 3 and 2 is 6, so use (1)×2 and (2)×3. (1)×2:  4x + 6y = 24
(2)×3:  15x − 6y = 33
2
+6y and −6y: different signs, so add. 19x = 57  →  x = 3
3
Substitute into the original (1). 2(3) + 3y = 12
3y = 6  →  y = 2
4
Check in both originals. (1) 6 + 6 = 12 ✓
(2) 15 − 4 = 11 ✓
x = 3,   y = 2
Note Always substitute into an original equation, not a scaled one. If you made a slip while multiplying, the check will then catch it.
Example 6Solve 5x + 3y = 4 and 2x − 5y = 14.
1
Eliminate y: the lowest common multiple of 3 and 5 is 15, so multiply (1) by 5 and (2) by 3. (1)×5:  25x + 15y = 20
(2)×3:  6x − 15y = 42
2
Different signs, so add. 31x = 62  →  x = 2
3
Substitute into (1). Expect a negative — that is fine. 5(2) + 3y = 4
3y = 4 − 10 = −6  →  y = −2
4
Check in both. (1) 10 + 3(−2) = 10 − 6 = 4 ✓
(2) 2(2) − 5(−2) = 4 + 10 = 14 ✓
x = 2,   y = −2
Watch out A negative answer is not a mistake. The trap is the double negative in the check: −5 × −2 = +10.
Example 7The perimeter of a rectangle is 34 cm. Its length is 5 cm more than its width. Find the area of the rectangle.
1
Define the letters. Let l = length (cm), w = width (cm)
2
Perimeter of a rectangle is 2l + 2w. Divide through by 2 straight away to keep the numbers small. 2l + 2w = 34
(1)  l + w = 17
3
“Length is 5 more than width” gives the second equation. Write it in the same layout as the first. l = w + 5
(2)  l − w = 5
4
The w terms have different signs, so add. 2l = 22  →  l = 11
5
Substitute into (1). 11 + w = 17  →  w = 6
6
Check both facts, then answer what was asked — the area. perimeter = 2(11 + 6) = 34 cm ✓
11 − 6 = 5 ✓
area = 11 × 6 = 66 cm²
area = 66 cm²
Exam tip Do not stop at l = 11 and w = 6. Geometry questions almost always want a length, an area or a perimeter at the end, with the correct unit.

Your turn

Practice set

  1. Solve 4x + y = 13 and 2x + y = 7.
  2. Solve 3x + 2y = 13 and x − 2y = −1.
  3. Use substitution to solve y = 3x − 4 and 2x + y = 11.
  4. Solve 3x + 2y = 5 and 2x + 5y = −4.
  5. The lines y = 2x + 1 and 2y = 4x − 6 are drawn on the same grid. Explain why these simultaneous equations have no solution.
  6. 4 coffees and 3 teas cost $16.10. 2 coffees and 5 teas cost $15.40. Find the cost of one coffee and one tea.

  1. Same signs, subtract: 2x = 6 → x = 3, then 2(3) + y = 7 → x = 3, y = 1 (check: 12 + 1 = 13 ✓)
  2. +2y and −2y, so add: 4x = 12 → x = 3, then 9 + 2y = 13 → x = 3, y = 2 (check: 3 − 4 = −1 ✓)
  3. 2x + (3x − 4) = 11 → 5x = 15 → x = 3, so y = 9 − 4 → x = 3, y = 5
  4. (1)×5 and (2)×2: 15x + 10y = 25, 4x + 10y = −8; same signs, subtract → 11x = 33, x = 3, then 9 + 2y = 5 → x = 3, y = −2
  5. The second equation divides by 2 to give y = 2x − 3. Both lines have gradient 2 but different intercepts, so they are parallel and never intersect — there is no pair satisfying both.
  6. 4c + 3t = 16.10, 2c + 5t = 15.40. (2)×2 gives 4c + 10t = 30.80; subtract → 7t = 14.70, t = 2.10, then 4c = 16.10 − 6.30 = 9.80 → coffee $2.45, tea $2.10

Key points to remember

  • Simultaneous equations must both be true at once; two unknowns need two equations.
  • Elimination: make the coefficients of one letter match in size, then same signs subtract, different signs add.
  • When nothing matches, multiply one or both equations by the numbers that make the coefficients equal to their lowest common multiple — and multiply every term, including the right-hand side.
  • Substitution: make one letter the subject, then replace it in the other equation, always using brackets.
  • Substitute your first value back into an original equation to find the second unknown.
  • Check the pair in both original equations before you move on — it is the only reliable check.
  • Graphically the solution is the point of intersection: parallel lines give no solution, identical lines give infinitely many.
  • In word problems, define each letter with its unit, get one equation per fact, and finish by answering the question that was actually asked.
