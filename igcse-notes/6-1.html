6.1  Solving Equations

Using the balance method to solve linear equations with brackets, fractions and the unknown on both sides, and forming your own equations from words and diagrams.

Balance Both sides Fractions Forming Examples Your turn Key points

The balance principle

An equation is a statement that two expressions have the same value. Think of the equals sign as the pivot of a set of scales: as long as you do exactly the same thing to both sides, the scales stay balanced and the equation stays true.

Definition To solve an equation is to find the value (or values) of the unknown letter that make the two sides equal. That value is called the solution or root of the equation.

Keeping the scales level

To get the letter on its own you undo the operations that have been done to it, using the inverse operation, and you undo them in reverse order.

Operation on the unknownInverse you apply to both sides
add 7subtract 7
subtract 7add 7
multiply by 4divide by 4
divide by 4multiply by 4
Example 1Solve 5x − 7 = 23.
1
The x has been multiplied by 5, then 7 has been subtracted. Undo in reverse order, so deal with the −7 first: add 7 to both sides. 5x − 7 + 7 = 23 + 7
5x = 30
2
Now undo the multiplication: divide both sides by 5. 5x ÷ 5 = 30 ÷ 5
x = 6
3
Check by substituting back into the original equation. 5 × 6 − 7 = 30 − 7 = 23  ✓
x = 6
Exam tip Write one line per step, with the equals signs lined up underneath each other. Method marks in Paper 2 and Paper 4 are given for the intermediate lines — a bare answer with no working scores nothing if it is wrong.

Balance-method stepper

Brackets, and the unknown on both sides

Two extra jobs turn up before the balancing can start.

Brackets

Expand first. Multiply every term inside the bracket by the number outside, then collect like terms. Take great care with a negative multiplier: −3(x − 4) = −3x + 12.

Unknown on both sides

Collect the letters on one side and the numbers on the other. It is usually tidiest to move the smaller x term, so that the coefficient you are left with is positive.

Example 2Solve 3(2x − 5) = 4(x + 1).
1
Expand both brackets. 6x − 15 = 4x + 4
2
The smaller x term is 4x, so subtract 4x from both sides. 2x − 15 = 4
3
Add 15 to both sides. 2x = 19
4
Divide by 2. A fraction or decimal answer is perfectly acceptable — do not assume solutions must be whole numbers. x = 192 = 9.5
5
Check. LHS = 3(19 − 5) = 3 × 14 = 42
RHS = 4(10.5) = 42  ✓
x = 9.5
Note When the x term is negative, move that one. In 7 − 2x = 5x − 14, adding 2x to both sides gives 7 = 7x − 14, then 21 = 7x, so x = 3. Check: 7 − 6 = 1 and 15 − 14 = 1 ✓. It does not matter that the letter ended up on the right — 3 = x and x = 3 say the same thing.
Watch out The commonest error in this topic is a sign slip when a term crosses the equals sign. A term that is being subtracted on one side becomes added on the other: 7 − 2x = 5x − 14 → 7 + 14 = 5x + 2x. If you always write the operation you are doing at the side of the line, you will not lose track.

Equations containing fractions

Fractions make an equation look worse than it is. Clear them in one move: multiply every term on both sides by the LCM of the denominators. Each denominator then cancels and you are left with an ordinary linear equation.

x3 + x4 = 7   →   12 × x3 + 12 × x4 = 12 × 7   →   4x + 3x = 84 LCM of 3 and 4 is 12 — multiply every term, including the one that has no fraction
Example 3Solve x + 33 + x − 24 = 4.
1
The denominators are 3 and 4, so the LCM is 12. Multiply every term by 12 — and put brackets round any numerator with more than one term. 12 × (x + 3)3 + 12 × (x − 2)4 = 12 × 4
2
Cancel: 12 ÷ 3 = 4 and 12 ÷ 4 = 3. 4(x + 3) + 3(x − 2) = 48
3
Expand and collect like terms. 4x + 12 + 3x − 6 = 48
7x + 6 = 48
4
Subtract 6, then divide by 7. 7x = 42
x = 6
5
Check. 93 + 44 = 3 + 1 = 4  ✓
x = 6
Watch out Those brackets in step 1 are not optional. Writing 4x + 3 + 3x − 2 = 48 loses two marks and gives the wrong answer.

When the unknown is in the denominator

If x sits underneath, multiply both sides by that denominator to lift it up. When each side is a single fraction you may go straight to cross-multiplication, which is the same move done twice:

pq = rs   ⇔   ps = qr Valid provided q ≠ 0 and s ≠ 0
Note Always check that your solution does not make a denominator zero. If solving 6x − 2 = 3 gave x = 2, that value would have to be rejected, because dividing by zero is undefined.

Forming an equation

Many marks in Paper 4 come from questions that give you a situation rather than an equation. The routine is always the same.

1. Name the unknown

Let x be the quantity you are asked for — and write down what it stands for, including units.

2. Write each fact in algebra

Translate every sentence or label into an expression in x.

3. Find the equals

Something in the question must be equal to something else: a total, a perimeter, an angle sum, two equal sides.

4. Solve, then answer

Solve the equation, then go back and answer the actual question — often it wants a length or an angle, not x itself.

Example 4The three angles of a triangle are (2x + 10)°, (3x − 25)° and (x + 15)°. Find the size of the largest angle.
1
The fact that links them: angles in a triangle add up to 180°. (2x + 10) + (3x − 25) + (x + 15) = 180
2
Collect like terms. Here the numbers happen to cancel: 10 − 25 + 15 = 0. 6x = 180
3
Divide by 6. x = 30
4
Now answer the question — substitute back to get the three angles. 2(30) + 10 = 70°
3(30) − 25 = 65°
30 + 15 = 45°
5
Check. 70 + 65 + 45 = 180  ✓
largest angle = 70°
Exam tip The check is free marks-insurance: three angles that do not total 180° tell you instantly that something has gone wrong.

More worked examples

Example 5Solve 52x − 3 = 2x + 1.
1
One fraction equals one fraction, so cross-multiply. 5(x + 1) = 2(2x − 3)
2
Expand both sides. 5x + 5 = 4x − 6
3
Subtract 4x from both sides, then subtract 5. x + 5 = −6
x = −11
4
Check — and confirm no denominator is zero. 2(−11) − 3 = −25, so LHS = 5−25 = −0.2
−11 + 1 = −10, so RHS = 2−10 = −0.2  ✓
x = −11
Example 6Solve 2x + 13 − x − 42 = 2.
1
LCM of 3 and 2 is 6. Multiply every term by 6, keeping the numerators bracketed. 2(2x + 1) − 3(x − 4) = 12
2
Expand. The −3 multiplies both terms in the second bracket, so −3 × −4 = +12. 4x + 2 − 3x + 12 = 12
3
Collect like terms and subtract 14. x + 14 = 12
x = −2
4
Check. −4 + 13 − −2 − 42 = −1 − (−3) = 2  ✓
x = −2
Watch out A minus sign in front of a fraction becomes a minus sign in front of the whole bracket. Forgetting this turns −3x + 12 into −3x − 12 and the answer is lost.
Example 7A rectangle has length (2x + 3) cm and width (x − 1) cm. Its perimeter is 40 cm. Find the area of the rectangle.
1
Perimeter of a rectangle is 2(length + width). 2[(2x + 3) + (x − 1)] = 40
2
Simplify inside the bracket first. 2(3x + 2) = 40
3
Divide both sides by 2 (cleaner than expanding), then solve. 3x + 2 = 20
3x = 18
x = 6
4
The question asks for the area, so find the two sides first. length = 2(6) + 3 = 15 cm
width = 6 − 1 = 5 cm
5
Check the perimeter, then multiply. 2(15 + 5) = 40  ✓
area = 15 × 5 = 75
area = 75 cm²
Exam tip Include the units in your final answer line. Area is in cm²; a missing or wrong unit can cost the accuracy mark.

Your turn

Practice set

  1. Solve 4x + 9 = 33.
  2. Solve 3 − 4x = 19.
  3. Solve 5(x − 2) = 3x + 4.
  4. Solve x + 52 + x3 = 9.
  5. Solve 6x − 2 = 4x + 1.
  6. Three angles on a straight line are (x + 20)°, (2x)° and (3x − 20)°. Find the largest of the three angles.

  1. 4x = 24 → x = 6 (check: 24 + 9 = 33)
  2. −4x = 16 → x = −4 (check: 3 − 4(−4) = 3 + 16 = 19)
  3. 5x − 10 = 3x + 4 → 2x = 14 → x = 7 (check: 5(5) = 25 = 21 + 4)
  4. × 6: 3(x + 5) + 2x = 54 → 5x + 15 = 54 → 5x = 39 → x = 7.8 (check: 12.8 ÷ 2 + 7.8 ÷ 3 = 6.4 + 2.6 = 9)
  5. Cross-multiply: 6(x + 1) = 4(x − 2) → 6x + 6 = 4x − 8 → 2x = −14 → x = −7 (both sides equal −23)
  6. Angles on a line sum to 180°: 6x = 180 → x = 30, giving 50°, 60°, 70° → largest is 70°

Key points to remember

  • Whatever you do to one side of an equation, do to the other side too — that is the whole method.
  • Undo operations using inverses in reverse order: deal with adding and subtracting before multiplying and dividing.
  • Expand brackets first, then collect the letters on one side and the numbers on the other. Move the smaller x term so the coefficient stays positive.
  • Clear fractions by multiplying every term by the LCM of the denominators, and bracket any numerator with more than one term.
  • If the unknown is in a denominator, multiply it up (or cross-multiply), then reject any solution that would make a denominator zero.
  • Solutions are allowed to be negative, fractional or decimal — do not force a whole number.
  • Always check by substituting your answer into the original equation; it takes ten seconds and catches nearly every sign slip.
  • When forming an equation, define the letter, translate each fact, find what is equal, solve, then answer the question that was actually asked — with units.
