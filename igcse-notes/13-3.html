13.3  Limits of Accuracy

Upper and lower bounds of rounded measurements, and how to combine them correctly in sums, differences, products and quotients.

Bounds Finding bounds Calculations Examples Your turn Key points

Every measurement is rounded

A ruler marked in centimetres cannot tell you a length exactly. If a pencil is “27 cm to the nearest centimetre”, its true length is somewhere in a small interval around 27. The two ends of that interval are the lower bound and the upper bound, and Paper 4 expects you to state them precisely.

Definition For a value rounded to a given degree of accuracy, take half of that unit and go each way:
lower bound = value − 12 unit     upper bound = value + 12 unit
For 27 cm to the nearest cm the unit is 1 cm, half of it is 0.5, so the bounds are 26.5 and 27.5.

The interval on a number line

Why one end uses ≤ and the other uses <

A value of exactly 26.5 rounds up to 27, so it belongs inside the interval. A value of exactly 27.5 rounds up to 28, so it does not. That is why the correct statement is

26.5 ≤ l < 27.5 The upper bound is 27.5, even though the length can never actually equal it
Watch out Do not write the upper bound as 27.49 or 27.4999. The examiner wants the exact boundary value 27.5, written with a < sign. Writing 27.5 with a ≤ sign loses the accuracy mark in a “write down an inequality” question, but 27.5 is still the number you use in every calculation.
Example 1The length of a rod is 12.4 cm correct to 1 decimal place. Write down an inequality for the true length l.
1
1 decimal place means the value was rounded to the nearest 0.1. Halve that unit. 0.1 ÷ 2 = 0.05
2
Subtract and add the half unit. 12.4 − 0.05 = 12.35
12.4 + 0.05 = 12.45
3
Write it as one inequality, using ≤ at the bottom and < at the top.
12.35 ≤ l < 12.45

Bounds for any degree of accuracy

Example 2The mass of a lorry is 8300 kg, correct to 2 significant figures. Write down its lower and upper bounds.
1
The two significant figures are the 8 and the 3. The 3 stands for 300, so the mass was rounded to the nearest 100 kg.
2
Half the unit is 50. 8300 − 50 = 8250
8300 + 50 = 8350
lower bound 8250 kg, upper bound 8350 kg  (8250 ≤ m < 8350)
Exam trap Reading 8300 as “nearest 1” gives 8299.5 and 8300.5. Always read the stated degree of accuracy before you touch the numbers.

Calculating with bounds

When rounded measurements are combined, the answer also has bounds. The whole skill is deciding which bound of each quantity to use, and for a difference and a quotient you have to mix them.

CalculationMaximum usesMinimum uses
a + bUBa + UBbLBa + LBb
a − bUBa − LBbLBa − UBb
a × bUBa × UBbLBa × LBb
a ÷ bUBa ÷ LBbLBa ÷ UBb

Why a difference mixes the bounds

To make a − b as big as possible, start with as much as possible and take away as little as possible — so use the upper bound of a and the lower bound of b. Reverse it for the minimum.

Why a quotient mixes them too

A fraction is largest when the numerator is largest and the denominator is smallest. So the maximum of ab is UBaLBb. Dividing by a smaller number gives a bigger answer.

Watch out These rules are for positive quantities, which is what every 0580 measurement question uses. Also, do not round the bounds themselves. Carry the exact boundary values through the whole calculation and round only the final answer — to 3 significant figures unless the question says otherwise.
Example 3A rectangle measures 8.4 cm by 5.7 cm, each correct to 1 decimal place. Find the upper and lower bounds of (a) its perimeter, (b) its area.
1
Half unit is 0.05, so write down the four bounds first. Set them out in a little table; it prevents nearly every mistake. length: 8.35 ≤ l < 8.45
width: 5.65 ≤ w < 5.75
2
(a) Perimeter is a sum, so the largest perimeter comes from the largest sides. max P = 2 × (8.45 + 5.75) = 2 × 14.2 = 28.4 cm
3
The smallest perimeter comes from the smallest sides. min P = 2 × (8.35 + 5.65) = 2 × 14.0 = 28 cm
4
(b) Area is a product, so multiply upper by upper and lower by lower. max A = 8.45 × 5.75 = 48.5875 cm²
min A = 8.35 × 5.65 = 47.1775 cm²
(a) 28 cm ≤ P < 28.4 cm    (b) 47.1775 cm² ≤ A < 48.5875 cm²
Exam tip Give bounds exactly when they are exact, as here. Rounding 48.5875 to 48.6 changes an upper bound into something the area could actually exceed.

More worked examples

Example 4Two masses are a = 15.6 g and b = 9.2 g, each correct to 1 decimal place. Find the upper and lower bounds of a − b.
1
Write the bounds down first. 15.55 ≤ a < 15.65
9.15 ≤ b < 9.25
2
For the maximum difference, take the largest possible a and the smallest possible b. max = 15.65 − 9.15 = 6.5
3
For the minimum, take the smallest a and the largest b. min = 15.55 − 9.25 = 6.3
6.3 g ≤ a − b ≤ 6.5 g
Exam trap Doing 15.65 − 9.25 = 6.4 uses both upper bounds and scores nothing. A difference always mixes an upper with a lower.
Example 5A runner covers 250 m, measured to the nearest 10 m, in 20 s, measured to the nearest second. Find the upper and lower bounds of her average speed, giving each to 3 significant figures.
1
Bounds of the distance (half unit 5) and of the time (half unit 0.5). 245 ≤ d < 255
19.5 ≤ t < 20.5
2
Speed is a quotient, so the fastest speed uses the longest distance in the shortest time. max speed = 255 ÷ 19.5 = 13.0769…
3
The slowest speed uses the shortest distance in the longest time. min speed = 245 ÷ 20.5 = 11.9512…
4
Round only now, to 3 significant figures.
min = 11.95 m/s  (12.0 m/s to 3 sf)    max = 13.1 m/s to 3 sf
Exam tip Note which way round the bounds go: a bigger denominator gives a smaller answer. Saying “fastest = furthest in the least time” out loud gets it right every time.
Example 6A metal sample has mass 450 g to the nearest 10 g and volume 60 cm³ correct to 2 significant figures. Find the upper bound of its density, in g/cm³, correct to 3 significant figures.
1
Mass: half unit 5. Volume to 2 sf means to the nearest 1, so half unit 0.5. 445 ≤ m < 455
59.5 ≤ V < 60.5
2
density = mass ÷ volume, so the upper bound needs the largest mass squeezed into the smallest volume. max density = 455 ÷ 59.5 = 7.6470…
3
(For completeness, the lower bound is 445 ÷ 60.5 = 7.3554…)
upper bound of density = 7.65 g/cm³ (3 sf)
Example 7A car travels 120 m, correct to the nearest metre, in 15.0 s, correct to the nearest 0.1 s. (a) Calculate the upper bound of the average speed. (b) Calculate the lower bound. (c) Use your answers to give the speed to a suitable degree of accuracy.
1
Bounds first. Nearest metre gives half unit 0.5; nearest 0.1 s gives 0.05. 119.5 ≤ d < 120.5
14.95 ≤ t < 15.05
2
(a) Upper bound of a quotient: biggest top, smallest bottom. 120.5 ÷ 14.95 = 8.0602… = 8.06 m/s (3 sf)
3
(b) Lower bound: smallest top, biggest bottom. 119.5 ÷ 15.05 = 7.9402… = 7.94 m/s (3 sf)
4
(c) Compare the two bounds digit by digit. They disagree in the first decimal place (8.0… and 7.9…) but both round to 8 to 1 significant figure, so that is as far as you may safely go. 8.06… → 8    7.94… → 8
(a) 8.06 m/s  (b) 7.94 m/s  (c) speed = 8 m/s (1 sf)
How part (c) is marked You must justify it: “both bounds round to 8 to 1 significant figure”. A bare answer of 8 with no comparison of the bounds scores nothing.

Your turn

Practice set

  1. Write an inequality for the true value of: (a) 36 cm to the nearest cm  (b) 4.70 kg to 2 dp  (c) 1500 m to the nearest 100 m.
  2. The population of a town is 42 000, correct to 2 significant figures. Write down the lower and upper bounds.
  3. p = 6.3 and q = 2.8, each correct to 1 decimal place. Find the upper bound of p + q and the lower bound of p − q.
  4. A square has side 7.5 cm correct to 1 decimal place. Find the upper and lower bounds of its area.
  5. A car uses 45 litres of fuel, to the nearest litre, to travel 560 km, to the nearest 10 km. Find the maximum possible number of km per litre, to 3 significant figures.
  6. A rectangle has area 96 cm² correct to the nearest cm² and one side of length 8.0 cm correct to 1 decimal place. Find the upper bound of the other side, to 3 sf.

  1. (a) half unit 0.5 → 35.5 ≤ x < 36.5  (b) half unit 0.005 → 4.695 ≤ x < 4.705  (c) half unit 50 → 1450 ≤ x < 1550
  2. 2 sf means the nearest 1000, so half unit 500 → 41 500 and 42 500
  3. Bounds: 6.25 ≤ p < 6.35, 2.75 ≤ q < 2.85. UB(p+q) = 6.35 + 2.85 = 9.2; LB(p−q) = 6.25 − 2.85 = 3.4
  4. 7.45 ≤ s < 7.55; area = s², so 7.45² = 55.5025 cm² and 7.55² = 57.0025 cm²
  5. Distance 555 ≤ d < 565, fuel 44.5 ≤ f < 45.5. Maximum = 565 ÷ 44.5 = 12.696… → 12.7 km per litre
  6. Area 95.5 ≤ A < 96.5, side 7.95 ≤ s < 8.05. Other side = A ÷ s, so the upper bound is 96.5 ÷ 7.95 = 12.138… → 12.1 cm

Key points to remember

  • Bounds are always the rounded value ± half of the unit it was rounded to.
  • Find that unit from the wording: nearest 10, 1 dp (0.1), 2 dp (0.01), or the place value of the last significant figure.
  • Write the interval as LB ≤ x < UB — the lower bound is included, the upper bound is not, but the upper bound is still the exact boundary number.
  • Sum: max = UB + UB, min = LB + LB.
  • Difference: max = UBa − LBb, min = LBa − UBb — mix the bounds.
  • Product: max = UB × UB, min = LB × LB.
  • Quotient: max = UBa ÷ LBb, min = LBa ÷ UBb — a smaller denominator gives a bigger answer.
  • Never round a bound part-way through; round only the final answer, to 3 significant figures unless told otherwise.
