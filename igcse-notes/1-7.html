1.7  Rounding and Estimating

Rounding to decimal places and significant figures, and using 1-figure estimates to check that an answer is sensible.

Rounding Sig figures Estimating Examples Your turn Key points

Rounding to a given place

Rounding replaces a number by a nearby, simpler one. The method never changes:

The rounding rule Find the deciding digit — the first digit you are about to throw away. If it is 5 or more, round the last kept digit up; if it is 4 or less, leave the last kept digit alone. Then delete everything after the kept digits (replacing them with zeros if they are in front of the decimal point).

Why 347 rounds to 350

Decimal places

“2 decimal places” (2 d.p.) means keep exactly two digits after the decimal point.

Nearest 10, 100 or 1000

Exactly the same rule, but the deciding digit is now to the left of the point, and the discarded digits are replaced by zeros to hold the place value.

Example 1Round 7.4863 to (a) 3 d.p., (b) 2 d.p., (c) 1 d.p.
1
(a) Keep three decimals: 7.486. The deciding digit is the fourth decimal, 3, which is less than 5, so nothing changes. 7.486|3  →  7.486
2
(b) Keep two decimals: 7.48. The deciding digit is 6, so round the 8 up to 9. 7.48|63  →  7.49
3
(c) Keep one decimal: 7.4. The deciding digit is 8, so round the 4 up to 5. 7.4|863  →  7.5
(a) 7.486    (b) 7.49    (c) 7.5
Never round twice Going from 7.4863 to 7.49 and then to 7.5 happens to work, but the method is unsafe: 0.449 → 0.45 → 0.5, whereas 0.449 to 1 d.p. is 0.4. Always round the original number.

Significant figures

Significant figures count from the first non-zero digit, reading left to right. They tell you how many digits actually carry information, whatever the size of the number.

Which zeros are significant?
  • Zeros before the first non-zero digit are never significant: in 0.00408 the significant figures are 4, 0, 8.
  • Zeros between significant digits always are: 4.05 has 3 s.f.
  • Zeros at the end of a whole number are place-holders, not extra accuracy: 46 000 written to 2 s.f. has significant figures 4 and 6.

Exam tip Unless a question says otherwise, 0580 answers are given correct to 3 significant figures (or exactly, if the answer is exact). Money is the usual exception: give it to 2 decimal places. Angles are usually asked to 1 decimal place.
Example 2Write 0.0030649 correct to 3 significant figures.
1
Find the first significant figure — the first non-zero digit. The two zeros after the point are only place-holders. 0.0030649  →  1st s.f. is 3
2
Count on: 3 (1st), 0 (2nd), 6 (3rd). The deciding digit is the next one, 4. 0.00306|49
3
4 is less than 5, so the 6 stays as it is and everything after it goes.
0.00306 (3 s.f.)
Note The leading zeros must stay — they fix the size of the number. Deleting them would turn 0.00306 into 3.06.

Estimating

An estimate is a quick, calculator-free check. The 0580 instruction is almost always the same: “By writing each number correct to 1 significant figure, estimate the value of…” You must show the rounded numbers — that is where the method mark lives.

38.7 × 5.20.49 ≈ 40 × 50.5 = 2000.5 = 400 Each number to 1 significant figure, then work it out exactly
Do not round the answer as well Round the numbers first, then do the arithmetic exactly. Writing 200 ÷ 0.5 ≈ 400 is right; writing ≈ 100 because “0.5 is about 1” throws away the whole point.

Estimating a square root

Trap the number between the two square numbers either side, then judge where it sits.

48

36 < 48 < 49, so 6 < 48 < 7. 48 is very close to 49, so the root is just under 7 — about 6.9.

(The true value is 6.928…)

19.6 × 4.1

Round first: 20 × 4 = 80. Then 64 < 80 < 81, so the answer is just under 9 — about 9.

(The true value is 8.964…)

Over-estimate or under-estimate?

What you didEffect on the estimate
Rounded a number on the top upestimate gets bigger
Rounded a number on the top downestimate gets smaller
Rounded the bottom upestimate gets smaller
Rounded the bottom downestimate gets bigger

If every rounding pushes the same way you can state the answer with certainty. If they pull in opposite directions, compare the estimate with the calculator value and say which is larger.

Example 3Estimate the value of 38.7 × 5.20.49 and say whether your estimate is an over- or under-estimate.
1
Write each number correct to 1 significant figure. 38.7 → 40,   5.2 → 5,   0.49 → 0.5
2
Work out the top exactly. 40 × 5 = 200
3
Dividing by 0.5 is the same as multiplying by 2. 200 ÷ 0.5 = 400
4
Two of the three roundings push the estimate down (5.2 → 5 shrinks the top; 0.49 → 0.5 enlarges the bottom), so compare with the exact value to be sure. 201.24 ÷ 0.49 = 410.7…  >  400
Estimate ≈ 400, an under-estimate

More worked examples

Example 4Round (a) 19.97 to 1 decimal place, (b) 0.9962 to 2 significant figures.
1
(a) Keep one decimal: 19.9. The deciding digit is 7, so the 9 must round up. That makes it 10, so carry into the units column. 19.9|7  →  19.9 + 0.1 = 20.0
2
(b) The first two significant figures are 9 and 9; the deciding digit is 6. 0.99|62  →  0.99 + 0.01 = 1.00
3
Two significant figures were asked for, so write 1.0 — the digits 1 and 0.
(a) 20.0    (b) 1.0
Watch out Answers of “20” and “1” would lose the mark: they do not show the required accuracy.

Exam tip An estimate question is worth 2 marks: one for the rounded numbers, one for the value. Never write only the final number.
Example 7Work out 2.7 × 3.61.9 correct to 3 significant figures, and show what goes wrong if you round part-way through.
1
Correct method — keep everything. 2.7 × 3.6 = 9.72
9.72 ÷ 1.9 = 5.11578…
2
Round only now. The fourth significant figure is 7, so the 1 in the hundredths place rounds up to 2. 5.11578… → 5.12
3
Wrong method — rounding the middle value 9.72 to 2 s.f. first. 9.7 ÷ 1.9 = 5.10526… → 5.11
5.12 (3 s.f.) — premature rounding gave 5.11, which is wrong
Exam tip Use Ans or the calculator memory between stages. If you must write a middle value down, write at least 4 or 5 significant figures.

Key points to remember

  • Find the deciding digit — the first digit thrown away. 5 or more rounds up, 4 or less leaves the kept digit alone.
  • Always round the original number. Rounding in two stages can give the wrong answer.
  • Significant figures start at the first non-zero digit. Leading zeros in a decimal are never significant; zeros in the middle always are.
  • When you cut a whole number short, fill the gap with zeros to hold the place value: 45 682 to 2 s.f. is 46 000.
  • Keep the trailing zero the accuracy demands: 2.397 to 2 d.p. is 2.40, and 0.9962 to 2 s.f. is 1.0.
  • To estimate, write every number to 1 significant figure, show that line, then work the result out exactly.
  • For a square root, trap the number between the two square numbers on either side.
  • Rounding the top up (or the bottom down) makes an estimate too big; the reverse makes it too small.
  • Never round mid-calculation — use Ans — and give final answers to 3 significant figures unless the question says otherwise.
