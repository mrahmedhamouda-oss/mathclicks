15.1  Scale Drawings

How to read a scale, convert between a drawing and real life in both directions, handle areas correctly, and draw plans and elevations.

What a scale means

A scale drawing is a picture of something real, drawn so that every length is reduced (or enlarged) by the same factor. A map, a floor plan and a model aeroplane are all scale drawings. The scale tells you how to get from the drawing to real life.

Definition A scale is the ratio drawing length : real length, with both lengths measured in the same unit. It is written with the drawing side reduced to 1, for example 1 : 50 000.

The two ways a scale is written

As a ratio: 1 : n

1 : 50 000 means 1 unit on the drawing represents 50 000 of the same units in real life. There are no units written down, because it works for any unit: 1 cm represents 50 000 cm, and 1 inch would represent 50 000 inches.

The number n is called the scale factor.

In words: “1 cm represents 2 km”

Here the units are different on the two sides, which is much friendlier for a map. To turn it back into a ratio you must first make the units match:

2 km = 2000 m = 200 000 cm so “1 cm represents 2 km” is the scale 1 : 200 000

The same house, drawn to a scale of 1 cm to 2 m

Watch out A ratio scale has no units. Writing “1 cm : 50 000 km” is wrong, and so is “the scale is 50 000”. Write either 1 : 50 000 or “1 cm represents 0.5 km”.
Example 1A map has a scale of 1 : 25 000. Write this scale in the form “1 cm represents …”, in metres and in kilometres.
1
Start with the ratio. The scale factor multiplies the drawing length, and both sides are in the same unit. 1 cm on the map → 25 000 cm in real life
2
Convert centimetres to metres by dividing by 100. 25 000 ÷ 100 = 250 m
3
Convert metres to kilometres by dividing by 1000. 250 ÷ 1000 = 0.25 km
1 cm represents 250 m, that is 0.25 km
Exam tip Learn the two common ones by heart: 1 : 50 000 is “1 cm to 0.5 km” and 1 : 100 000 is “1 cm to 1 km”. Everything else you can build from ÷100 then ÷1000.

Converting in both directions

Only two operations exist, and choosing the right one is the whole battle.

Drawing → real

You are making things bigger, so multiply.

real = drawing × n

Real → drawing

You are making things smaller, so divide.

drawing = real ÷ n
Exam tip Before you press a single calculator button, ask: “should my answer be bigger or smaller than the number I started with?” On a 1 : 50 000 map a real distance is always a much bigger number than the map distance. That one sentence catches nearly every ×/÷ slip.
Example 2A map is drawn to a scale of 1 : 50 000. (a) Two villages are 7.4 cm apart on the map. Find the real distance in km. (b) A straight road is 12 km long. How long is it on the map?
1
(a) Map to real, so multiply by the scale factor. Keep everything in centimetres first. 7.4 × 50 000 = 370 000 cm
2
Now convert to kilometres: divide by 100, then by 1000. 370 000 cm = 3700 m = 3.7 km
3
(b) Real to map, so divide. Convert the real length to centimetres first so the units match the scale. 12 km = 12 000 m = 1 200 000 cm
4
Divide by the scale factor. 1 200 000 ÷ 50 000 = 24 cm
(a) 3.7 km     (b) 24 cm
Watch out Never mix units inside one calculation. Get both sides into centimetres, do the multiplication or division, and only then convert the answer to the unit the question asks for.

Choosing a scale and making the drawing

If a question says “choose a suitable scale”, you get the marks for a scale that makes the drawing as large as possible while still fitting the space, and that is easy to work with.

How to choose a scale in three moves

  1. Find the largest real length you must draw.
  2. Divide it by the space available: this is the smallest number of real units each centimetre must carry.
  3. Round that up to a friendly number — 1, 2, 5, 10, 20, 50, 100, 200, 500, …
Note Friendly scales matter because you will have to convert several lengths by hand. “1 cm to 5 m” is pleasant; “1 cm to 4.7 m” is a guaranteed arithmetic slip.

Drawing accurately

For a construction question the examiner is checking your ruler and protractor, so accuracy marks are usually within ±2 mm and ±2°. Always write the scale on your drawing, label every vertex, and leave your construction lines showing.

Example 3A triangular field ABC has AB = 40 m, AC = 30 m and angle BAC = 55°. (a) Choose a suitable scale for a drawing that fits inside a space 10 cm wide. (b) Make the scale drawing and use it to find the length of BC.
1
(a) The longest side is 40 m and it must fit in 10 cm. 40 ÷ 10 = 4, so 1 cm must carry at least 4 m
2
Round up to a friendly value: use 1 cm to 5 m. As a ratio, 5 m = 500 cm. scale 1 : 500
3
Convert the two given sides by dividing by 5. AB: 40 ÷ 5 = 8 cm   AC: 30 ÷ 5 = 6 cm
4
Draw AB = 8 cm. At A measure 55° with a protractor, draw a ray, and mark C 6 cm along it. Join CB.
5
Measure BC on the drawing and convert back by multiplying by 5. BC = 6.7 cm → 6.7 × 5 = 33.5 m
Scale 1 : 500   ·   BC ≈ 33.5 m
Exam tip A measured answer is never exact, so anything from about 33 m to 34 m would score. State the scale you used — without it the examiner cannot follow your conversion.

Reading a map, and why areas are different

Distances on a map behave exactly as you expect: multiply by the scale factor. Areas do not. An area is a length multiplied by a length, so both lengths get multiplied by the scale factor.

Key idea If lengths are multiplied by k, then areas are multiplied by k² (and volumes by k³).

Why the factor is squared

Watch out This is one of the most heavily penalised errors on Paper 4. On a 1 : 25 000 map, 1 cm² does not represent 25 000 cm² — it represents 25 000² = 625 000 000 cm².

In practice the safest route is to work out what 1 cm represents in a sensible unit first, then square that number to get what 1 cm² represents.

1 cm → 0.25 km   so   1 cm² → 0.25² = 0.0625 km² Square the “1 cm represents” number, not the ratio
Example 4A map has a scale of 1 : 25 000. A forest covers 12 cm² on the map. Find the real area of the forest in km².
1
Work out what 1 cm represents, in kilometres. 25 000 cm = 250 m = 0.25 km
2
Square it to find what 1 cm² represents. 0.25² = 0.0625, so 1 cm² → 0.0625 km²
3
Multiply by the map area. 12 × 0.0625 = 0.75 km²
real area = 0.75 km²
Check it the long way 12 × 25 000² = 7.5 × 109 cm², and 1 km² = 1010 cm², so the area is 7.5 × 109 ÷ 1010 = 0.75 km². Same answer, many more chances to slip.

Plans and elevations

A solid is often described by three flat scale drawings, each one what you see looking straight at the solid from a particular direction. There is no perspective and no hidden depth — you draw exactly the outline you would see, plus any edge where the surface steps.

Plan

The view from directly above, looking straight down.

Front elevation

The view from the front, looking horizontally.

Side elevation

The view from the side, looking horizontally, at right angles to the front.

The three viewing directions

Exam tip Elevation questions are usually drawn on centimetre squared paper, so they are scale drawings too: count squares carefully and mark every internal edge where the solid changes height. A missing step line is the commonest lost mark.

More worked examples

Example 5The solid below is a step: a cuboid 6 cm by 3 cm by 2 cm with a second cuboid 3 cm by 3 cm by 2 cm sitting on its left-hand half. Draw the plan, the front elevation and the side elevation seen from the right.
1
Plan. Looking down, the outline is just the footprint of the bottom cuboid, because nothing sticks out beyond it. a 6 cm by 3 cm rectangle Draw one extra line 3 cm from the left edge: that is the top edge of the step, which you can see from above.
2
Front elevation. Looking from the front you see the step shape itself: 6 cm wide overall, 2 cm high on the right, 4 cm high on the left. an L-shape, 6 cm wide and 4 cm tall
3
Side elevation from the right. The width you see is the depth of the solid, 3 cm, and the height is the full 4 cm, because the tall block hides behind the short one. a 3 cm by 4 cm rectangle Draw a line across it 2 cm up: that is the top edge of the lower cuboid.
plan 6 × 3 ·  front an L-shape 6 wide by 4 tall ·  side 3 × 4
Watch out The side elevation is a rectangle, not an L-shape. From the side the tall part is directly behind the short part, so its outline adds nothing new — but the edge where the step happens is still visible, so the internal line must be drawn.
Example 6A model car is made to a scale of 1 : 24. The real car is 4.32 m long and its windscreen has an area of 1.44 m². Find (a) the length of the model in cm, (b) the area of the model windscreen in cm².
1
(a) Real to model, so divide by 24. Convert the real length to centimetres first. 4.32 m = 432 cm
2
432 ÷ 24 = 18 cm
3
(b) For area the factor is squared. 24² = 576
4
Convert the real area to cm² — remember 1 m² = 100² = 10 000 cm². 1.44 m² = 1.44 × 10 000 = 14 400 cm²
5
Divide by the area factor. 14 400 ÷ 576 = 25 cm²
(a) 18 cm     (b) 25 cm²
Watch out 1 m² is 10 000 cm², not 100. Squaring the unit conversion is just as important as squaring the scale factor.
Example 7A map has a scale of 1 : 20 000. A rectangular field measures 5 cm by 3.5 cm on this map. (a) Find the real dimensions in metres. (b) Find the real area in km². (c) The same field is redrawn on a map of scale 1 : 50 000. Find its dimensions on the new map.
1
(a) First find what 1 cm represents. 20 000 cm = 200 m
2
Multiply each map length by 200 m. 5 × 200 = 1000 m   3.5 × 200 = 700 m
3
(b) Real area from real lengths. 1000 × 700 = 700 000 m²
4
Convert to km² using 1 km² = 1000² = 1 000 000 m². 700 000 ÷ 1 000 000 = 0.7 km²
5
(c) On the new map 1 cm represents 50 000 cm = 500 m, so divide the real lengths by 500. 1000 ÷ 500 = 2 cm   700 ÷ 500 = 1.4 cm
(a) 1000 m by 700 m  ·  (b) 0.7 km²  ·  (c) 2 cm by 1.4 cm
Exam tip Check part (b) with the squared factor: 1 cm represents 0.2 km, so 1 cm² represents 0.2² = 0.04 km², and the map area is 5 × 3.5 = 17.5 cm². Then 17.5 × 0.04 = 0.7 km². Two independent methods agreeing is worth the twenty seconds.

Your turn

Practice set

  1. A map has a scale of 1 : 200 000. Complete: 1 cm represents ___ km.
  2. On a 1 : 25 000 map two masts are 6.8 cm apart. Find the real distance in km.
  3. A road is 4.5 km long. How long is it on a 1 : 50 000 map?
  4. A model is built to a scale of 1 : 20. The real object is 3.6 m long and one face has area 2.4 m². Find the model's length in cm and that face's area in cm².
  5. A lake covers 8 cm² on a 1 : 50 000 map. Find its real area in km².
  6. A rectangular garden is 18 m by 12 m. (a) Choose a suitable scale so that the drawing fits inside a box 10 cm by 8 cm. (b) Give the dimensions of the drawing. (c) How long is the diagonal path on the drawing, and in real life?

  1. 200 000 cm = 2000 m → 2 km
  2. 1 cm represents 0.25 km, so 6.8 × 0.25 = 1.7 km
  3. 4.5 km = 450 000 cm; 450 000 ÷ 50 000 = 9 cm
  4. Length 3.6 m = 360 cm, 360 ÷ 20 = 18 cm. Area factor 20² = 400; 2.4 m² = 24 000 cm², 24 000 ÷ 400 = 60 cm²
  5. 1 cm → 0.5 km so 1 cm² → 0.5² = 0.25 km²; 8 × 0.25 = 2 km²
  6. (a) 18 ÷ 10 = 1.8, so round up to 1 cm to 2 m (that is 1 : 200); check the other side: 12 ÷ 2 = 6 ≤ 8 ✓  (b) 9 cm by 6 cm  (c) diagonal = √(9² + 6²) = √117 = 10.8 cm, so real = 10.8 × 2 = 21.6 m

Key points to remember

  • A scale is drawing : real with both sides in the same unit, written as 1 : n. A ratio scale never has units attached.
  • To turn 1 : n into words, convert n centimetres: ÷100 gives metres, then ÷1000 gives kilometres.
  • Drawing → real: multiply by n. Real → drawing: divide by n. Ask yourself whether the answer should be bigger or smaller before you calculate.
  • Convert units before you multiply or divide, and never mix units inside one line of working.
  • Areas scale by k², not by k (and volumes by k³). Squaring “what 1 cm represents” is the safest route.
  • Remember the squared unit conversions too: 1 m² = 10 000 cm² and 1 km² = 1 000 000 m².
  • Choose a scale by dividing the longest real length by the space available, then rounding up to a friendly number such as 1, 2, 5, 10, 20, 50 or 100.
  • In a construction, work to ±2 mm and ±2°, label every point, and always write the scale on the drawing.
  • The plan is the view from above, the front and side elevations are the horizontal views; draw internal lines wherever the solid steps.
