19.1  Symmetry in Two Dimensions

Lines of symmetry and order of rotational symmetry — how to count them, how to use them to complete a shape, and the results you are expected to know for every standard polygon.

Line symmetry

A shape has line symmetry if one half is the mirror image of the other. The mirror is called a line of symmetry (also called an axis of symmetry or a mirror line). Two tests decide it, and both are worth knowing because different questions suit different tests.

Definition A line of symmetry is a line about which a shape can be reflected onto itself. Every point of the shape has a matching point the same perpendicular distance on the other side of the line.

Test 1 — the folding test

Imagine folding the shape along the line. If the two halves land exactly on top of each other, with no overhang, the fold line is a line of symmetry.

This is the test to use in the exam room: trace the shape, fold, look.

Test 2 — the mirror test

Put a mirror along the line. If what you see (half the shape plus its reflection) looks identical to the whole original shape, it is a line of symmetry.

Tracing paper does the same job: trace one half, flip it over the line, check it fits.

Counting the mirror lines

Watch out A diagonal of a rectangle is not a line of symmetry. Fold a non-square rectangle along its diagonal and the two halves do not fit — they are congruent, but one is a rotation of the other, not a reflection. The same trap appears with the diagonal of a parallelogram.
Example 1Write down the number of lines of symmetry of (a) an isosceles trapezium, (b) a parallelogram that is not a rectangle, (c) a regular pentagon, (d) a kite.
1
(a) An isosceles trapezium has one pair of equal sloping sides, so folding down the middle matches them. 1 line — the vertical line through the midpoints of the parallel sides
2
(b) Try both diagonals and both centre lines of a parallelogram — none of them fold onto themselves. 0 lines
3
(c) A regular polygon with n sides has n lines of symmetry. 5 lines
4
(d) A kite folds along the diagonal joining the two vertices where unequal sides meet. 1 line — the long diagonal only
(a) 1    (b) 0    (c) 5    (d) 1

Rotational symmetry

Turn a shape about its centre. If it fits exactly onto its own outline before you have gone all the way round, it has rotational symmetry. The order counts how many times it fits in one complete turn.

Definition The order of rotational symmetry is the number of positions, in a full turn of 360°, in which the shape looks exactly the same as it did at the start. The fixed point it turns about is the centre of rotation.
angle of the smallest rotation = 360°order Order 4 → it fits every 90°. Order 3 → every 120°.

Order 2 and order 3

Order 1 means none Every shape fits onto itself after a full 360° turn, so the smallest possible order is 1. An answer of “order 1” means the shape has no rotational symmetry. Never write order 0.
Example 2Write down the order of rotational symmetry of (a) a rectangle, (b) an equilateral triangle, (c) a regular octagon, (d) a scalene triangle. For (c), state the angle of the smallest rotation.
1
(a) A rectangle turned 180° about its centre fits its outline; a quarter turn does not (unless it is a square). order 2
2
(b) and (c) A regular n-sided polygon has order n. order 3  and  order 8
3
(d) A scalene triangle has no matching sides, so only a full turn works. order 1 (no rotational symmetry)
4
Smallest rotation for the octagon: 360° ÷ 8 = 45°
(a) 2    (b) 3    (c) 8, rotating 45°    (d) 1

Example 3A regular polygon has rotational symmetry of order 12. Find (a) the number of lines of symmetry, (b) the angle of the smallest rotation that maps it onto itself, (c) the size of one interior angle.
1
(a) For a regular polygon the order equals the number of sides, and the number of lines of symmetry also equals the number of sides. 12 sides → 12 lines of symmetry
2
(b) Divide a full turn by the order. 360° ÷ 12 = 30°
3
(c) The exterior angle of a regular 12-gon is also 360° ÷ 12 = 30°, and interior + exterior = 180°. interior angle = 180° − 30° = 150°
(a) 12    (b) 30°    (c) 150°

Completing a shape and reading patterns

A very common question gives you part of a shape and the symmetry it must have, and asks you to finish it. Work point by point — never try to sketch the whole thing by eye.

Completing for line symmetry

For each marked point, count the squares perpendicular to the mirror line, then count the same number across on the other side and mark the image. Join the images in the same order.

Completing for rotational symmetry

Put tracing paper over the shape, mark the centre, trace, then turn the paper through 360° ÷ order and copy the shape into its new position. Repeat until you are back at the start.

Example 4A triangle has vertices at (1, 1), (3, 1) and (1, 2). A second triangle is drawn so that the completed shape has line symmetry about x = 4. Find the coordinates of the second triangle’s vertices.
1
The mirror is vertical, so the y-coordinates do not change. Only x moves.
2
Find how far each point is from the mirror line, then go that far past it: (1, 1) is 3 to the left of x = 4, so its image is 3 to the right. x’ = 4 + 3 = 7  →  (7, 1)
3
Repeat. A quick rule for a vertical mirror x = a is x’ = 2a − x, here x’ = 8 − x. (3, 1) → (5, 1)    (1, 2) → (7, 2)
(7, 1),  (5, 1)  and  (7, 2)

Symmetry of capital letters

Watch out N, S and Z catch people out: they have no line of symmetry at all, yet turning them 180° gives back the same letter, so their order is 2. Line symmetry and rotational symmetry are separate questions — always answer both.

More worked examples

Example 5The shape below is made from four identical squares. Write down its number of lines of symmetry and its order of rotational symmetry.
1
Try a vertical mirror down the middle: the top pair of squares would have to sit above the bottom pair, and they do not. Try a horizontal mirror, then the two diagonals — none of them fold the shape onto itself. 0 lines of symmetry
2
Now turn it. The centre of the whole tile is the orange dot. A half turn sends the top-left square to the bottom-right square and vice versa, so the outline is unchanged. fits after 180°, then again after 360°
0 lines of symmetry, rotational symmetry of order 2
Exam tip When the shape is drawn on squared paper, use the paper. Count squares to test a mirror line, and find the centre of rotation by looking for the middle of the whole shape, not the middle of one piece.
Example 6From the capital letters A,  H,  N,  S,  T,  X,  Z write down those with (a) exactly one line of symmetry, (b) rotational symmetry of order 2, (c) both line symmetry and rotational symmetry.
1
Take them one at a time and record both numbers. A: 1 line, order 1    H: 2 lines, order 2    N: 0 lines, order 2
S: 0 lines, order 2    T: 1 line, order 1    X: 2 lines, order 2    Z: 0 lines, order 2
2
(a) Pick the ones with exactly one mirror line. A and T
3
(b) Pick the ones that map onto themselves after a half turn. H, N, S, X, Z
4
(c) Pick the ones appearing in both a line-symmetry list and the order-2 list. H and X
(a) A, T    (b) H, N, S, X, Z    (c) H, X
Example 7Two squares of a 4 × 4 grid are shaded. Shade two more squares so that the completed pattern has rotational symmetry of order 2 about the centre of the grid, but no line symmetry.
1
Order 2 means a 180° turn about the centre of the grid must reproduce the pattern. The centre is the orange dot, where the four middle squares meet.
2
A half turn about that centre sends the square in row r, column c to row 5 − r, column 5 − c. (row 1, col 2) → (row 4, col 3)
(row 2, col 4) → (row 3, col 1)
3
Shade those two squares (green above). Now check the mirror lines: reflecting in the vertical, horizontal or either diagonal moves at least one shaded square onto an unshaded one, so there is no line symmetry — exactly as required.
Shade row 4 column 3 and row 3 column 1
Watch out A half turn is not the same as a reflection. Students often shade the mirror image squares (row 1 column 3 and row 2 column 1) by mistake; that pattern would have a line of symmetry and would fail the question.

Your turn

Practice set

  1. Write down the number of lines of symmetry and the order of rotational symmetry of (a) a square, (b) a kite, (c) a parallelogram that is not a rectangle, (d) a regular polygon with 9 sides.
  2. A regular polygon has 15 lines of symmetry. Write down its order of rotational symmetry and the angle of the smallest rotation that maps it onto itself.
  3. From A,  H,  M,  S,  T,  Z, which letters have (a) exactly one line of symmetry, (b) rotational symmetry of order 2?
  4. A triangle has vertices (1, 1), (4, 1) and (1, 3). The completed shape must have line symmetry about y = 4. Write down the coordinates of the three image vertices.
  5. A shape has rotational symmetry of order 6. Through what angle must it turn to map onto itself for the first time? If the shape is a regular hexagon, how many lines of symmetry does it have?
  6. The four corner squares of a 3 × 3 grid are shaded. Write down the number of lines of symmetry and the order of rotational symmetry. Then state which single extra square could be shaded without changing either answer.

  1. (a) 4 lines, order 4  (b) 1 line, order 1  (c) 0 lines, order 2  (d) regular n-gon gives both numbers as n → 9 lines, order 9
  2. 15 lines → 15 sides → order 15; 360° ÷ 15 = 24°
  3. (a) A, M, T (all have one vertical mirror line)  (b) H, S, Z (H also has 2 lines; S and Z have none)
  4. Horizontal mirror y = 4, so y’ = 8 − y and x is unchanged → (1, 7), (4, 7), (1, 5)
  5. 360° ÷ 6 = 60°; a regular hexagon has 6 lines of symmetry
  6. The four corners give a pattern that folds along both centre lines and both diagonals: 4 lines, order 4. The centre square lies on every mirror line and at the centre of rotation, so shading the middle square changes nothing.

Key points to remember

  • A line of symmetry is a fold line: the two halves must land exactly on top of each other.
  • The order of rotational symmetry counts the positions in a full turn where the shape looks unchanged; the smallest rotation is 360° ÷ order.
  • Order 1 means no rotational symmetry. Never write order 0.
  • A regular polygon with n sides has n lines of symmetry and rotational symmetry of order n.
  • Parallelogram: 0 lines but order 2. Kite: 1 line but order 1. The two kinds of symmetry are independent — always answer both parts.
  • A diagonal of a rectangle or parallelogram is not a line of symmetry.
  • To complete a shape, work point by point: reflect each vertex the same perpendicular distance across the mirror, or turn each vertex through 360° ÷ order about the centre.
  • On squared paper use tracing paper: trace, fold or turn, and check the fit before you draw.
