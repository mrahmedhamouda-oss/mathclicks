15.2  Bearings

Measuring direction from north, writing bearings in three figures, finding back bearings and solving journey problems on a scale drawing.

What a bearing is

A bearing is how navigators, pilots and examiners describe a direction. Everyday words such as “north-east” are not precise enough, so a bearing gives the direction as an angle.

Definition A bearing is an angle that is (1) measured from the north line, (2) measured clockwise, and (3) written with three figures.

All three rules earn marks. A direction of 65 degrees must be written 065°, and a direction of 8 degrees must be written 008°. Bearings run from 000° all the way round to 360°, so a bearing is never negative and never more than 360°.

Two bearings, measured the only legal way

Watch out “The bearing of B from A” means you stand at A, draw the north line at A, and turn clockwise until you face B. The order of the letters decides where the north line goes, and swapping them changes the answer completely.
Example 1Write each of these directions as a three-figure bearing: north, north-east, east, south-east, south, south-west, west, north-west. Also write “a direction of 8 degrees east of north” correctly.
1
Start at north, which is 0°, and add 45° for each step clockwise round the compass. N 000°  NE 045°  E 090°  SE 135°
2
Keep going clockwise past south. S 180°  SW 225°  W 270°  NW 315°
3
For the last part, 8° is only one figure, so pad it to three with leading zeros. 8° → 008°
N 000°  NE 045°  E 090°  SE 135°  S 180°  SW 225°  W 270°  NW 315° ·  and 008°
Exam tip North can be written 000° or 360°; both are accepted. Everything else must have exactly three figures before the degree sign.

Reading and drawing a bearing

A protractor only measures up to 180°, so bearings split into two cases.

Bearing less than 180°

Put the protractor centre on the point, the zero line along north, and read the clockwise scale directly.

Bearing more than 180°

Measure the smaller angle anticlockwise from north instead, then subtract from 360°.

anticlockwise angle = 360° − bearing
Note Some protractors let you measure clockwise past 180° from the south line: if the angle from south, measured clockwise, is x, then the bearing is 180° + x. Use whichever you find easier — but always sketch the arrow first so you can see roughly what the answer should be.
Example 2The bearing of Y from X is 250°. Explain how to draw this accurately with a protractor.
1
Mark X and draw a north line straight up from it. Label it N.
2
250° > 180°, so a single protractor reading clockwise is impossible. Work out the anticlockwise angle instead. 360 − 250 = 110°
3
Place the protractor centre on X with its zero line along the north line, and measure 110° anticlockwise. Mark the point and draw the ray XY.
4
Sanity check the picture: 250° lies between 180° (south) and 270° (west), so the arrow must point into the south-west quarter. It does.

Back bearings

If you know the bearing of B from A, the bearing of A from B is called the back bearing. It is the direction of the return journey.

The rule
back bearing = bearing + 180°   (if the bearing is less than 180°)
back bearing = bearing − 180°   (if the bearing is 180° or more) Choose whichever keeps the answer between 000° and 360°

Why the rule works

North lines are parallel, and the line joining the two points is a transversal across them. The angle at A between north and AB, and the angle at B between north and BA, are co-interior (allied) angles, so they add up to 180°.

Two parallel north lines and one transversal

Exam tip If a question says “give a reason”, the reason is co-interior angles between parallel lines add to 180° (some books call them allied angles). Naming the angle fact is worth a mark on its own.
Example 3(a) The bearing of B from A is 062°. Find the bearing of A from B. (b) The bearing of Q from P is 205°. Find the bearing of P from Q.
1
(a) 062° is less than 180°, so add 180°. 62 + 180 = 242°
2
Check with the diagram: B is north-east of A, so A must be south-west of B. 242° is south-west. ✓
3
(b) 205° is more than 180°, so subtract 180° — adding would give 385°, which is not a bearing. 205 − 180 = 25° → 025°
(a) 242°     (b) 025°
Watch out Do not forget the third figure. Writing “25°” instead of “025°” can cost the accuracy mark.
Example 4The bearing of B from A is 118°. Use the north lines to find the bearing of A from B, giving a reason for each step.

Use the diagram of the two parallel north lines above.

1
The north lines at A and B are parallel, and AB is a transversal. So the angle at A and the angle at B marked on the diagram are co-interior angles and add to 180°. angle NAB = 118°
2
So the angle at B between its north line and BA is 180 − 118 = 62°
3
A lies to the west of B, so from north at B you must turn clockwise nearly all the way round. 360 − 62 = 298°
4
Confirm with the shortcut: 118 < 180, so add 180°. 118 + 180 = 298°  ✓
bearing of A from B = 298°
Why both methods agree The co-interior angle gives 180 − b on the other side of the north line, and going clockwise the long way round gives 360 − (180 − b) = 180 + b. That is the +180° rule.

Bearings on a scale drawing

Most bearing questions are really journey questions: go so far on one bearing, then so far on another, and describe where you end up. On Paper 4 you may be told to answer by accurate drawing.

The method

  1. Choose a scale and write it down, for example 1 cm represents 1 km.
  2. Mark the starting point and draw a north line at it.
  3. Measure the first bearing clockwise from that north line and draw the leg to the correct length.
  4. Draw a new north line at the next point — this is the step candidates forget — and repeat for the second bearing.
  5. Join the start to the finish, measure that length with a ruler and its bearing with a protractor, then convert the length back using the scale.
Example 5A walker leaves village V, walks 5 km on a bearing of 048° to W, then 7 km on a bearing of 155° to X. Using a scale of 1 cm to 1 km, find the distance and the bearing of X from V.
1
Choose the scale and convert the two distances. 1 cm to 1 km, so VW = 5 cm and WX = 7 cm
2
Draw a north line at V, measure 048° clockwise from it and draw VW = 5 cm.
3
Draw a new north line at W (parallel to the first), measure 155° clockwise from it and draw WX = 7 cm.
4
Join VX, measure it, and convert back with the scale. VX = 7.3 cm → 7.3 km
5
Measure the bearing of X from V clockwise from the north line at V. bearing = 114°
VX ≈ 7.3 km on a bearing of 114°
Exam tip The same journey can be calculated. The angle at W is (048 + 180) − 155 = 73°, so the cosine rule gives VX² = 5² + 7² − 2(5)(7)cos73° = 53.5, giving VX = 7.32 km. Calculated answers are exact; measured answers are accepted within about ±0.1 cm and ±2°.
Where this goes next When a bearing question says “calculate” rather than “by accurate drawing”, you need the tools of the rest of this chapter: Pythagoras and SOHCAHTOA when a right angle appears (very common, because bearings that differ by 90° give one), and the sine rule or cosine rule when it does not. The bearings work is identical — you still find the angle inside the triangle first.

More worked examples

Example 6From a point A, the bearing of B is 072° and the bearing of C is 145°. Given that AB = AC, find (a) angle BAC, (b) angle ABC, (c) the bearing of C from B.
1
(a) Both bearings are measured from the same north line at A, so subtract them. angle BAC = 145 − 72 = 73°
2
(b) AB = AC, so triangle ABC is isosceles and the two base angles are equal. angle ABC = (180 − 73) ÷ 2 = 53.5°
3
(c) Start from the direction you already know at B: the back bearing of A. bearing of A from B = 72 + 180 = 252°
4
Turning from BA to BC is a turn of 53.5°. Adding would give 305.5°, which points north-west; but the diagram shows C is south of B, so subtract. 252 − 53.5 = 198.5°
(a) 73°  ·  (b) 53.5°  ·  (c) 198.5°
Watch out Adding or subtracting the triangle angle is decided by the diagram, never by habit. Always sketch first and ask which quarter of the compass the answer should be in.
Example 7A ship sails from port P for 8 km on a bearing of 070° to a buoy Q, then 6 km on a bearing of 160° to a lighthouse R. Calculate the distance PR and the bearing of R from P.
1
Find the angle inside the triangle at Q. Start with the back bearing of P from Q. 70 + 180 = 250°
2
Both directions at Q are now measured from the same north line, so subtract. angle PQR = 250 − 160 = 90°
3
A right angle, so use Pythagoras for the distance. PR² = 8² + 6² = 64 + 36 = 100
PR = 10 km
4
For the bearing, first find angle QPR using tan (opposite 6, adjacent 8). tan(QPR) = 6 ÷ 8 = 0.75
QPR = 36.87°
5
R is further clockwise from north at P than Q is, so add. bearing of R from P = 70 + 36.87 = 106.87°
PR = 10 km on a bearing of 106.9° (3 s.f.)
Exam tip Give bearings to 1 decimal place or to the nearest degree only if the question says so — otherwise the usual 3 significant figures rule applies, so 106.9° is safe here. Keep the full value in your calculator until the very last line; rounding 36.87° to 37° early can shift the final answer.

Your turn

Practice set

  1. Write as three-figure bearings: (a) south-west  (b) a turn of 8° clockwise from north  (c) due north.
  2. Find the back bearing of (a) 073°  (b) 310°  (c) 180°.
  3. The bearing of X from Y is 148°. Find the bearing of Y from X.
  4. From A the bearing of B is 035° and the bearing of C is 290°. Find angle BAC.
  5. A boat sails 12 km due east, then 5 km due south. Calculate its distance and bearing from the start.
  6. B is 8 km from A on a bearing of 140°. C is 6 km from B on a bearing of 210°. Calculate AC and the bearing of C from A.

  1. (a) 225°  (b) 008°  (c) 000° (or 360°)
  2. (a) 73 + 180 = 253°  (b) 310 − 180 = 130°  (c) 180 − 180 = 000°
  3. 148 < 180, so add: 148 + 180 = 328°
  4. Going clockwise from B to C is 290 − 35 = 255°, so the angle inside is 360 − 255 = 105°
  5. Right angle between the legs: d = √(12² + 5²) = √169 = 13 km. tanθ = 5 ÷ 12 so θ = 22.6° south of east, giving bearing 90 + 22.6 = 112.6°
  6. Bearing of A from B is 140 + 180 = 320°, so angle ABC = 320 − 210 = 110°. Cosine rule: AC² = 8² + 6² − 2(8)(6)cos110° = 132.8, so AC = 11.5 km. Sine rule: sin(BAC) = 6 sin110° ÷ 11.525 = 0.4892 so angle BAC = 29.3°, and the bearing is 140 + 29.3 = 169.3°

Key points to remember

  • A bearing is measured from north, clockwise, and written with three figures: 045°, 008°, 237°.
  • “The bearing of B from A” puts the north line at A. Reversing the letters gives a different angle.
  • Learn the compass values: N 000°, NE 045°, E 090°, SE 135°, S 180°, SW 225°, W 270°, NW 315°.
  • To draw a bearing over 180°, measure 360° − bearing anticlockwise from north.
  • Back bearing: add 180° if the bearing is under 180°, subtract 180° if it is 180° or more. The answer must stay between 000° and 360°.
  • The rule works because north lines are parallel, so the two angles at the ends of the journey are co-interior angles and add to 180°. Quote that fact when a reason is asked for.
  • Two bearings taken from the same point subtract to give the angle between the directions.
  • On a scale drawing, write the scale down and draw a fresh north line at every point before measuring the next bearing.
  • When a question says calculate, find the triangle's angles from the bearings first, then use Pythagoras and SOHCAHTOA for right angles, or the sine and cosine rules otherwise.
